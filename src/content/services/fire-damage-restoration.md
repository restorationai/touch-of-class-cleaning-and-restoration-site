## What fire damage restoration actually involves

Fire damage restoration covers everything between the fire department clearing the structure and the home being livable again: soot and smoke residue removal, odor neutralization, structural cleaning, and coordination with contents cleaning or pack-out when belongings are affected. The work differs a lot depending on what burned. A grease fire that stayed in the kitchen produces thick, oily protein soot that's nearly invisible but extremely pungent. A fire that involved synthetic materials like furniture foam or carpet leaves a dry, powdery soot that smears easily and spreads further through the air. Structural fires that burn through framing or roofing require coordination between cleanup and rebuild, since charred lumber, compromised drywall, and scorched electrical need to come out before any surface cleaning matters.

Timelines vary with scope. A single-room smoke event with no structural damage might be cleaned and deodorized within a few days. A whole-house fire with structural loss and contents pack-out can run several weeks once drying, cleaning, odor treatment, and any necessary demolition are factored in.

## Our process

1. **Site assessment and soot characterization** - identifying whether the residue is protein-based, synthetic, dry, or wet changes which cleaning agents and tools get used, since the wrong approach can set soot stains instead of lifting them.
2. **Structural cleaning and deodorization** - HEPA vacuuming, dry sponging, and wet cleaning of walls, ceilings, and hard surfaces, following industry fire restoration guidance such as IICRC S700 for sequencing and material-specific handling.
3. **Odor treatment** - thermal fogging or hydroxyl/ozone treatment to break down smoke molecules trapped in porous materials rather than masking them with fragrance.
4. **Contents pack-out and inventory** - salvageable belongings are documented, packed, and moved off-site for specialized cleaning while structural work continues without obstruction.
5. **Final walk-through and documentation** - photos, moisture readings where firefighting water was involved, and a written scope that supports the insurance claim.

## What separates a good fire damage response from a bad one

The most common mistake is treating odor as a fragrance problem instead of a chemistry problem. Spraying an air freshener over smoke-saturated drywall or carpet pad doesn't remove the smoke particles, it just adds a second smell on top of the first, and the odor returns within days. A second mistake is stopping the cleanup at visible soot and skipping the HVAC system, which recirculates trapped smoke residue every time the system runs.

Adjusters generally look for a few specific things: pre-work photos of the full extent of damage (not just the room of origin), documentation of soot type and cleaning method used, moisture readings if any firefighting water reached framing or subfloor, and a clear line between what was cleaned versus what was replaced. A scope that lumps everything together without that breakdown tends to slow down claim approval.
