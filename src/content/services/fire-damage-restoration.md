## What fire damage restoration actually involves

Fire damage restoration is the work of stabilizing a structure after combustion, then removing soot, smoke residue, and odor from everything the fire touched, directly or indirectly. That includes charred framing and drywall near the point of origin, but just as often it means ceiling tile, duct interiors, cabinetry, and fabric throughout a home that smell like smoke even though they look fine. Equipment ranges from HEPA vacuums and chemical sponges for dry soot to thermal foggers and ozone generators for odor that's worked into porous materials. Timelines vary with the size of the fire: a single-room kitchen fire with heavy smoke travel might take a week of active work, while a structural fire that compromised framing or roof decking runs into reconstruction and can take considerably longer.

## Our process

1. **Soot characterization.** Different fires leave different residue. Protein soot from a grease or food fire is nearly invisible but carries an intense odor and tends to discolor painted surfaces. Synthetic soot from burning plastics and furnishings is heavier, oilier, and smears if wiped with the wrong method. Identifying which type you're dealing with determines the cleaning agents and technique used on every surface.
2. **Structural stabilization and containment.** Boarding openings, tarping roof damage, and shutting down HVAC systems to stop soot from recirculating comes before any cleaning starts.
3. **Soot and residue removal.** Dry sponge cleaning for light soot, wet cleaning with specialized detergents for heavier residue, and media blasting or abrasive methods for charred structural members that will be kept rather than demolished.
4. **Odor neutralization.** Thermal fogging and ozone or hydroxyl treatment address smoke molecules that have penetrated drywall, insulation, and subflooring, not just the surfaces you can see.
5. **Contents pack-out and cleaning.** Furniture, textiles, and personal belongings are inventoried, removed to a cleaning facility when needed, and either restored or documented as a loss for the claim.

## What separates a good fire damage response from a bad one

The most common mistake is treating fire cleanup as a surface problem. Wiping visible soot off walls without addressing what's inside the HVAC system means the smell comes right back the first time the furnace or AC kicks on. Another frequent miss is skipping a proper assessment of hidden fire travel: flames can run inside wall cavities and above drop ceilings, leaving char that's invisible until drywall comes down.

Adjusters evaluating a fire claim generally want to see documented soot testing (identifying protein vs. synthetic residue), room-by-room photos before any cleaning begins, and a clear line between what's being restored versus what's being replaced. A rushed cleanup that paints over smoke-stained drywall instead of sealing or removing it is a common point of rework, since odor bleeds back through fresh paint within weeks. Good fire restoration also separates contents that can be cleaned from those that should be claimed as a total loss, which protects the homeowner from under-documenting the claim.
