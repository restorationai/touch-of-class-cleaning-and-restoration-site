North Texas storms move fast, and a line of severe thunderstorms with straight-line winds or a spring hailstorm can strip shingles, snap limbs onto a roofline, and drive wind-blown rain through a compromised attic in under an hour. The damage that matters most isn't always what you can see from the curb. ## What storm damage restoration actually involves

Storm damage restoration covers the work needed after wind, hail, tornado activity, or a fallen tree compromises a roof, siding, windows, or the building envelope. That can mean emergency tarping over an open roof section, boarding a window blown in by debris, removing a tree limb pinning a roof deck, and extracting any water that made it inside before structural drying equipment goes to work. The scope depends heavily on what the wind actually did: a missing ridge cap is a different job than a limb through a second-story bedroom, and the drying timeline changes based on how long water sat before it was found. Equipment typically includes tarping and board-up materials rated for exterior exposure, chainsaws and rigging for safe limb removal, moisture meters to track hidden intrusion, and air movers or dehumidifiers if water reached the interior.

## Our process

1. **Safety and hazard check.** Before anyone climbs a ladder or touches debris, downed power lines, unstable limbs, and compromised roof decking get identified and flagged.
2. **Emergency tarp or board-up.** Open roof sections, broken windows, and breached walls get covered to stop ongoing water and pest intrusion while a full repair plan comes together.
3. **Debris and tree removal.** Limbs, branches, and storm debris are cleared from the roof and structure so an accurate damage assessment is possible underneath.
4. **Water extraction and drying, if needed.** If wind-driven rain reached the interior, standing water is extracted and affected materials are dried and monitored the same way any water intrusion would be handled.
5. **Documentation and scope for repair.** Photos, moisture readings, and a written damage scope are compiled so the homeowner has a clear record for their carrier and a defined path to full repair.

## What separates a good storm damage response from a bad one

The most common mistake after a storm is tarping the obvious hole and missing the secondary damage: a hairline fracture in a vent pipe collar, lifted flashing at a chimney, or a soaked attic insulation pocket that keeps feeding moisture into the ceiling below long after the tarp goes up. A rushed job also skips moisture mapping, so a drywall section that looks dry on the surface still has a saturated stud cavity behind it. Adjusters look for dated photos of the damage before any tarping or debris removal, a clear description of what caused the loss (wind versus pre-existing wear), and moisture documentation if water intrusion occurred. Good storm response treats the tarp as a temporary stabilization step, not the end of the job, and keeps a paper trail from the first walk-around through the final repair scope.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

North Texas sees two distinct storm windows each year: spring severe weather season, when supercells bring large hail and straight-line winds, and the tail end of hurricane season, when remnant tropical systems can dump heavy rain well inland. Richardson's mix of mature trees and older roofing stock means wind events frequently combine tree damage with roof penetration in the same loss, and homes built before more recent wind-rating code updates can be more vulnerable to shingle blow-off in sustained gusts.
