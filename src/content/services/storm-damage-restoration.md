## What storm damage restoration actually involves

Storm damage restoration covers the work needed after wind, hail, tornadoes, or heavy rain compromise a roof, siding, windows, or fencing and let water, debris, or outside air into the structure. That can mean tarping a torn-open roof section, boarding a blown-out window, removing a fallen limb that's resting on a damaged ridge line, or extracting rainwater that came through a breach before it reaches the drywall and insulation below. The work is time-sensitive in a different way than routine water damage: the priority is stopping ongoing intrusion, not just drying what's already wet. Equipment includes tarping and plywood for temporary enclosure, chainsaws and rigging for safe debris removal, moisture meters to track how far water traveled once it got inside, and extraction and drying equipment if rain has already soaked flooring, drywall, or insulation.

## Our process

1. **Stabilize the structure.** Tarp roof openings, board broken windows and doors, and secure anything that could shift or fall further before weather returns.
2. **Remove debris safely.** Tree limbs, roofing material, and fencing often need to come off the structure before anyone can assess what's underneath, and that work is done carefully to avoid further damage to compromised framing.
3. **Assess what got inside.** Moisture readings on walls, ceilings, and flooring near the breach tell us how far rainwater traveled and whether insulation or drywall needs to come out.
4. **Extract and dry.** If water made it past the breach, standing water is pulled first, then air movers and dehumidifiers are set to bring moisture content back to a dry standard, tracked with daily readings.
5. **Document for the claim.** Photos of the original breach, debris, and affected materials are logged alongside moisture readings, since this is usually the evidence an adjuster will ask for first.

## What separates a good storm damage response from a bad one

The most common mistake after a storm is tarping the visible hole and calling it done while water has already tracked along rafters or down into a wall cavity well past the opening. A tarp stops new water from coming in, but it doesn't dry out what already got wet, and insulation left damp inside a closed-up wall is exactly how a secondary mold problem starts two or three weeks later. Adjusters also look closely at the distinction between wind-created damage and pre-existing wear, so photos taken before any debris is moved or any tarp goes up matter more than people expect. Another gap: crews that remove a fallen tree without checking whether the point of impact cracked rafters or sheathing that isn't visible from the ground, which means repairs get scoped for cosmetic damage when there's a structural issue underneath.

## What does storm damage restoration cost?

## Seasonal & regional considerations

North Texas sees its heaviest severe weather load in spring and early summer, when supercells bring large hail and straight-line winds, along with occasional tornado activity across the Dallas-Fort Worth area. Late summer and fall can also bring heavy rain from the remnants of Gulf hurricanes, which drop less wind but more sustained rainfall, and that combination is what tends to find existing roof weaknesses that didn't matter until the rain lasted for hours instead of minutes. Homes with aging asphalt shingle roofs, common across many Richardson neighborhoods, are especially vulnerable to wind uplift at the edges and ridge line even when the rest of the roof looks intact from the street.
