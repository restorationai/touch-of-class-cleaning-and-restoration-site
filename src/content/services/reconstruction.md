Water mitigation crews pull out the wet materials. Fire crews clear the soot and char. But when the drywall is gone, the subfloor is cut back to the joists, or a load-bearing wall has fire damage that affects the framing underneath, someone still has to put the house back together in a way that passes inspection and holds up. That's the gap reconstruction services fill in Richardson: the rebuild phase that starts after demolition and mitigation end and doesn't finish until the room is livable again.

## What reconstruction services actually involve

Post-damage reconstruction covers everything from a single sheet of drywall and a patch of flooring to full structural rebuilding after a fire, flood, or storm has compromised framing, subfloor, or roof decking. The scope depends entirely on what the damage took out. A bathroom that lost its vanity and lower drywall after a slow leak is a different job than a kitchen that lost cabinets, subfloor, and part of a load-bearing wall after a grease fire. Timelines reflect that range too: a single-room drywall and paint job might run a week or two, while structural reconstruction involving framing, electrical rough-in, and permitted inspections can take several weeks to a few months depending on how much has to be rebuilt and how fast permits move through the city.

## Our process

1. **Scope of work.** Before any rebuilding starts, we document what was removed during mitigation or demolition and write a detailed scope covering materials, labor, and sequencing. This is the document your adjuster will compare against the claim.
2. **Permits.** Structural work, electrical, plumbing rough-in, and some drywall replacement in Richardson require permits pulled through the city's building department. We identify what needs a permit before framing starts, not after an inspector flags it.
3. **Framing and rough-in.** Damaged studs, joists, or subfloor are replaced to match existing structure. Electrical and plumbing that was disconnected or removed during mitigation gets rough-in work and inspection before walls close up.
4. **Insulation, drywall, and finishing.** Insulation is replaced where it was removed or contaminated, drywall goes up and gets taped and textured to match surrounding surfaces, and trim, paint, and flooring bring the space back to a finished condition.
5. **Final walkthrough.** We compare the finished space against the original scope of work and address any punch-list items before calling the job complete.

## What separates a good reconstruction response from a bad one

The most common mistake is closing up walls before an inspection or before moisture readings confirm the framing is actually dry. If a wall gets rebuilt over studs that still read wet, the new drywall can develop the same mold or warping problem within months, and now it's hidden behind a finished surface. A second mistake is skipping permits on work that technically requires them. Unpermitted electrical or structural work can create problems at resale or with a future insurance claim, even if it looks fine today.

A good reconstruction job also pays attention to material matching, flooring that doesn't quite match the existing run, trim profiles that are close but not identical, paint sheen that reads differently under certain light. Adjusters and homeowners both notice these details, and they're usually the difference between a rebuild that looks seamless and one that looks like a patch. We write the scope of work with those details specified up front rather than leaving them to guesswork mid-project.

## What does reconstruction cost?

Reconstruction costs vary more than almost any other restoration service because the scope ranges from a single wall to a full structural rebuild. Every loss is different, which is why we provide a written scope of work with itemized costs before any rebuilding begins rather than a flat number up front. Homeowners insurance typically covers reconstruction tied to a covered peril, such as fire or a sudden pipe failure, but coverage for the rebuild is usually tied to the same claim that covered the mitigation, so documentation matters.

| Scenario | Typical range |
|---|---|
| Drywall and paint, single room | $1,500 - $4,000 |
| Flooring replacement, one room | $2,000 - $6,000 |
| Bathroom reconstruction (vanity, drywall, flooring) | $4,000 - $10,000 |
| Kitchen reconstruction (cabinets, subfloor, drywall) | $10,000 - $30,000 |
| Structural framing repair (wall or floor section) | $5,000 - $20,000 |
| Full room rebuild after fire damage | $15,000 - $50,000+ |

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Richardson's housing stock includes a good mix of homes built from the 1960s through the 1990s, along with newer construction in redeveloped areas. Older homes more often turn up surprises once walls open up, outdated wiring, plumbing that doesn't match current code, or framing that was never built to today's standards. That can affect both the permit process and the final scope, since code-compliant repairs sometimes require more than a like-for-like replacement. North Texas's swing between hot, humid summers and occasional hard freezes also means framing and subfloor need to be fully dry before they're closed up, especially after winter pipe breaks, so moisture verification during reconstruction isn't a formality.
