Industrial restoration deals with the scale, equipment, and safety concerns that separate a 200,000-square-foot plant from a residential basement.

## What industrial restoration actually involves

Industrial and manufacturing facilities bring problems a home restoration job never sees: open floor plans with tens of thousands of square feet to dry, racking systems and machinery that can't get wet or can't be moved, HVAC systems built for production tolerances rather than comfort, and electrical panels or control systems that have to be de-energized before anyone touches standing water. Warehouse restoration often means pulling water out from under pallet racking and around dock levelers. Manufacturing facility restoration might mean protecting a production line from corrosion while the surrounding slab dries. Plant restoration services frequently involve coordinating with a safety officer or facilities manager before any crew can even walk the floor. The equipment scales up accordingly: truck-mount extractors move to trailer-mounted units, portable dehumidifiers give way to desiccant systems sized to the cubic footage of the space, and drying timelines get measured against production schedules, not just moisture meters.

## Our process

1. **Site safety assessment.** Before extraction or demolition starts, we walk the facility to identify electrical hazards, chemical storage, structural concerns, and any area that needs to be isolated or de-energized.
2. **Large-volume extraction or debris removal.** Industrial-grade pumps and extractors clear standing water fast; for fire losses, this stage covers soot, charred material, and contents removal from affected zones.
3. **Structural drying and moisture mapping.** Desiccant dehumidification is sized to the square footage involved, with moisture readings logged across the floor, walls, and any affected racking or framing to confirm drying is progressing evenly.
4. **Equipment and machinery protection.** Exposed metal surfaces and electrical components get inspected and treated to limit corrosion, since industrial humidity swings during drying can cause more damage to machinery than the original loss.
5. **Documentation and reporting.** Photos, moisture logs, and a written scope are compiled for insurance and for your own facility records, which matters most when a business interruption claim is part of the picture.

## What separates a good industrial restoration response from a bad one

The most common mistake on large-loss jobs is undersizing the drying equipment for the space, which stretches a one-week dry-out into three weeks of lost production. A good response calculates dehumidification capacity against actual cubic footage and ceiling height, not a rule of thumb built for residential rooms. Another frequent miss is skipping moisture checks inside wall cavities and under flooring in older industrial buildings, where water can travel along conduit runs or under slab expansion joints well past the visible wet area. Adjusters reviewing industrial claims generally want to see moisture logs over time, not a single reading, along with clear documentation of what equipment was running, for how long, and why. On fire losses, soot composition matters: the cleaning approach for protein-based residue from a contents fire is different from synthetic soot off burned plastics or coatings, and treating them the same way usually means redoing the work.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Richwood sits in a humid Gulf Coast climate with a long stretch of high ambient humidity for much of the year, which slows natural evaporation and makes mechanical dehumidification more important on any industrial water loss here than it would be in a drier region. The area also sees hurricane season roll through late summer into fall, which raises the risk of wind-driven water intrusion and power outages that can shut down HVAC and dehumidification equipment mid-dry. Facilities near the Gulf Coast's industrial corridor should also factor in that corrosion moves faster on exposed metal in this humidity, which makes early equipment protection a priority rather than an afterthought.
