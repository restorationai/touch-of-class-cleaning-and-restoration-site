## What commercial restoration actually involves

Commercial restoration covers the same core disciplines as residential work (water extraction, structural drying, soot and smoke removal, repair of affected finishes) but applied at a different scale and under different constraints. An office floor may have raised flooring over cabling, a retail space may have a concrete slab under commercial carpet tile, and a multi-tenant building may require work to stop at a suite's demising wall. Equipment runs larger: commercial dehumidifiers and air movers sized for open floor plans, moisture meters and pinless probes for slab and subfloor readings, and in fire losses, HEPA air scrubbers sized for the cubic footage of the space rather than a single room. Timelines depend heavily on occupancy: a vacant suite can be worked around the clock, while an active business often needs work scheduled around operating hours to limit disruption to staff, customers, and inventory.

## Our process

1. **Loss assessment and operational triage** - a walkthrough with the owner or property manager to identify what's affected, what's salvageable, and what systems (server racks, POS terminals, walk-in coolers, inventory) need protecting first.
2. **Containment and workflow separation** - barriers go up to isolate the affected area from areas still in use, so a retail front can often stay partially open while the back of house is worked.
3. **Extraction, drying, and smoke/soot removal** - water is pulled, structural drying begins and is tracked with moisture readings over several days, or soot is characterized and removed from affected surfaces and HVAC where applicable.
4. **Documentation and stakeholder coordination** - photos, moisture logs, and scope notes are compiled for the property owner, the insurance carrier, and property management, since commercial claims often involve more than one party.
5. **Make-ready and handback** - final cleaning and repair work brings the space back to a condition where normal business can resume.

## What separates a good commercial restoration response from a bad one

Residential habits don't transfer cleanly to commercial spaces. A crew that doesn't account for commercial HVAC can leave a duct system carrying smoke odor or microbial growth back into occupied areas weeks later. Concrete slabs under commercial flooring hold moisture longer than wood subfloor and need to be checked with relative humidity testing, not just surface moisture readings, before flooring goes back down. Fire-rated wall assemblies common in commercial construction have to be rebuilt to match their original rating, not just patched for appearance. Adjusters reviewing a commercial claim look closely at documented drying logs, equipment placement records, and whether the scope accounts for business interruption and code-driven repairs (ADA path of egress, fire-rated assemblies), not just the visible damage.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Richardson's winters bring occasional hard freezes that split commercial supply lines and sprinkler risers, often discovered Monday morning after a cold weekend. North Texas summers bring heavy thunderstorms and hail that stress flat commercial roofs, a common source of slow leaks into ceiling tile and insulation long before staff notice a stain. The region's humidity also means any water event in a commercial space, especially one with closed-up HVAC over a weekend, needs fast attention to keep microbial growth from starting in wall cavities and ceiling systems.
