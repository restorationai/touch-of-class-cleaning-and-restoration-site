## What commercial restoration actually involves

Commercial work is residential restoration scaled up and complicated by business constraints: multiple tenants, suspended ceiling grids, raised flooring, point-of-sale equipment, walk-in coolers, server racks, and inventory that can't just sit wet while drying equipment runs. A commercial water loss might mean pulling wet carpet tile in a reception area while keeping the rest of the office open, or extracting water from under a raised computer floor without shutting down the whole system. A commercial fire or smoke event often means cleaning soot off acoustic ceiling tile, HVAC returns, and merchandise, not just walls and trim.

Equipment is sized to the space, not the house: commercial-grade air movers and desiccant dehumidifiers for larger square footage, HEPA air scrubbers where dust or soot has traveled through return air, and moisture meters checked daily against drying logs so the drying can be documented for the property's insurance carrier or risk manager.

## Our process

1. **Scope and safety walk**: before equipment goes in, we walk the space to identify electrical hazards, structural concerns, and anything that affects employee or customer access, then document pre-loss conditions with photos.
2. **Extraction or soot removal**: standing water is pulled from flooring, carpet, and subfloor assemblies; for fire losses, loose soot is removed with HEPA vacuums before any wet cleaning, which keeps it from grinding into porous surfaces.
3. **Containment where the business needs it**: if part of the building stays open, we section off the work area with poly barriers and negative air where needed so dust, odor, or moisture doesn't migrate into occupied space.
4. **Drying or deodorization**: commercial dehumidification runs on a monitored schedule with daily moisture readings; smoke odor is addressed with thermal fogging or ozone treatment once materials are dry, never masked over wet or damp surfaces.
5. **Reinspection and sign-off**: before we close the job, moisture readings and visual inspection confirm the space is back within normal range, and we provide documentation your insurance adjuster or property manager can reference.

## What separates a good commercial restoration response from a bad one

The most common mistake on commercial jobs is treating them like an oversized residential job. A 3,000 square foot retail floor doesn't dry on the same equipment count as a bedroom, and undersizing dehumidification just means a longer closure. Another frequent miss: not accounting for what's behind the walls or under the floor. Office buildings built with suspended ceilings often hide ductwork and wiring that soot or water reaches before anyone sees a stain. Retail spaces on slab foundations can trap moisture under vinyl or VCT flooring long after the surface looks dry.

Adjusters reviewing commercial claims look for daily moisture logs, photos tied to specific rooms or zones, and a clear line between what's restorable and what needs replacement, things like water-damaged ceiling tile or insulation that won't dry clean. A response that skips documentation or dries to "looks dry" instead of a measured baseline tends to get questioned, and sometimes reopened, later.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Gulf Coast humidity means commercial spaces around Richwood don't get much of a dry season to recover in, so moisture trapped in slab-on-grade flooring or behind vinyl base can sit at elevated levels for days before it's noticeable. Heavy rain events and the occasional tropical system also mean commercial roofs and loading dock areas see more water intrusion risk than buildings further inland, which is worth factoring into how fast a leak or roof failure gets addressed.
