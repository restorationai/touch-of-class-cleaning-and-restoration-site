## What Water Damage Restoration actually involves

Water damage restoration is the work of getting standing and absorbed water out of a structure, then drying what's left behind before it becomes a mold problem or a rot problem. That means pulling water off hard flooring with truck-mounted or portable extractors, pulling it out of carpet pad and cushion, and in many cases cutting into wet drywall or removing baseboards so trapped moisture behind the wall cavity has somewhere to go. Equipment stays on site for days, not hours: low-grain-refrigerant dehumidifiers, air movers positioned to create airflow across wet surfaces, and sometimes injection systems for hardwood or cabinetry that can't be removed. The category of water matters, too. Clean water from a supply line is handled differently than gray water from an appliance or black water from a sewage backup, and that distinction drives what gets dried in place versus what gets removed and replaced.

## Our process

1. **Extraction.** Standing water comes out first, using extraction equipment sized to the volume of the loss. The faster this happens, the less water has a chance to wick into subfloor, baseboards, and wall cavities.
2. **Moisture mapping.** Moisture meters and sometimes thermal imaging identify exactly how far water traveled, including areas that look dry on the surface but are still saturated underneath.
3. **Structural drying.** Air movers and dehumidifiers are set based on the moisture map, not guesswork, with equipment placement adjusted as readings change over the following days.
4. **Daily monitoring.** Materials are re-checked against drying standards until moisture content returns to normal, with documentation at each visit for the insurance file.
5. **Containment for Category 2 or 3 water.** When water is contaminated, affected porous materials are typically removed rather than dried, and the work area is contained to keep the rest of the structure clean.

## What separates a good water damage response from a bad one

The most common mistake isn't missing the visible water, it's declaring a job finished because the carpet feels dry and the surface of the drywall reads normal. Water moves sideways through subfloor and wicks up inside wall cavities well past where it's visible, and skipping moisture mapping means those hidden pockets keep feeding mold growth for weeks after the crew leaves. Another frequent miss is drying wood flooring in place when the water has already caused cupping or delamination that won't reverse, running equipment for a few extra days on a floor that needed to come up on day one.

Insurance adjusters look for the same things a careful technician should already be tracking: moisture readings taken daily and logged, photos of the affected area before demolition starts, and a drying log that shows readings trending down, not flat. A scope of work that explains what's being dried versus removed, and why, holds up a lot better during a claim review than a one-line invoice.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

North Texas sits on expansive clay soil that shifts with wet and dry cycles, and that movement is a common contributor to slab leaks in Richardson homes, especially in foundations built before modern plumbing codes. Winters here are usually mild, but the region has seen hard freezes that catch exposed or poorly insulated pipes off guard, and a frozen line that bursts on thaw can dump water into a structure for hours before anyone's home to notice. Spring and early summer bring heavy rain events that strain older drainage systems and can push water in through grade-level entry points, particularly around older slab foundations without proper flashing.
