## What Water Damage Restoration actually involves

Water damage restoration is the process of removing standing water, pulling moisture out of wall cavities, subfloor, and framing, and confirming with instruments, not guesswork, that the structure is back to a safe moisture range. It starts with water extraction using truck-mounted or portable pumps and wet vacs to remove free-standing water, followed by structural drying with high-velocity air movers and dehumidifiers positioned according to the layout of the affected rooms. Category of water matters here: clean water from a supply line behaves differently than water that has contacted soil, sewage, or has sat long enough to become contaminated (Category 2 or 3), which changes what materials can be dried in place versus what needs to be removed. A straightforward single-room loss might take three to five days of monitored drying; a multi-room loss with wet subfloor or plaster walls often runs longer because those materials hold moisture and release it slowly.

## Our process

1. **Water extraction.** Standing water is pumped and vacuumed out first, because every hour it sits, more of it wicks into drywall, baseboards, and subfloor.
2. **Structural drying setup.** Air movers and dehumidifiers are placed based on affected square footage and material type, not a one-size-fits-all grid.
3. **Moisture monitoring.** Moisture meters and thermal imaging track drying progress day over day in wood framing, drywall, and subfloor until readings return to a normal range, not just until the carpet feels dry to the touch.
4. **Containment for Category 2/3 water.** When water is contaminated (sewage backup, floodwater, or water that has sat long enough to turn), affected porous materials like carpet pad or drywall are contained and typically removed rather than dried in place.
5. **Final verification.** Drying equipment comes down only after moisture readings confirm the structure is within an acceptable range, documented for your records and your insurance claim.

## What separates a good water damage response from a bad one

The most common mistake is treating "looks dry" as "is dry." Carpet and surface drywall can feel dry within a day while the subfloor or wall cavity behind it stays saturated, which is exactly how hidden mold growth starts within 24 to 48 hours of a water event. A good response uses moisture meters and, where needed, thermal imaging to verify what's happening behind the surface, not just what's visible. Adjusters look for daily drying logs, moisture readings at each visit, and photos of the affected areas before equipment is pulled, so a restoration record that skips this step can slow down or shrink a claim. Another frequent miss: not distinguishing water category correctly, drying contaminated water in place instead of removing and discarding porous materials that can't be safely cleaned.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Richardson sees hard freezes often enough that unprotected exterior pipes and attic lines are a recurring cause of winter water losses, especially in homes built before stricter insulation codes. Texas clay soil also shifts with wet and dry cycles, which can stress slab plumbing and lead to slow leaks that go unnoticed until flooring or baseboards start to show damage. Spring and early summer storms bring heavy, fast rainfall that can overwhelm gutters and grading on older lots, pushing water toward foundations faster than it can drain.
