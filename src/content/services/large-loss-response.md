When a loss stops being a single room or a single unit and turns into an entire building, a multi-building commercial property, or a structure with hundreds of thousands of square feet affected, the response has to change shape. Large loss and catastrophic response covers the events that overwhelm a standard residential crew: a burst main that floods multiple floors of an apartment complex, a fire that moves through a shared attic in a strip center, a storm that tears roofing off several buildings on the same parcel. These losses move on a different clock, involve different documentation, and usually put a commercial adjuster or a third-party administrator in the loop from day one.

## What large loss and catastrophic response actually involves

A catastrophic loss is defined less by the cause and more by the scale: square footage affected, number of units or tenants displaced, and the complexity of coordinating multiple trades at once. This can mean a multi-story office building with water traveling through drywall cavities and HVAC chases, a manufacturing facility with standing water around equipment pads, or a residential complex with dozens of units needing simultaneous moisture mapping. The work typically runs on a longer timeline than a single-family loss, sometimes weeks rather than days, and requires equipment deployed at a different scale: desiccant dehumidification for large open floor plates, negative air setups across multiple containment zones, and moisture monitoring logs that track dozens of readings instead of three or four.

## Our process

1. **Initial scope and triage.** We walk the property to identify which areas are life-safety concerns, which are structurally at risk, and which can wait, then build a phased plan instead of treating every square foot as equal priority.
2. **Documentation for the claim.** Photos, moisture readings, and a written scope are compiled early because large loss claims almost always involve a commercial adjuster who needs a clear paper trail before authorizing extended drying or demolition.
3. **Equipment deployment at scale.** Extraction, dehumidification, and air movement equipment get placed by zone, not by room, with daily monitoring logs that track drying progress across the entire affected footprint.
4. **Coordination with other trades.** Large losses frequently need electricians, plumbers, or structural engineers involved alongside the drying or cleanup work, and that sequencing has to be managed so one trade isn't waiting on another unnecessarily.
5. **Final verification and handoff.** Moisture readings are brought to dry standard, documentation is finalized for the carrier, and the property is handed back ready for repairs or reconstruction to begin.

## What separates a good large loss response from a bad one

The biggest mistake on large losses is treating them like an oversized version of a residential job. A single dehumidifier strategy that works in a 1,200 square foot house does not scale linearly to a 40,000 square foot warehouse, and under-equipping a large space is one of the most common ways secondary damage sets in before anyone notices. Adjusters on commercial and catastrophic files are also looking for something specific: daily monitoring logs, not just a start and end reading, and a scope that explains why certain materials were removed rather than dried in place. Missing documentation on square footage affected, moisture content by zone, or the reasoning behind demolition decisions is one of the fastest ways a large claim gets delayed or disputed. A good response also means recognizing when a loss needs to be escalated to a structural engineer or when contents need to be inventoried and moved before drying equipment goes in, rather than working around furniture and equipment that should have been relocated first.

Costs on large loss and catastrophic claims vary enormously depending on square footage, number of units or tenants affected, and whether structural materials need to be removed versus dried in place. ## Seasonal & regional considerations

Gulf Coast storm season brings the highest concentration of catastrophic losses in this region, with high wind and heavy rainfall events capable of damaging multiple structures on a single property at once. Humidity levels in the Richwood area also mean that large open spaces left wet for even a short window can develop secondary moisture problems in wall cavities and ceiling plenums faster than in drier climates, which is part of why equipment placement and daily monitoring matter more on a catastrophic timeline than on a smaller job.
