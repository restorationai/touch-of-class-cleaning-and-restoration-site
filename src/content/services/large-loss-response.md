When a water main breaks on an upper floor of an apartment complex and three levels below take on water before anyone notices, or a hailstorm tears through a business park and a dozen units file claims in the same week, the loss stops being a standard restoration job. It becomes a logistics problem: multiple structures, multiple stakeholders, a property manager who needs daily updates, and an adjuster who needs documentation that holds up. Large loss and catastrophic response is built for that scale, not for a single flooded bedroom.

## What large loss and catastrophic response actually involves

A large loss is generally defined by scope and dollar value rather than by a single trigger event: multi-unit residential buildings, commercial office floors, hotels, industrial space, or any property where the damage spans multiple structures, floors, or tenant spaces at once. The work itself pulls from the same core disciplines, water extraction, structural drying, fire and smoke mitigation, but at a scale that requires staged mobilization instead of a single crew showing up with a truck. Equipment needs multiply: dozens of air movers and dehumidifiers instead of a handful, moisture mapping across thousands of square feet instead of one room, and often multiple mitigation zones running in parallel so different sections of a building can dry on their own timeline. Project management becomes as important as the physical labor, because a property manager, a general contractor, and several insurance adjusters all need to be looking at the same information.

## Our process

1. **Site-wide assessment and scope documentation.** Before any equipment moves in, we walk the full affected footprint, floor by floor or unit by unit, and document moisture readings, material categories, and visible damage with photos and notes an adjuster can actually use.
2. **Mobilization planning.** Large loss work means matching crew size and equipment count to the actual square footage, not sending a standard crew to an oversized job. We build a mobilization plan that sequences which zones get attacked first based on contamination risk and occupancy needs.
3. **Phased mitigation across zones.** Rather than treating the building as one job, we break it into drying zones so sections that are further along can be monitored and closed out while active mitigation continues elsewhere.
4. **Daily progress reporting.** On a loss this size, the adjuster, property manager, and any general contractor involved in rebuild need a consistent record, moisture logs, drying curves, photos, so nobody is working from guesses about where the project actually stands.
5. **Handoff to reconstruction.** Once drying and mitigation are verified complete, we document the structure as ready for the rebuild phase so the transition to any contractor doesn't lose the paper trail built during mitigation.

## What separates a good large loss response from a bad one

The most common failure on big losses isn't bad technique, it's under-resourcing. A crew sized for a single-family home sent to a 40-unit building will fall behind the drying timeline, and materials that should have been salvageable end up gutted because moisture sat too long. The second failure is documentation gaps: adjusters reviewing large claims expect zone-by-zone moisture logs and dated photos, and a project that can't produce that paper trail tends to get disputed on scope and cost. Good large loss response also means one point of contact who can speak for the whole project, not five crew leads giving a property manager five different timelines. And drying should be tracked against an actual standard, structural materials monitored until they hit dry targets per IICRC S500 guidelines, not eyeballed and called finished.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

North Texas sees both ends of the weather spectrum feed large loss claims. Spring brings severe storm and hail systems that can damage roofing and envelopes across entire business parks or apartment complexes in a single pass, generating simultaneous claims on multiple buildings. Winter freeze events, the kind that hit the region periodically, cause pipe bursts across multiple units in older multifamily properties built with plumbing that wasn't designed for sustained hard freezes. Summer humidity extends drying timelines on large structural drying jobs, which is part of why zone-based moisture tracking matters more on a catastrophic loss than on a single room.
