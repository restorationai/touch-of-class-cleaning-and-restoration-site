A burst pipe rarely announces itself politely. ## What burst pipe cleanup and repair actually involves

Burst pipe cleanup and repair is two jobs running at once: stopping and fixing the source of water, and removing the water that already got into the building. The water itself is usually clean (Category 1) if it came straight from a supply line, but once it sits in flooring, drywall, or cabinetry for more than a day or two, or if it's picked up contaminants from the slab or crawlspace, it moves toward Category 2 and the drying protocol changes. Equipment on a typical pipe break job includes submersible pumps or wet vacuums for standing water, air movers positioned to push air across wet surfaces, and low-grain-refrigerant dehumidifiers to pull moisture out of the air so the building materials can actually release it. Moisture meters and sometimes a thermal camera track what's wet behind the walls, not just what looks wet on the surface. Depending on how long the water was active before it was caught, structural drying typically runs three to five days, with daily monitoring to confirm the materials are actually drying and not just reading dry at the surface.

## Our process

1. **Locate and stop the source.** Before any drying starts, the active leak has to be isolated, whether that's a shutoff valve, the main, or a section of pipe that needs immediate repair.
2. **Extract standing water.** Pumps and extraction tools remove bulk water from flooring, subfloor, and any cavities before drying equipment goes in, since drying wet material under standing water is a waste of equipment hours.
3. **Assess and open up affected materials.** Baseboards, drywall, and flooring are checked and, where needed, opened or removed to expose wet framing, insulation, or subfloor that would otherwise trap moisture and feed mold growth.
4. **Dry and monitor.** Air movers and dehumidifiers are positioned per a drying plan, and moisture readings are tracked daily against a drying goal, not a fixed number of days, so equipment comes down when the materials are actually dry.
5. **Repair the pipe and restore affected areas.** Once the source is fixed and the structure is dry, drywall, flooring, and trim are repaired or replaced to bring the space back to pre-loss condition.

## What separates a good burst pipe response from a bad one

The most common mistake is treating this as only a plumbing problem. Fixing the pipe and mopping up visible water without checking what happened inside the wall cavity or under the flooring leaves wet insulation and subfloor behind, which is exactly what mold needs. A second mistake is pulling drying equipment too early because the floor "feels dry" to the touch, when the moisture meter still reads elevated numbers in the wall base or subfloor. Insurance adjusters evaluating a pipe break claim typically look for documented moisture readings at each stage, photos of the affected materials before and after demolition, and a clear timeline between when the pipe failed and when mitigation started, since that window affects whether later damage is covered as part of the original loss. A written scope that separates water categories (clean supply water versus water that picked up contamination from the slab) also matters, because it affects both the drying protocol and what the policy will pay for.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

North Texas doesn't see hard freezes often, but when one does hit, it tends to catch homes off guard: exposed pipes in attics, garages, and exterior walls that were never insulated for sustained cold are the most common failure points in Richardson during a freeze event. Outside of freeze season, the more common cause is aging supply lines, particularly in slab-foundation homes built before the 1980s, where galvanized or early copper piping has simply reached the end of its service life.
