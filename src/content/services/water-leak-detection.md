A water bill that jumps for no obvious reason, a warm spot on the slab, a musty smell behind a baseboard that won't go away no matter how many times you mop. These are the signs of a hidden leak, and in Richardson's mix of slab-on-grade ranch homes and newer two-story builds, the water is often moving somewhere you can't see it: under the foundation, inside a wall cavity, or along a copper line buried in the slab. Finding the source before it becomes a bigger repair is what leak detection is for.

## What water leak detection actually involves

Leak detection is diagnostic work, not demolition. The goal is to find the exact location and cause of a hidden water leak using the least invasive methods available, so that whoever makes the repair, whether that's a plumber or our own crew handling the resulting water damage, knows exactly where to cut. For a suspected slab leak, that typically means correlating sound through the concrete with acoustic listening equipment, checking line pressure, and using thermal imaging to spot temperature differences where hot water lines are leaking into the slab. For a water leak in a wall, we combine a moisture meter reading on the interior surface with thermal imaging and, when needed, a borescope through a small access point to confirm what's happening inside the cavity before anyone opens drywall. Most inspections take one to two hours on-site depending on the size of the home and how many fixtures or lines need to be checked.

## Our process

1. **Interview and visual walkthrough.** We start with what you've noticed, water bill trends, damp spots, sounds, musty odor, then walk the affected area and any adjacent rooms.
2. **Moisture mapping.** A moisture meter establishes the boundaries of the wet area on walls, baseboards, and flooring, giving us a starting zone rather than guessing.
3. **Acoustic and thermal scanning.** For suspected slab leaks, acoustic listening devices pick up the sound of water escaping under pressure, and thermal imaging highlights temperature anomalies consistent with a hot or cold line leak.
4. **Isolation and confirmation.** Where needed, we isolate plumbing zones or use pressure testing to narrow the leak to a specific line or fixture, confirming the location before any opening is made.
5. **Written findings.** You get a clear explanation of where the leak is, what's likely causing it, and what the repair path looks like, including whether water damage restoration is needed alongside the plumbing fix.

## What separates a good leak detection response from a bad one

The most common mistake is treating leak detection as a reason to start cutting drywall or jackhammering slab before the source is actually confirmed. That approach costs homeowners money in unnecessary opening and patching, and it can still miss the real leak if the first guess is wrong. A careful process cross-references at least two detection methods, acoustic plus thermal, or moisture mapping plus pressure testing, before recommending any invasive step. Insurance adjusters pay close attention to documentation here too: photos of moisture readings, thermal images, and a written explanation of method and findings support the claim far better than a verbal description of "found it under the floor." We also flag when a leak is actually a symptom of something else, like a failed wax ring under a toilet mimicking a slab leak, so the repair addresses the actual cause.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Richardson's clay-heavy soil shifts with the wet and dry cycle each year, and that movement puts stress on slab plumbing lines, particularly in homes built before PEX became standard. Summer heat also pushes more water usage through irrigation and AC condensate lines, which can mask or mimic a plumbing leak if the two aren't told apart. In older neighborhoods with copper supply lines run through the slab, pinhole leaks from age and water chemistry are a recurring pattern worth checking even without an obvious symptom yet.
