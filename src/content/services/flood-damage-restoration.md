## What flood damage restoration actually involves

Flood cleanup starts with identifying the water's category. Floodwater that has touched soil, storm drains, or septic overflow is typically classified as Category 2 or Category 3, which changes how materials are handled. Porous materials like carpet pad, drywall, and insulation that have been in contact with contaminated floodwater usually can't be dried in place and have to come out. Hard surfaces, framing, and subfloor can often be dried and disinfected if the response happens before saturation sets deep. Equipment on a flood job runs heavier than a typical water loss: submersible pumps for standing water over an inch or two, truck-mount extraction for carpet and pad, and a larger air mover and dehumidifier count because the structure has absorbed more moisture over a longer window. A post-flood restoration timeline for a single-story home runs anywhere from a few days to over a week depending on how long the water sat before the call came in.

## Our process

1. **Water categorization and safety check.** Before anything else, we identify the water source and category, check for electrical hazards, and confirm the structure is safe to work in.
2. **Extraction and contaminated material removal.** Standing water gets pumped and extracted first, followed by removal of flood-contacted porous materials that can't be safely dried, such as carpet pad, baseboards, and drywall up to the flood line.
3. **Cleaning and antimicrobial application.** Remaining structural surfaces get cleaned and treated to address contamination left behind by floodwater, which is a step that doesn't apply to a clean-water pipe break.
4. **Drying with daily moisture monitoring.** Air movers and dehumidifiers run continuously while we track moisture content in framing, subfloor, and drywall with meters, adjusting equipment placement as readings change.
5. **Documentation for the claim.** Photos, moisture logs, and material inventories are compiled throughout so the paperwork matches what actually happened on site.

## What separates a good flood response from a bad one

The most common mistake on flood jobs is treating floodwater like a clean water loss and trying to dry materials that should have been removed. Carpet pad and drywall that absorb contaminated water and get dried in place instead of replaced can trap bacteria and organic matter inside wall cavities, which shows up later as odor or mold growth that's harder and more expensive to chase down. Another frequent miss is stopping moisture monitoring too early because surface readings look dry while the framing or subfloor underneath is still holding moisture. Insurance adjusters reviewing a flood claim typically look for a clear water category determination, documented removal of contaminated materials, and moisture logs that show the structure reached dry standard before equipment came out, not just that it looked dry on day three.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Richardson sees its heaviest flood risk during spring and early summer storm systems, when heavy rainfall can overwhelm storm drains and low-lying lots faster than the ground can absorb it. Clay-heavy soil common across North Texas doesn't drain quickly, which means water can pool against foundations and work its way in through cracks or slab penetrations well after the rain stops. Homes with older cast-iron or clay sewer laterals are also more prone to backup during heavy rain events when municipal lines surcharge, which is a different flood scenario than surface water intrusion and changes the contamination level of the water involved.
