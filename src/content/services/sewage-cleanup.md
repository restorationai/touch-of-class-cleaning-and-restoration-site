**Sewage backing up through a floor drain, toilet, or tub is a biohazard emergency, not a mop-and-bucket job. Call now for emergency service.** Once wastewater breaches a fixture or line, it carries bacteria, viruses, and parasites into flooring, drywall, and subfloor within minutes, and the longer it sits, the more porous material it ruins. Whether the cause is a collapsed sewer lateral, a septic tank that's overflowed, or a clog pushing waste back into the house, the response has to move fast and follow a different protocol than a clean-water loss.

## What sewage cleanup and sanitization actually involves

Sewage intrusion is classified as Category 3 water, the most contaminated tier in the restoration industry, which means every porous material it touches (carpet, pad, drywall below the flood line, most insulation) is generally treated as unsalvageable rather than dried in place. The work starts with containing the affected area to stop cross-contamination into clean parts of the home, then removing standing water and solid waste, pulling out ruined materials, and sanitizing everything that remains with hospital-grade antimicrobials. Depending on how far the water traveled and how long it sat, this can mean anything from a single bathroom to multiple rooms where the backup pushed up through low-lying fixtures. Air scrubbers and negative air setups are common where odor and airborne contamination are a concern, and moisture meters track drying progress on the structural materials that are kept.

## Our process

1. **PPE and containment staging.** Crews suit up in protective gear before entering, and the affected zone is sealed off with poly barriers to keep contaminated water and airborne particles from spreading to unaffected rooms.
2. **Extraction and solid waste removal.** Standing sewage and solid material is pumped and removed first, using equipment dedicated to contaminated water so nothing cross-contaminates a future clean-water job.
3. **Demolition of unsalvageable materials.** Carpet, pad, and drywall that absorbed Category 3 water are cut out and bagged per standard waste-handling practice; this step is non-negotiable for anything porous that contacted raw sewage.
4. **Antimicrobial treatment and sanitization.** Remaining studs, subfloor, and hard surfaces are cleaned and treated with EPA-registered disinfectants, then re-checked before any drying equipment goes in.
5. **Drying, monitoring, and odor control.** Air movers and dehumidifiers bring remaining structural moisture down to normal levels, with daily readings logged until the space tests dry, and deodorizing treatment addresses any residual odor from the backup.

## What separates a good sewage cleanup response from a bad one

The most common mistake is treating a sewage backup like a regular water loss: drying out carpet and drywall instead of removing it. Category 3 water saturates porous materials with bacteria that drying alone doesn't eliminate, and skipping demolition to save money up front almost always means a mold or odor callback later. Another gap is incomplete containment, where cleanup crews work the visible mess but don't seal off HVAC returns or adjoining rooms, letting contaminated air migrate through the house. Adjusters reviewing a sewage claim typically want to see documentation of what was removed versus dried, photos of the contamination path, and antimicrobial application logs, since this supports both the scope of work and the health and safety rationale for demolition that a clean-water loss wouldn't require.

## What does sewage cleanup and sanitization cost?

Costs vary with how much of the home was affected, how long the sewage sat before cleanup started, and whether the source was a sewer line, septic system, or plumbing failure inside the house. Every loss gets a written scope before work begins, since the extent of demolition and sanitization can't be accurately priced from a phone call.
