**Raw sewage in your home is a health emergency, not a cleanup chore. Call now for emergency service.**

A sewage backup is different from any other water event in the house. Toilet paper, waste solids, and bacteria-laden water that backs up through a floor drain or overflows from a toilet is classified as Category 3, the most contaminated water category in the industry. It carries E. coli, salmonella, and other pathogens that can soak into drywall, subflooring, and carpet pad within minutes, and the smell alone signals that porous materials are already compromised. Richardson homes on older sewer laterals, especially in neighborhoods built before the 1980s, see this more often than newer construction with updated clay or PVC lines.

## What sewage cleanup and sanitization actually involves

Sewage cleanup is not the same scope as a clean-water extraction. Crews entering a sewage-affected space wear PPE (gloves, respirators, boot covers) before anything else happens, because the water itself is a contamination source, not just a moisture problem. Standing sewage gets pumped or extracted first, then every porous material it touched, carpet, pad, baseboard, drywall below the flood line, often gets removed rather than dried, because Category 3 water saturates those materials faster than it can be sanitized out. Hard surfaces (tile, sealed concrete, some hardwood) can sometimes be cleaned and treated in place. The timeline depends on how long the sewage sat before discovery: a backup caught within an hour is a different job than one that soaked a finished basement overnight.

## Our process

1. **PPE staging and containment.** The affected area is isolated with poly sheeting and negative air where needed, so contaminated water and odor don't migrate into unaffected rooms.
2. **Sewage extraction and solids removal.** Standing waste water and solid material are pumped and removed first, using extraction equipment rated for contaminated water, not standard wet-vacs.
3. **Removal of unsalvageable porous materials.** Carpet, pad, and drywall that absorbed Category 3 water are cut out and bagged per disposal protocol rather than dried, since drying does not neutralize the pathogen load.
4. **Antimicrobial and enzymatic treatment.** Remaining structural materials and surfaces are treated with hospital-grade antimicrobials and enzymatic cleaners that break down residual organic contamination, cleaned to the IICRC S540 standard for sewage-contaminated structures.
5. **Drying, deodorization, and verification.** Structural drying equipment runs until moisture readings confirm the space is dry, and odor treatment (not just masking spray) addresses what's left in subfloor and wall cavities.

## What separates a good sewage cleanup response from a bad one

The most common mistake is trying to dry and save materials that should have been removed. Drywall, insulation, and carpet pad that sat in sewage for more than a short window can't be reliably sanitized; drying them just locks contamination behind a clean-looking surface. A second mistake is skipping enzymatic or antimicrobial treatment on hard surfaces because they "look clean" after wiping. Insurance adjusters reviewing a sewage claim typically want documentation of the contamination category, photos of affected materials before removal, and a disposal record for anything discarded, so a response that skips that paper trail can slow down claim approval even when the physical work was done correctly.

## What does sewage cleanup and sanitization cost?

Costs vary with how much sewage was involved, how long it sat, and how many porous materials have to be removed versus just sanitized. ## Seasonal & regional considerations

Heavy spring and early-summer rain across North Texas pushes groundwater and stormwater into aging sewer laterals, which is when municipal lines and private laterals alike are most likely to surcharge back into a home through the lowest drain. Root intrusion into older clay sewer lines is also a recurring cause of slow backups that show up as recurring drain gurgling before a full backup happens. Warm, humid stretches accelerate bacterial growth in any sewage-affected material that wasn't fully removed or treated, which is one more reason porous materials get cut out rather than dried in place.
