A kitchen that's five years past its prime, a bathroom with a leaking shower pan, or a wall that still needs framing after a restoration crew finished drying it out: these are different starting points, but they land in the same place. Someone has to scope the work, pull the right permits, and rebuild it so it passes inspection and looks like it was never a problem in the first place. That's general contracting, and it's a different skill set than the demo-day version most homeowners picture.

## What renovations, remodels and general contracting actually involves

General contracting covers everything between "here's what we want" and a finished space that's move-in ready: design coordination, permitting, demolition, framing, rough-in trades (electrical, plumbing, HVAC), inspections, and finish work like cabinetry, flooring, paint, and trim. Kitchen remodels usually touch plumbing and electrical at minimum; bathroom remodels almost always involve waterproofing and ventilation changes. Post-damage rebuild work is its own category: after a restoration crew has extracted water, removed damaged materials, and verified the structure is dry, someone has to frame it, rough it in, and finish it back to code. Timelines vary widely. A single bathroom remodel might run four to six weeks. A full kitchen can run six to ten. A post-damage rebuild depends entirely on how much was removed during mitigation and how fast permits clear.

## Our process

1. **Walkthrough and scope of work.** We document existing conditions, measurements, and (for post-damage jobs) what was removed during mitigation, then put the scope in writing before any demo starts.
2. **Permitting and design coordination.** Structural changes, electrical panel work, and most plumbing relocations require permits through the City of Richardson. We handle the submission and schedule required inspections at each stage.
3. **Demolition and framing.** Damaged or outdated materials come out, framing goes back in true and level, and we flag anything the original construction didn't account for (undersized headers, outdated wiring, missing vapor barriers) before closing walls.
4. **Rough-in and inspection.** Electrical, plumbing, and HVAC trades complete their rough-in work, and we schedule inspections before insulation and drywall go up, not after.
5. **Finish work and final walkthrough.** Cabinetry, tile, flooring, paint, and fixtures go in last. We walk the finished space with the homeowner against the original scope before calling the job done.

## What separates a good general contracting response from a bad one

The most common failure point is sequencing: closing up walls before inspections are scheduled, or finishing a bathroom before confirming the waterproofing passed. That creates rework and, on insurance-related jobs, can create documentation gaps an adjuster will flag. A second failure point on post-damage rebuilds specifically: rebuilding before moisture readings confirm the structure is actually dry, which traps the next problem behind new drywall. A third is material mismatch, replacing a section of flooring, trim, or siding with something that doesn't match the surrounding material closely enough to pass muster on an insurance claim or simply look right. A contractor who's done rebuild work alongside restoration work knows to photograph conditions before demo, keep moisture logs where relevant, and write a scope detailed enough that there's no ambiguity about what's included.

## What does renovations, remodels and general contracting cost?

Costs vary by scope, materials, and whether the work is a straightforward remodel or a rebuild following a water, fire, or storm loss. Every project is different, which is why Touch of Class Cleaning and Restoration provides a written scope of work before any work begins, rather than a ballpark figure. Where a rebuild follows a covered loss, homeowners insurance typically covers restoring the space to its pre-loss condition, not upgrades beyond that, so betterments are usually a separate line item.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Richardson sits on clay-heavy North Texas soil that expands and contracts with seasonal moisture swings, which is part of why foundation movement and slab cracking show up often in renovation scopes here, especially in homes built before current foundation standards. Summer heat and humidity also affect timeline: exterior work and anything involving open walls moves faster outside peak heat, and HVAC rough-in scheduling tightens up during the hottest months when trade crews are in high demand across the metro.
