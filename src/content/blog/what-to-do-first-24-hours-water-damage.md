Water damage doesn't wait for a convenient time, and neither does the clock that starts the moment a pipe bursts, a water heater fails, or a storm finds its way through your roof. The first 24 hours determine whether you're dealing with a straightforward dry-out or a mold problem that follows you for months. In short: stop the water source, cut power to affected areas if there's any risk of contact with standing water, remove items that can be saved, and start documenting everything before you touch or move anything else. Then get air moving and call a restoration company or your insurance carrier, whichever makes sense for your situation.

Here's how to work through that first day without making the damage worse.

## Why the First 24 Hours Matter So Much

Drywall, insulation, and subflooring don't need days to become a mold problem. Under the right conditions, warm, humid, and no airflow, mold spores that are already present in nearly every home can begin colonizing wet materials within 24 to 48 hours. Richardson's North Texas climate makes this worse than it would be in a drier region: summer humidity sits high for months at a time, and homes built on slab foundations (common throughout the area) can trap moisture against the concrete with nowhere to evaporate.

The category of water matters too. Clean water from a supply line behaves very differently than water that's backed up through a drain or come in from outside. The longer any of it sits, the more materials it touches and degrades, and the more those materials shift from "salvageable" to "replace."

## Immediate Steps: What to Do First

1. **Stop the water at the source.** For a burst pipe or supply line failure, shut off the main water valve, usually located where the line enters the house or near the water heater. For an appliance leak (dishwasher, washing machine, water heater), shut off the valve feeding that specific unit.
2. **Cut power to the area if water is near outlets, switches, or appliances.** If the breaker panel itself is in or near standing water, do not approach it. Call an electrician or wait for a professional with the right equipment.
3. **Move what you can.** Lift furniture legs onto foil or wood blocks, pull rugs off wet flooring, and relocate boxes, electronics, and anything on the floor to a dry room.
4. **Photograph everything before you clean up.** Wide shots of the room, close-ups of damaged materials, and the source of the leak itself. Insurance adjusters work from documentation, and photos taken in the moment carry more weight than a description later.
5. **Start pulling moisture out of the air.** Open windows if the outdoor humidity is lower than indoors (check a weather app, don't guess), run ceiling fans, and point box fans or a shop vac at standing water if you have one.
6. **Call your insurance company and a water damage restoration company.** Even if you're not sure yet whether you'll file a claim, getting a timeline started protects you either way.

## What NOT to Do

- **Don't use a regular household vacuum on standing water.** It's an electrocution risk and will likely destroy the vacuum. Use a wet/dry shop vac rated for liquid pickup, or wait for a professional extraction unit.
- **Don't walk into standing water near outlets, appliances, or the breaker panel** until power to that area is confirmed off.
- **Don't assume carpet padding can be saved.** Padding acts like a sponge and almost always needs to be removed and replaced after any significant water intrusion, even when the carpet itself is salvageable.
- **Don't close up the house and leave for the day** thinking you'll deal with it tonight. Trapped moisture with no airflow is exactly what accelerates mold growth and warps wood flooring and cabinetry.
- **Don't throw away damaged items before documenting them.** Once it's in the trash, it's much harder to include in an insurance claim.
- **Don't paint or patch drywall yourself right away** if the wall cavity is still wet. Sealing in moisture behind fresh paint or patched drywall is a common mistake that leads to hidden mold later.

## When the Situation Calls for a Professional

A small, caught-early leak, like a dishwasher supply line that leaked a cup of water onto a tile floor, is often something a homeowner can dry out with fans and a mop. The situations below are different:

- Water has been sitting for more than a few hours, or you don't know how long it's been leaking.
- The water came from a source other than clean supply lines (a sewer backup, groundwater, or storm flooding), which carries contamination risk.
- Water has reached behind walls, under flooring, or into a ceiling cavity. If a wall feels soft, a baseboard has started to separate from the drywall, or you hear a faint trickling sound after the leak source is already shut off, water has traveled somewhere you can't see.
- You're filing an insurance claim and want professional documentation of moisture levels and affected materials.
- You notice a musty smell, which usually means mold growth has already started, even if you can't see it yet.

## What Recovery Looks Like After Day One

Once the standing water is gone and affected items are out of the space, the next phase is structural drying, which typically runs several days, not hours. This is the part homeowners underestimate most often: materials that feel dry to the touch on the surface, like drywall or a wood subfloor, can still hold significant moisture underneath. Professional drying uses air movers and dehumidifiers set up in a specific configuration, with moisture readings taken daily to confirm when materials have actually reached a safe, dry standard rather than just looking dry.

Depending on what the water touched, this phase may also include removing and disposing of materials that can't be dried, such as saturated insulation or swollen particleboard cabinetry. Anything structural that was cut out or removed during drying, like a section of drywall, will need to be replaced before the space is back to normal.

If you filed an insurance claim, expect an adjuster to review the documentation and either visit the property or request photos and the restoration company's moisture logs. Having a restoration company's paperwork on hand, including daily readings and a drying log, tends to make that part of the process move faster.
