If a fire has damaged your Richwood home, the restoration process generally follows five stages: securing the structure, assessing damage, removing soot and smoke residue, deep cleaning and deodorizing, and repairing or rebuilding what was lost. The timeline depends on how much of the home burned versus how much suffered smoke and water damage from firefighting efforts, but most of the work happens in the first two weeks, with full recovery often taking one to three months. Here's what actually happens at each stage, and what you should be doing while it's underway.

## What a Fire Actually Does to a House

Flames are often the smallest part of the damage. Smoke travels through HVAC ducts, gaps in framing, and even closed drawers, leaving soot on surfaces far from the fire's origin. That soot is acidic and will keep etching into metal, grout, and finishes for days if it isn't removed. On top of that, water from sprinklers or hoses soaks into drywall, subfloor, and insulation, which means a fire loss is almost always a water loss too.

The type of smoke matters for how it's cleaned. A fast, high-heat fire (wood, paper, typical furniture) leaves dry, powdery soot that's easier to wipe away. A slow, smoldering fire (synthetic materials, foam, electrical fires) leaves a thick, oily residue that smears if you touch it and bonds to porous surfaces like drywall and carpet. Protein fires, common in kitchen fires where food burns, leave an almost invisible residue with an intense odor that's hard to trace to a single surface.

## Immediate Steps After the Fire Is Out

1. **Wait for the all-clear from the fire department** before re-entering. Structural damage isn't always visible, and smoldering pockets can reignite.
2. **Document everything before you touch anything.** Photograph every room, every damaged item, and the exterior of the structure. Insurance adjusters rely heavily on early photos.
3. **Shut off utilities if the fire department hasn't already.** Gas lines and electrical systems exposed to fire can be compromised even if they look intact.
4. **Board up broken windows and tarp damaged roof sections** to keep rain and wildlife out while the claim is processed. This is often the single most important step for preventing a second round of water damage on top of the fire.
5. **Call your insurance company** to open the claim and ask what documentation they require before cleanup begins.
6. **Remove undamaged valuables** like documents, electronics, and medications if it's safe to access those areas, since lingering soot and moisture will continue to degrade them.

## What Not to Do

- **Don't wipe soot with a wet cloth.** Water activates and sets the acidic residue into fabric, drywall, and finishes, making it far harder to remove later. Dry chemical sponges are the standard tool for this reason.
- **Don't turn on the HVAC system.** If smoke got into the ductwork, running the system spreads soot and odor to rooms that were otherwise untouched.
- **Don't throw away damaged items before they're photographed and itemized.** Even items that look like a total loss may need to be documented for the claim.
- **Don't assume a room with no visible flame damage is fine.** Smoke odor embeds in drywall, insulation, and framing, and it will resurface in humid weather if it isn't properly treated.
- **Don't try to repaint over soot stains.** Residue will bleed through paint within weeks if the surface wasn't cleaned and sealed first.

## When to Call a Professional

Small, contained incidents, like a scorched pot on the stove with minimal smoke spread, can sometimes be handled with thorough cleaning and ventilation. But most house fires involve some combination of structural char, smoke penetration into HVAC and wall cavities, and water intrusion from suppression efforts, and that combination is difficult to resolve with store-bought cleaners and an open window.

A professional fire damage restoration crew brings specialized equipment: HEPA air scrubbers to pull soot particulates out of the air, thermal foggers to neutralize odor at a molecular level rather than masking it, and moisture meters to check whether water from firefighting has reached the subfloor or wall cavities. ## The Longer Recovery Process

Once the structure is secured and the loss is documented, the actual restoration work typically moves through these phases:

**Assessment and scoping.** A technician walks the property to map which materials are salvageable, which need deep cleaning, and which need to be removed entirely. Drywall, insulation, and carpet padding that absorbed smoke odor or firefighting water often fall into the removal category even if they look structurally fine.

**Content and structural cleaning.** Soot removal starts with dry sponging and HEPA vacuuming on salvageable surfaces, followed by wet cleaning with specialized agents for anything the dry methods can't fully lift. Hard contents like furniture and electronics may be cleaned on-site or packed out to a cleaning facility depending on the severity.

**Odor treatment.** This is the step people underestimate. Surface cleaning removes visible soot, but smoke odor requires a separate process, thermal fogging, ozone treatment, or hydroxyl generators, to neutralize particles that have worked into porous materials. Skipping this step is the most common reason a home smells like smoke again once the weather turns humid.

**Drying and dehumidification.** Any water introduced during the fire or suppression effort has to be dried according to the same standards used for a standalone water loss, since trapped moisture behind drywall or under flooring sets up conditions for mold within 24 to 48 hours.

**Repair and reconstruction.** Depending on the damage, this ranges from replacing a section of drywall and repainting to rebuilding framing, flooring, and cabinetry in badly burned rooms. This is typically the longest phase and the one most tied to insurance approval timelines.

Most homeowners are back in a fully restored home within a few weeks for moderate damage, though severe structural loss can extend that to a few months. Staying in close contact with your adjuster and your restoration contractor throughout keeps the process moving instead of stalling on paperwork.
