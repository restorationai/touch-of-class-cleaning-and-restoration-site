A burst pipe means water is actively escaping your plumbing system, and your first move is to shut off the water at the main valve, not just the fixture nearest the break. After that, kill the power to any affected area if water is near outlets or panels, then start moving water and valuables away from the area while you figure out what broke and how bad it is.

## What's Actually Happening

A "burst" pipe is different from a slow leak. Copper and galvanized lines fail from corrosion or age, CPVC and PEX can split at a weak fitting, and in North Texas the most common trigger is a hard freeze: water inside the pipe expands, pressure builds past a joint or a thin section of wall, and when temperatures climb back up the ice plug melts and the break lets go all at once. Richardson sees enough freeze events most winters that homes built in the 1960s through 80s, many on slab foundations with plumbing runs through exterior walls or the attic, are especially exposed. A burst under a slab or inside a wall cavity often shows up first as a damp baseboard, a soft spot in flooring, or a faint running-water sound with no faucet on, well before you see standing water.

## Immediate Steps

1. **Shut off the main water supply.** The valve is usually near the water meter or where the line enters the house. Turning off just the fixture valve won't stop water feeding a wall or slab leak.
2. **Cut power to the area if water is near wiring, outlets, or a breaker panel.** If reaching the panel means wading through water, skip it and call an electrician instead.
3. **Contain and remove standing water.** Towels, a wet-vac, or buckets for small amounts. For anything beyond what you can mop up in a few minutes, it's a job for extraction equipment, not a mop.
4. **Move furniture, rugs, and electronics off wet flooring.** Prop up furniture legs on foil or wood blocks if you can't move pieces entirely.
5. **Open cabinets and pull back baseboards where you suspect hidden water.** Trapped water behind cabinetry or baseboard keeps materials wet long after the surface looks dry.
6. **Photograph everything before you clean up.** Wet drywall, pooled water, damaged belongings. Your insurance adjuster will ask for this.
7. **Call your insurance carrier** to open a claim and ask what documentation they require going forward.
8. **Call a restoration company** if the water has touched flooring, drywall, insulation, or has been sitting for more than an hour or two.

## What Not to Do

- **Don't use a household shop vac rated for dry debris only on standing water near an outlet.** Check it's rated for wet pickup, and don't run it if you're standing in water near live power.
- **Don't turn on fans and walk away for the day.** Air movement without proper dehumidification can push moisture into wall cavities and ceilings instead of drying the room.
- **Don't assume a floor that looks dry is dry.** Hardwood, laminate, and subfloor can read dry on the surface while the material underneath stays saturated for days. This is exactly how mold gets a foothold, often within 24 to 48 hours in a warm, humid space.
- **Don't cut into drywall or pull up flooring yourself if you plan to file an insurance claim**, until the damage is documented. Demolition before documentation can complicate what the carrier is willing to cover.
- **Don't ignore a burst pipe connected to a washing machine, dishwasher, or ice maker line** just because it seems like a small appliance issue. Supply line failures behind appliances cause some of the most damage precisely because they go unnoticed until flooring or cabinetry is already compromised, which is its own category of loss separate from a wall or slab break.

## When to Call a Professional

A wet-vac and a box fan handle a small spill. They don't handle water that has gotten into wall cavities, under flooring, into insulation, or has been sitting long enough to start smelling musty. Signs it's time to call in a water damage restoration team: water traveled beyond the room where the pipe broke, you can hear or feel sponginess underfoot, baseboards or drywall are swelling, or more than a few hours passed before you caught the leak. Professional extraction and drying equipment, along with moisture meters that can read what's happening inside a wall or under a floor, catches what a home fan setup can't. If the burst came from behind a washing machine, refrigerator, or water heater, that's worth a call specifically about appliance leak cleanup, since those losses often involve cabinetry, subfloor, and sometimes the wall behind the unit in ways a simple mop-up won't resolve.

## The Recovery Process

Once the active leak is stopped and water is extracted, recovery follows a few predictable phases. First is moisture mapping: a technician checks walls, flooring, and framing with a moisture meter to find out how far the water actually traveled, which is almost always farther than it looks. Second is structural drying, placing air movers and dehumidifiers and tracking moisture readings daily until materials return to a normal range, not just until they look dry. Depending on how long the water sat, this can mean several days of monitoring. Third, if drywall, insulation, flooring, or cabinetry absorbed water and can't be dried in place, those materials get removed and the space is prepped for rebuild. Last is the repair itself: patching drywall, replacing flooring, reinstalling trim and cabinetry so the space looks like nothing happened. Keep every receipt and photo through this process. Most insurance carriers want a clear paper trail from the moment of discovery to the final repair, and a restoration company that documents as they go makes that claim conversation much easier.
