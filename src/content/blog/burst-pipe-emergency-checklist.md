**Pipe just burst?** Shut off your main water valve first, then keep reading; every minute of running water adds to the cleanup ahead. A burst pipe can dump gallons of water into your home in minutes, soaking flooring, drywall, and insulation before you've even found a towel. The single most important thing you can do in the first sixty seconds is stop the water at the source, not mop up what's already on the floor. Once the water is off, the next hour is about containing damage and documenting what happened, not panicking over the mess.

## What's Actually Happening When a Pipe Bursts

Most residential pipe failures in Richwood come from one of three causes: a sudden freeze that expands trapped water inside copper or PEX lines, a slow-building corrosion failure in older galvanized piping, or a joint that finally gives out under water pressure after years of wear. Unlike a dripping faucet, a true burst releases water under full line pressure, which is why a small split in a supply line can flood a laundry room or kitchen in minutes. If the break is inside a wall, you may not see water right away; instead, look for a baseboard that's starting to bow, paint that's bubbling, or a damp spot spreading across drywall. By the time you can see it, the water has usually already reached the wall cavity, subfloor, or insulation behind it.

## Immediate Steps: The First 30 Minutes

1. **Shut off the main water valve.** This is usually near the water meter or where the line enters the house. If you don't know where yours is, find it now, before you need it in a hurry.
2. **Cut the power to the affected area** at the breaker panel if water is near outlets, switches, or anything electrical. Never step into standing water to reach a breaker panel yourself.
3. **Move anything you can out of the water's path**, rugs, furniture, electronics, boxes stored on the floor. Every minute these sit in water increases the chance they're unsalvageable.
4. **Start removing standing water** with towels, a mop, or a wet/dry shop vac if you have one. Focus on pooled water first; a few inches on a hard floor can wick into baseboards and subflooring within hours.
5. **Photograph everything** before you move or dry anything: the source of the leak, the extent of standing water, and any items that got wet. These photos matter later for an insurance claim.
6. **Open windows and run fans** if the weather allows it, to start air movement. Don't rely on this alone though; surface-dry doesn't mean the wall cavity or subfloor underneath is dry.

If the burst happened at an appliance connection, a washing machine hose, a dishwasher line, an ice maker supply, the fix is often as simple as shutting off that appliance's individual shutoff valve rather than the whole house main. Appliance leak cleanup tends to be more contained but just as urgent if it's been running unnoticed for hours.

## What Not to Do

- **Don't turn the water back on** to "test" whether the pipe is still leaking. If it failed once, it can fail again immediately under pressure.
- **Don't run a space heater or heat gun directly on wet drywall or flooring** to speed up drying. Uneven heat can warp materials and doesn't address water trapped behind walls or under flooring.
- **Don't ignore water that "looks dry" on the surface.** Drywall, subfloor, and wood framing can hold moisture well after the visible wetness is gone, and that trapped moisture is exactly what mold needs to take hold within 24 to 48 hours.
- **Don't wait to call your insurance company** if the damage is significant. Most policies require prompt notice, and delays can complicate a claim.
- **Don't assume a shop vac and a box fan fully dried the space.** Surface drying and structural drying are different things; a moisture meter reading tells you what your eyes can't.

## When to Call a Professional

A few towels and a fan might genuinely handle a small, quickly caught leak with no wall or flooring involvement. But if any of the following apply, it's worth bringing in a water damage restoration team rather than handling it solo:

- Water reached carpet padding, subfloor, or the inside of a wall
- The leak ran for more than a few minutes before you found it
- You can smell a musty or damp odor starting within the first day
- The affected area includes finished flooring like hardwood, laminate, or tile
- You're filing an insurance claim and need documented moisture readings

Professional drying isn't just bigger fans. Equipment like air movers and dehumidifiers is positioned and run based on moisture readings taken with a meter, following the IICRC S500 water damage drying standard, and those readings get rechecked daily until the materials are actually back to a normal moisture range, not just "not wet to the touch." Skipping that step is how a one-day leak turns into a mold problem three weeks later.

## The Recovery Process After the Water Stops

Once the active leak is resolved, recovery typically moves through a few phases: extracting any remaining standing water, assessing which materials can be dried in place versus which need to be removed (think water-logged carpet pad or swollen baseboard), setting up drying equipment, and monitoring moisture levels until everything reads dry. Drywall that was saturated for an extended period may need to come out entirely, since wet gypsum board loses structural integrity and rarely dries back to its original state. Flooring reactions vary a lot: solid hardwood sometimes survives with careful drying, while laminate and engineered wood with a particleboard core usually doesn't. Throughout this process, keep your documentation, photos, a written timeline of when you noticed the leak and when the water was shut off, and any receipts for emergency mitigation. That paperwork makes the insurance side of this much smoother.
