## Restoration emergencies common in Plano

Plano sits in the Blackland Prairie region of North Texas, where heavy clay soil expands when wet and shrinks when dry. That movement stresses slab foundations and the plumbing lines running through and under them, which is why so many Plano water losses start as a slab leak rather than a visible pipe burst. Add in North Texas's pattern of long dry stretches followed by intense spring and early-summer thunderstorms, and you get a city that sees both foundation-related leaks and sudden storm water intrusion in the same year. Summer hail and straight-line wind events also bring a steady stream of roof damage that turns into attic and ceiling water intrusion if a tarp doesn't go up quickly. Winter adds its own risk: Texas's occasional hard freezes catch homes that aren't built for prolonged cold, and pipes in exterior walls or garages split when insulation wasn't designed for single-digit nights.

## Building stock, site conditions, and permits in Plano

Most of Plano's housing stock dates from the 1970s through the 2000s suburban growth era, built slab-on-grade with wood-frame walls and composition shingle roofs. That construction means water intrusion typically travels sideways through baseboards and into wall cavities rather than pooling in a basement, since there isn't one. Plumbing in homes from the 1980s sometimes includes polybutylene supply lines, a material known for failing at fittings, while newer construction uses copper or PEX.
