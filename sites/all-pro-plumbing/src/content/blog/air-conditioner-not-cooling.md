**TL;DR:** When your AC runs but stops cooling, the cause is almost always one of nine things: a wrong thermostat setting, a clogged air filter, a tripped breaker on the outdoor unit, a failed run capacitor, low refrigerant from a leak, a dirty condenser coil, a frozen evaporator coil, a failing compressor, or undersized equipment. The first three you can check in five minutes. The rest need a licensed HVAC technician with gauges and a meter.

Your air handler is running, air is moving through the vents, but the house is sitting at 85 degrees and climbing. This is the most common AC complaint during a heat stretch, and it almost always means one thing: the refrigeration cycle is broken somewhere. The fan circuit and the cooling circuit are separate. A running fan tells you the electrical side is partially alive. It tells you nothing about whether refrigerant is moving, whether the compressor is pumping, or whether the outdoor coil can actually shed heat. Here is how to work through the list.

## Why is my AC running but not blowing cold air?

Your AC blows warm air when the refrigeration cycle fails, even though the blower fan keeps running. The fan and the compressor run on different circuits, so the blower can operate normally while the cooling side is completely dead. The fix depends on which part of the cycle failed.

The refrigeration cycle works like this: the indoor evaporator coil absorbs heat from your house air, the refrigerant carries that heat outside, and the condenser coil dumps it into the outdoor air. Break any link in that chain and the air coming out of your vents will be room temperature or warmer. Nine things break that chain most often.

## What can I check myself before calling a technician?

Three causes are safe for a homeowner to check without tools: thermostat settings, the air filter, and the outdoor unit's breaker.

**1. Thermostat set to FAN instead of AUTO.** If someone switched the fan setting from AUTO to ON, the blower runs continuously even when the compressor is off. The air coming out will be uncooled room air. Set the fan back to AUTO and give the system 15 minutes.

**2. Clogged air filter.** A filter that is packed with dust restricts airflow across the evaporator coil. Without enough airflow, the coil gets too cold, frost forms on it, and the system stops cooling. Check the filter first. If it is gray and dense, replace it (1-inch filters should be swapped every 30-60 days in a dusty climate). Then turn the system off and let the coil thaw for 2-3 hours before restarting.

**3. Tripped breaker on the outdoor unit.** Most central AC systems have two breakers: one for the air handler and one for the outdoor condenser. If the condenser breaker tripped, the fan inside keeps running but the compressor and outdoor fan are off. Check your electrical panel for a breaker that is in the middle position (tripped) and reset it once. If it trips again immediately, stop and call a technician. A breaker that keeps tripping means a short or an overloaded component, and forcing it is a fire risk.

## What are the mechanical causes that need a technician?

If the thermostat, filter, and breaker check out, the problem is inside the refrigeration system. These four causes account for most professional AC calls.

**4. Failed run capacitor.** The run capacitor is a small cylindrical component inside the outdoor unit that gives the compressor and condenser fan motor the electrical kick they need to start and keep running. Capacitors fail from heat and age, and Bakersfield summers are hard on them. A failed capacitor often makes the outdoor unit hum without the fan spinning, or the unit may short-cycle (run for a few seconds, shut off, repeat). A technician can test a capacitor with a multimeter in about two minutes and replace it in under 30. It is one of the more affordable AC repairs.

**5. Low refrigerant from a leak.** Refrigerant does not get used up like fuel. If the system is low, there is a leak somewhere. Low refrigerant means the evaporator coil cannot absorb enough heat, so the air coming out of your vents is barely cooler than room temperature. Signs include ice on the refrigerant line near the indoor unit, a hissing or bubbling sound near the outdoor unit, and a system that runs constantly without reaching setpoint. A technician will check system pressures with manifold gauges, locate the leak, repair it, and recharge to the manufacturer's specified pressure. Under [EPA Section 608](https://www.epa.gov/section608), refrigerant must be handled by a certified technician. It cannot legally be purchased or charged by a homeowner.

**6. Dirty condenser coil.** This one deserves its own section.

## Why does a dirty outdoor coil matter so much in Kern County?

A dirty condenser coil is one of the most common reasons a central AC system runs continuously but cannot keep up on a 105-degree day. The outdoor coil has to dump all the heat it pulled from your house into the outside air. When the coil fins are packed with dust, cottonwood, and debris, that heat transfer is blocked and the system loses capacity fast.

Kern County's combination of agricultural dust, cottonwood season, and triple-digit heat makes this problem worse here than in most parts of California. A condenser coil that looks clean from a distance can have a thick layer of fine dust bonded to the fins on the inside face, invisible until you pull the unit apart.

A technician will shut the unit down, remove the top panel, and wash the coil from the inside out with a low-pressure coil cleaner and water. Do not use a pressure washer on condenser fins. The aluminum fins bend easily and a bent fin is a blocked fin. After a proper cleaning, a system that was struggling to reach 78 degrees on a hot afternoon will often hit setpoint again without any other repair.

For most Bakersfield homes, a condenser coil cleaning once a year before peak summer is the single best maintenance investment you can make. The [U.S. Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/air-conditioning) estimates that a well-maintained AC system uses significantly less energy than a neglected one.

**7. Frozen evaporator coil.** Ice on the indoor coil blocks airflow completely. The air coming out of the vents will drop to almost nothing, or will feel slightly cool but with very little volume. Causes include a clogged filter (see above), low refrigerant, or a failing blower motor. If you see ice on the refrigerant line where it enters the air handler, turn the system off and switch the fan to ON to thaw the coil. Do not run the compressor while the coil is frozen. Once thawed, replace the filter and restart. If it freezes again, call for a refrigerant check.

**8. Failing or failed compressor.** The compressor is the heart of the refrigeration cycle. It pressurizes the refrigerant and keeps it moving. A failing compressor may run but produce little to no cooling, or it may not start at all (you will hear the outdoor fan spin but no compressor hum). Compressor failure is the most expensive AC repair, often running $1,200-2,500 or more depending on the unit. On a system that is 12-15 years old, a failed compressor is usually the point where replacement makes more financial sense than repair. See our post on [how much a new AC unit costs](/blog/how-much-does-a-new-ac-cost/) for a realistic budget breakdown.

**9. Undersized or mismatched equipment.** If your AC has never been able to keep up on days above 100 degrees, the system may simply be undersized for your home's heat load. This is especially common after a room addition, after attic insulation was removed and not replaced, or in homes where the original equipment was sized by a rule-of-thumb rather than an [ACCA Manual J](https://www.acca.org) load calculation. An undersized system will run continuously, never shut off, and still fall short of setpoint on the hottest days. The only fix is right-sized replacement equipment.

## What should I do right now if my AC is not cooling?

Work through the checklist in order. Check the thermostat setting first, then the filter, then the outdoor breaker. If all three are fine and the outdoor unit is running but not cooling, turn the system to FAN ONLY to keep air moving and call for a diagnostic appointment.

Do not keep cycling the system on and off trying to force it to cool. Running a compressor that is low on refrigerant or struggling against a seized capacitor can damage it further. One service call to diagnose is far cheaper than a compressor replacement.

If the outdoor unit is making a loud grinding or screeching sound, shut it off at the breaker and call immediately. That sound is usually a bearing failing in the condenser fan motor, and running it to failure can take out the motor winding.
