## Why Woody Properties See Leak Detection Issues

The agricultural character of the Woody area shapes its plumbing in ways that differ from a suburban tract. Many parcels here carry both domestic water service and separate irrigation lines, sometimes sharing trenches laid decades apart. Older galvanized and cast-iron supply lines that haven't been replaced are prone to pinhole corrosion, especially where the soil holds residual fertilizer salts that accelerate oxidation. When a line fails in that environment, the leak can travel several feet underground before it surfaces, or it never surfaces at all, just quietly saturating the subsoil and inflating your meter reading month after month.

Kern County's water infrastructure in rural communities like Woody can also mean longer runs from the meter to the structure, which increases the length of pipe exposed to ground movement. A small crack anywhere along that run is hard to locate by sight alone.

## Our Leak Detection Process in Woody

Electronic leak detection removes the guesswork that used to mean tearing up concrete or landscaping to find a break. The process starts at the meter. We isolate zones and use acoustic listening equipment to pick up the frequency signature of water escaping under pressure, a sound that travels through soil and pipe material in a pattern that lets a trained technician pinpoint the source within inches rather than feet.

For pressurized supply lines, we can also introduce a safe tracer gas that migrates to the surface at the leak point, detectable with a calibrated sensor even under a slab or a paved driveway. On irrigation systems, pressure decay testing identifies which zone or lateral is losing volume without requiring excavation across the whole yard.

Because many Woody properties have dual-use water systems, we map both the domestic and irrigation feeds before we begin so we're testing the right circuit. Skipping that step on a mixed-use parcel is how crews end up chasing the wrong line.

## Equipment and Methods We Use for Leak Detection

The right tool depends on what's buried and how it's buried. Acoustic correlators work well on metal pipe because sound travels efficiently through the material; on older PVC or polyethylene, we shift to tracer gas or thermal imaging depending on conditions. Ground-penetrating radar is useful when we need to map the pipe path before testing, particularly on parcels where original plumbing records don't exist or don't match what's actually in the ground.

All of these methods share one goal: a confirmed location before any soil is disturbed. That matters especially on agricultural parcels where irrigation infrastructure, electrical conduit, and drainage tile can all share the same trench corridor.
