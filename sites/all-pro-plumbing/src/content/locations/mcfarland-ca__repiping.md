McFarland sits in the southern San Joaquin Valley where summer heat regularly pushes past 100°F and the region's agricultural water history has left many residential supply lines carrying mineral-heavy water for decades. That combination, heat cycling through older pipe materials and hard water depositing scale from the inside out, accelerates the deterioration of galvanized steel and polybutylene lines faster than homeowners typically expect. If your water pressure has been dropping gradually or you are seeing rust-colored water at the tap, those are not random plumbing quirks. They are the pipe telling you it is running out of time.

## Why McFarland Properties See Repiping Issues

McFarland's housing stock includes a significant share of homes built during the agricultural boom decades of the mid-20th century. Many of those homes were originally plumbed with galvanized steel, which has a functional lifespan that, under normal conditions, runs roughly 40 to 70 years. Under the San Joaquin Valley's hard water conditions, that window shortens. Calcium and magnesium deposits build up on the interior walls of galvanized pipe, narrowing the effective diameter over time until water pressure at the shower head or kitchen faucet tells the story clearly.

Polybutylene pipe is a separate concern. Installed widely through the 1980s and into the early 1990s, it reacts poorly to chlorine compounds used in municipal water treatment. The pipe degrades from the inside, and failures often happen without visible warning. If your home was built or substantially renovated during that era, it is worth knowing what material is running through your walls before a failure makes the decision for you.

The valley's temperature swings also matter. Pipes in unconditioned crawl spaces and attic runs expand and contract through the seasons. Over years, those micro-movements stress joints and fittings, particularly in older copper lines where soldered connections may have begun to weep.

## Our Whole-House Repiping Process in McFarland

A whole-house repipe is a multi-day project, and the sequence matters as much as the materials. The first step is a full assessment of what is currently in the walls, under the slab if applicable, and running through the attic. In McFarland's older ranch-style homes, supply lines often run through interior walls that were not insulated, which actually makes access more straightforward than in newer construction with dense-pack insulation.

Once the scope is mapped, the crew works systematically through the home, isolating and replacing sections while keeping at least partial water service available where possible. For most single-family homes in this area, the work runs two to three days depending on square footage and access conditions. PEX is the material most commonly chosen for replacements today because it handles hard water better than galvanized, resists the kind of pinhole corrosion that plagues older copper in high-mineral-content water, and flexes rather than cracks under temperature stress. Copper remains a solid option for homeowners who prefer it or whose local resale market values it.

All work is performed under the appropriate Kern County building permits. Inspections are coordinated through the county process, and the permit record stays with the property, which matters when you sell.
