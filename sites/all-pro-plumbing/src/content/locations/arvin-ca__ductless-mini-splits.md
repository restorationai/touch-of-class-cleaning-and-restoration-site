Arvin sits at the southern end of the San Joaquin Valley, where summer temperatures regularly push past 105°F and the surrounding agricultural fields offer little shade or wind relief. A central HVAC system that was sized for a milder climate often can't keep up with that kind of heat load, and older homes in town frequently lack the ductwork to support one at all. Ductless mini-split systems solve both problems: precise, room-by-room cooling and heating without tearing open walls or ceilings, installed by the team at All Pro Plumbing Heating and Air.

## Why Arvin Properties Are a Strong Fit for Mini-Splits

Much of Arvin's housing stock was built quickly and economically to house agricultural workers and their families, which means a lot of homes have minimal insulation, slab-on-grade foundations, and no existing duct infrastructure. Retrofitting traditional forced-air into a slab home means either running ducts through the attic in a space that can exceed 150°F in July, or cutting into finished walls. Neither option is cheap or efficient.

Mini-splits sidestep that entirely. The refrigerant line set runs through a small penetration in an exterior wall, the indoor air handler mounts high on the wall where heat stratifies, and the outdoor condenser sits on a pad beside the house. For detached garages, converted outbuildings, and the kind of add-on rooms common in Arvin's older neighborhoods, a single-zone unit is often the most cost-effective solution available. Multi-zone systems let a single outdoor unit serve two, three, or four indoor heads, which is useful when a home has grown room by room over the decades without a unified mechanical plan.

The valley's air quality is also a real factor here. Arvin and the surrounding Kern County communities regularly see some of the worst particulate readings in California. Many mini-split systems include multi-stage filtration that captures fine dust and agricultural particulates before they circulate through living spaces, which is a meaningful benefit in a community this close to active farmland.

## Our Mini-Split Installation Process in Arvin

Every installation starts with a load calculation specific to the room or rooms being served. Arvin's climate data shows extreme cooling degree days, so undersizing a unit is a real mistake that shows up on the first hot week of June. We select equipment rated for the actual heat load, not just the square footage.

From there, the process runs in a predictable sequence: mounting the indoor air handler, running the refrigerant line set and condensate drain through the wall penetration, setting the outdoor condenser on a level pad or wall bracket, pulling a vacuum on the refrigerant circuit, and commissioning the system. Most single-zone installs are complete in a day. Multi-zone systems with three or four heads typically take two days depending on the layout of the home.

## Equipment and Methods We Use

We install inverter-driven mini-split systems, which modulate compressor speed rather than cycling on and off. In a climate like Arvin's, where a unit may run continuously for eight or ten hours on a peak summer day, inverter technology reduces wear and holds the set temperature more consistently than single-stage equipment. Heat pump models also provide efficient heating through the valley's cooler winters without requiring a separate system.

For garages and workshops, we pay close attention to the placement of the outdoor condenser. Arvin's summer afternoons bring direct western sun, and a condenser sitting in full afternoon exposure works harder and runs less efficiently. Where the site allows, we orient the unit to take advantage of shade from the structure itself or adjacent landscaping.

## Local Note

Homes in Arvin built during the mid-twentieth century agricultural expansion often have exterior walls framed with non-standard stud spacing, and some were constructed with adobe or concrete block rather than wood frame. Both conditions affect how the refrigerant line set penetration is made and sealed. Adobe and block walls require a core drill rather than a standard hole saw, and the penetration needs to be sealed against both air infiltration and the fine dust that moves through the valley during wind events. It is a small detail, but getting it wrong means the unit works against itself pulling unconditioned air from outside. Our crews account for wall construction before the first hole is cut.
