## Why Lamont Homes See Furnace Problems When They Do

The Central Valley climate creates a specific stress pattern for heating equipment. Summers are long and brutal, which means furnaces sit completely idle for six or seven months. When cooler weather finally arrives, a system that has been dormant since March is asked to run hard almost immediately. Dust and debris accumulate in burner assemblies and heat exchangers during that long off-season. Ignitors that were borderline in spring are fully degraded by fall. Blower motors that ran fine in March may have developed bearing wear over the summer.

Lamont's agricultural surroundings add another layer. Fine particulate matter from nearby fields works its way into return-air grilles, loading filters faster than manufacturers' replacement schedules assume. A clogged filter restricts airflow enough to trip a high-limit switch, which shuts the furnace down as a safety measure. To a homeowner, the symptom looks like a dead furnace. The fix may be straightforward, but only after a proper diagnosis rules out the more serious possibilities.

Older housing stock in the area, much of it built for farmworker families in the mid-twentieth century, often has ductwork that was sized for smaller, less efficient units. When a furnace is replaced without addressing duct sizing, static pressure climbs and the new unit runs shorter cycles, wears components faster, and never quite heats the house evenly.

One pattern that comes up repeatedly in this part of Kern County: furnaces in homes originally built without central heat, where a gas system was added later, sometimes have flue venting that was routed through exterior walls rather than through a proper chase.
