## Why Bear Valley Springs Properties See Slab Leak Issues

The geology beneath Bear Valley Springs is a mix of decomposed granite and expansive clay-rich soils that behave very differently depending on the season. When winter rains saturate the ground, clay layers swell and push upward against slab edges. When summer arrives and the soil dries and contracts, that same slab settles unevenly. Copper supply lines and older galvanized drain lines embedded in the concrete are not designed to flex with that kind of seasonal movement. Over years, the pipe wall thins at the stress points, and a pinhole becomes a slow leak that can go undetected until it has softened the subgrade beneath the slab itself.

Homes in Bear Valley Springs were largely built during the community's development as a private residential community, and many of them share a similar construction profile: post-tension or conventionally reinforced slabs poured over soil that was not always compacted to the standards used in newer valley-floor subdivisions. That combination of construction era and soil behavior puts the under-slab plumbing at higher-than-average risk compared to newer tract homes on stable valley floor soil.

## Our Slab Leak Detection and Repair Process

Finding a slab leak without tearing up flooring requires listening equipment and pressure testing, not guesswork. The process starts with electronic leak detection, using acoustic amplification to isolate the sound of water escaping under pressure through the slab. Once the leak is localized to within a few inches, the repair decision depends on what is found: the pipe's age, material, and whether the leak is isolated or part of a broader corrosion pattern.

For a single-point failure on an otherwise sound copper line, a direct access repair through the slab is often the most efficient path. For older galvanized or copper systems showing multiple stress points, a full reroute above the slab through interior walls or the attic space eliminates the risk of a second leak appearing months after the first repair. In Bear Valley Springs, where the soil movement that caused the first failure is not going away, a reroute is frequently the longer-term answer.
