## Why Keene Properties See Furnace Problems

Keene's position at elevation in the Tehachapi Mountains means it experiences a more dramatic swing between daytime and nighttime temperatures than the valley floor does. Furnaces here cycle harder and more frequently during the cold months, and that accelerated wear shows up in specific ways: cracked heat exchangers, failing ignitors, and pressure switches that trip repeatedly because the unit is working against cold return air from poorly insulated crawl spaces.

Many homes in the Keene area were built as railroad-era worker housing or as modest rural structures that predate modern HVAC standards. Ductwork in these older homes is often undersized, poorly sealed, or routed through uninsulated spaces that bleed heat before it reaches the living area. A furnace that seems to be "blowing cold air" in a Keene home is sometimes a duct problem masquerading as a furnace problem, and diagnosing the difference matters before any parts are ordered.

## Local Note

One thing worth knowing about furnaces in the Keene area: the canyon wind patterns that make the Tehachapi Pass a prime spot for wind energy also create negative pressure events inside older homes. When strong winds run across a roof or around a structure, they can briefly reverse the draft in a flue, tripping a furnace's pressure switch or draft inducer fault code. Homeowners sometimes call this a "furnace that keeps shutting off for no reason." It is not a random fault, it is the unit protecting itself from a combustion hazard. A technician who does not know the local wind behavior might replace the pressure switch unnecessarily. We check flue draft and combustion air conditions before condemning any component.
