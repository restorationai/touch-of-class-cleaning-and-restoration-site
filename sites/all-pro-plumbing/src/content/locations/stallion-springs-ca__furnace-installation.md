## Why Stallion Springs Homes Make Furnace Sizing a Real Conversation

The housing stock in Stallion Springs skews toward single-family homes built for recreational and retirement use, many of them constructed with minimal insulation standards that were common in mountain communities before energy codes tightened. Vaulted ceilings, exposed-beam construction, and large window walls are common design choices that look great but bleed heat fast. A proper load calculation, sometimes called a Manual J, is not optional here. Skipping it and defaulting to "same size as the old unit" almost always results in a furnace that short-cycles in mild weather and falls short on the coldest nights in January and February.

Gas service in this area runs through Southern California Gas, and line pressure and meter capacity are worth confirming before committing to a high-BTU unit. If a home has been vacant or lightly used, the meter may be undersized for a modern high-efficiency furnace.

## High-Efficiency Options and What They Mean at This Elevation

High-efficiency condensing furnaces, typically rated at 96 AFUE or above, are an excellent fit for Stallion Springs because the heating season is longer and colder than the valley. The payback period on the efficiency premium is shorter when a furnace is running hard from October through April. One consideration specific to mountain installations: condensing furnaces produce acidic condensate that must drain properly, and in a home that sees freezing temperatures in crawl spaces or garages, the condensate line needs to be routed and insulated to prevent freeze-ups that can lock the unit out.

Two-stage and variable-capacity furnaces also perform noticeably better in mountain climates because they can run at a lower stage during moderate cold and ramp up only when temperatures drop sharply overnight, which reduces temperature swings and keeps the system quieter.
