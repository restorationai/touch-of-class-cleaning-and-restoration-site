You came home to a wet carpet squishing underfoot, or you walked downstairs and found four inches of standing water. Maybe a sump pump failed during a summer storm, or a supply line finally gave out behind the water heater. Either way, the clock is already running: mold can begin colonizing wet drywall and wood within 24 to 48 hours. The first thing you need to know is what kind of water you're dealing with, because that determines everything else.

## What kind of water is in your basement?

The water category determines the cleanup method, the health risk, and what your insurance will pay. The [IICRC S500 standard](https://www.iicrc.org/page/IICRCS500) defines three categories:

**Category 1 (clean water)** comes from a broken supply line, a failed water heater, or a leaking appliance inlet hose. It poses no immediate health risk. You can safely wade in with rubber boots, and porous materials like carpet may be salvageable if dried within 24 to 48 hours.

**Category 2 (gray water)** includes washing machine discharge, dishwasher overflow, or a sump pit backup. It contains biological or chemical contaminants and requires protective gloves and a respirator during cleanup. Carpet padding is almost always a loss.

**Category 3 (black water)** is sewage backup, rising groundwater, or any water that has been standing long enough to become contaminated. This is the most dangerous category. It contains pathogens, bacteria, and potentially raw sewage. Porous materials (drywall, carpet, insulation, wood framing at the waterline) must be removed and disposed of, not dried in place. This is not a DIY job.

If you smell sewage, see discoloration, or the water came up through a floor drain, assume Category 3 until a pro tests otherwise. ## Will homeowners insurance cover a flooded basement?

It depends on the cause, and this is where most homeowners get surprised. Standard homeowners policies cover sudden, accidental water damage from inside the home. They do NOT cover flooding from outside the home.

Here is how the typical coverage split works:

- **Burst pipe or appliance failure (Cat 1):** Usually covered under the dwelling and personal property portions of a standard HO-3 policy, subject to your deductible.
- **Sewer or drain backup:** Usually NOT covered under a standard policy. Many insurers offer a sewer backup rider for $50 to $150 per year. If you don't have it, you're paying out of pocket.
- **Groundwater or surface flooding:** Not covered by homeowners insurance. This requires a separate [National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP)](https://www.fema.gov/flood-insurance) policy through FEMA or a private flood insurer. Standard NFIP policies have a 30-day waiting period, so you cannot buy one after the water is already in your basement.
- **Slow leak or long-term seepage:** Almost always denied. Insurers call this a maintenance issue.

For a deeper look at what policies cover and how to document a claim, see our guide: [Does Homeowners Insurance Cover Water Damage?](/blog/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-water-damage/)

The documentation you create in the first hour matters for your claim. Photograph everything before moving anything. Note the time you discovered the water. Save the failed component if it's safe to do so (a cracked supply line hose, for example).

## How long does flooded basement cleanup take?

Most residential basement water damage dries in 3 to 5 days with commercial drying equipment. That timeline assumes extraction starts quickly and the structure is sound. Here is what each phase looks like:

**Day 1: Extraction and assessment.** A crew extracts standing water with a truck-mounted or portable extractor. A technician takes moisture readings with a calibrated meter at the walls, floor, and subfloor to map the wet zone. If the basement is finished, some drywall may be removed at the waterline (called flood cuts) to allow air circulation behind the wall cavity.

**Days 1-3: Drying setup.** Commercial LGR (low-grain refrigerant) dehumidifiers and high-velocity air movers are placed based on the drying plan. The [EPA recommends](https://www.epa.gov/mold/what-do-after-flood) keeping indoor humidity below 60 percent during drying to prevent mold growth. Commercial dehumidifiers pull far more moisture per day than a consumer unit from a hardware store.

**Days 2-5: Monitoring.** A technician returns each day to log moisture readings and adjust equipment placement. Drying is complete when readings return to normal dry standard (typically below 16 percent moisture content in wood, per IICRC S500 guidelines). Equipment is removed only when the structure is confirmed dry, not when it feels dry to the touch.

**After drying: Reconstruction.** If drywall, flooring, or insulation was removed, reconstruction follows. This is a separate phase from mitigation and is often handled under a different insurance line item.

Delaying extraction by even 24 hours significantly increases both cost and drying time. Wet drywall that could have been dried in place often requires demolition if mold colonization begins. ## When should you call a professional instead of handling it yourself?

Call a professional restoration company immediately if any of the following apply:

- There is standing water more than an inch deep
- The water came from a drain, toilet, or sewer line (Category 3)
- The basement is finished with drywall, carpet, or drop ceilings
- The water has been sitting for more than 24 hours
- You can smell a musty or sewage odor
- You are not certain the electrical circuits in the basement are off

For minor clean-water incidents with less than an inch of water in an unfinished basement, a wet/dry shop vac, fans, and a consumer dehumidifier may be sufficient if you start within a few hours. But consumer equipment dries slowly, and slow drying creates mold risk. If you have any doubt, get a professional moisture assessment first. Many restoration companies, including DISS Restoration, will assess the situation and give you an honest read on whether you need full mitigation or can manage it yourself.

For Category 3 sewage backup, there is no safe DIY option. The pathogens present require professional-grade disinfectants, proper PPE, and disposal protocols that are not available to the general public.

## What to do right now if your basement is flooded

If you have standing water in your basement at this moment, here is the immediate sequence:

1. **Cut power at the breaker panel** before entering the basement. Water and live electrical circuits are a fatal combination.
2. **Identify the water source** and stop it if you safely can. A burst supply line has a shutoff valve; a sewer backup does not.
3. **Do not run HVAC or a forced-air furnace** through a flooded basement. It spreads contaminated air and moisture through the duct system.
4. **Document everything** with photos and video before moving anything.
5. **Call a restoration company** for extraction and assessment. The sooner extraction starts, the lower the final cost.
