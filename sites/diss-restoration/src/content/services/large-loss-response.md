When a loss stops being a single room or a single unit and becomes a whole floor, a whole building, or a whole portfolio, the math changes. A burst main in a mixed-use building, a fire that moves through a shared attic in a multifamily complex, or a roof failure that floods three floors of a commercial property all create the same problem: standard residential mitigation crews and equipment counts are not built for it. Large loss and catastrophic response is about scale, speed, and documentation that holds up when a claim runs into six or seven figures.

## What large loss and catastrophic response actually involves

A large loss is typically defined less by the type of damage and more by its size and complexity: multiple affected units or floors, structural involvement, business interruption exposure, or a claim value that pushes past what a single mitigation crew can reasonably handle in a normal timeline. Think a burst domestic water line in a multi-story apartment building, a fire in a commercial kitchen that spreads through a shared HVAC plenum, or wind and hail damage across an entire industrial roof.

The work itself scales up every variable: dozens of air movers and desiccant dehumidifiers instead of a handful, generator-powered equipment when building power is compromised, multiple extraction crews working different zones simultaneously, and a project manager whose job is coordinating trades, engineers, and the adjuster rather than running a wand. Documentation moves from a single moisture log to zone-by-zone psychrometric tracking across an entire structure, because a large loss claim lives or dies on whether the paper trail matches the physical work.

## Our process

1. **Rapid mobilization and scope triage.** Crews, equipment trailers, and generators are staged based on an initial walkthrough that breaks the loss into zones, identifies life-safety hazards, and flags structural members that need an engineer before anyone starts demo.
2. **Emergency mitigation and stabilization.** Board-up, temporary power, roof tarping, and large-capacity water extraction happen first to stop the loss from spreading while the rest of the response is planned.
3. **Zone-based moisture mapping and documentation.** Every affected area gets its own moisture log, photo and video record, and drying equipment count, so the adjuster can see exactly what was wet, how wet, and what it took to dry each zone, dried per the IICRC S500 standard.
4. **Phased reconstruction and trade coordination.** Framing, electrical, mechanical, and finish trades are sequenced around permits and around any portions of the building still in active use, so operations aren't shut down longer than necessary.
5. **Claim reconciliation and close-out.** Final documentation packages, cost reconciliation, and scope sign-off are assembled for the adjuster and ownership before the project closes.

## What separates a good large loss response from a bad one

The most common failure on a large loss is under-resourcing it: a contractor treats a five-story building like a slightly bigger house and shows up with residential-scale equipment. The drying timeline stretches, secondary damage (mold, delamination, warping) sets in on areas that should have been dry in days, and the claim value climbs because the mitigation phase ran too long.

The second common failure is documentation that doesn't scale with the loss. Large loss adjusters are trained to look for zone-specific moisture readings, equipment placement logs, and daily progress reports, not a single summary at the end. A scope that can't show what happened in each affected area, on each day, gets challenged, and challenges delay payment.

The third is poor coordination between mitigation, structural engineers, and general contractors. On a catastrophic loss, structural stabilization, drying, and reconstruction often need to happen in parallel in different parts of the same building. Good project management means a single point of contact tracking all of it, not three separate vendors working off three separate schedules.

## What does large loss and catastrophic response cost?

Large loss costs vary enormously depending on square footage, number of affected units, structural involvement, and how quickly mitigation starts after the loss. The ranges below reflect typical industry figures for commercial and multifamily catastrophic losses, not a quote: every loss gets its own written scope before DISS Restoration starts work.

| Scenario | Typical range |
|---|---|
| Single-floor commercial water loss, ~5,000 sq ft | $25,000 - $75,000 |
| Multi-floor combined fire and water loss | $150,000 - $500,000 |
| Large apartment complex, multiple units affected | $300,000 - $1,200,000 |
| Catastrophic structural loss (major collapse, fire spread) | $1,000,000+ |
| Emergency mitigation and mobilization only (first 72 hours) | $15,000 - $60,000 |

Commercial property policies generally cover sudden and accidental large losses, including water, fire, and wind damage, along with business interruption coverage where applicable; the specific limits and sublimits depend on the policy and should be confirmed with the carrier early in the claim.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Youngstown's winter freeze-thaw cycles are a recurring trigger for large commercial losses: older cast iron and galvanized supply lines in multi-story buildings in the Mahoning Valley are prone to splitting during hard freezes, and a failure on an upper floor can cascade water damage through several levels before it's shut off. Spring storm season brings wind and hail exposure for flat and low-slope commercial roofs common in the area's older industrial and mixed-use buildings, and heavy snow load is an occasional factor in roof-related structural losses.
