Smoke behaves differently than the fire itself. Even after flames are out and the structure is deemed safe to re-enter, soot particles keep working, settling into carpet fibers, HVAC ductwork, cabinetry, and wall cavities long after the smoke itself has cleared. A grease fire in a kitchen leaves a different residue than a smoldering electrical fire in a wall void, and wildfire smoke drifting into a home through open windows or a running HVAC system leaves yet another kind of film. Treating all smoke damage the same way is one of the most common mistakes in this industry.

## What smoke damage restoration actually involves

Smoke damage restoration starts with identifying what kind of soot is on the surfaces, because that determines the cleaning agents and tools used. Dry smoke from a fast, hot fire (often wood or paper) leaves a powdery residue that's easier to wipe but tends to migrate into every crevice. Wet smoke from a smoldering, low-oxygen fire (common with foam, plastics, and rubber) leaves a sticky, smeary film that bonds to surfaces and smears if handled with the wrong technique. Protein residue from a kitchen fire is nearly invisible but carries an intense odor that soaks into cabinetry and finishes.

Equipment used depends on the surface and residue type: dry sponges for soot on porous materials like drywall and unpainted wood, solvent-based or alkaline cleaners for greasy film on hard surfaces, HEPA vacuums to lift particulate without grinding it deeper into fibers, and thermal foggers or hydroxyl generators to neutralize odor at a molecular level rather than mask it. Timelines vary with the scope: a single room with surface soot might clear in a day or two, while a structure fire with smoke traveling through HVAC ducts and wall cavities can take one to two weeks between cleaning, odor treatment, and verification.

## Our process

1. **Soot characterization.** We identify whether residue is dry, wet, or protein-based, since this dictates cleaning method and chemical selection before any work begins.
2. **Pre-cleaning and dry sponge pass.** Loose particulate is removed from walls, ceilings, and contents before wet cleaning, which prevents smearing residue deeper into porous materials.
3. **Wet cleaning and surface treatment.** Affected surfaces, trim, and hard contents are cleaned with agents matched to the soot type, then sealed or primed where staining has penetrated finishes.
4. **Thermal fogging or ozone/hydroxyl treatment.** Odor-causing particles that have bonded to surfaces and settled into soft materials are neutralized rather than covered with fragrance.
5. **HVAC and duct inspection.** Smoke traveling through return air ducts can redistribute odor and soot throughout a structure for months if ductwork isn't cleaned or sealed as part of the scope.

## What separates a good smoke damage response from a bad one

The most common mistake is treating smoke odor with air fresheners or ozone alone without first removing the residue that's causing it. Odor won't fully resolve until the particulate source is cleaned, because ozone and hydroxyl treatments work on airborne and surface-level molecules, not on soot sitting inside carpet pad or behind baseboards.

Another frequent miss is skipping metal surfaces. Acidic soot residue corrodes hinges, electrical contacts, and appliance components within days if untreated, which is why a thorough response includes neutralizing metal fixtures even when they look unaffected. Insurance adjusters also look closely at whether HVAC systems were inspected and cleaned, since smoke circulated through ductwork is a common source of recurring odor complaints after a claim has already closed. A written scope that documents soot type, affected materials, and odor source locations before work starts gives both the homeowner and the carrier a clear record of what was found and what was done.
