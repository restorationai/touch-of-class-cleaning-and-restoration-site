When a water or fire loss is finally dry and demolished, you're left staring at open studs, missing drywall, and a punch list that insurance paperwork alone won't fix. Reconstruction is the bridge between "the damage is stopped" and "the house is whole again," and it's where a lot of restoration jobs stall out, either because the drying crew wasn't licensed to rebuild or because the rebuild crew never saw the original moisture or char documentation. DISS Restoration carries both halves of the job under one scope, so the framing, electrical rough-in, and finish work match what the demo and drying teams actually found behind the walls.

## What reconstruction services actually involves

Post-damage reconstruction covers everything from replacing a single section of drywall and baseboard after a small pipe leak to full structural rebuilds after a fire burned through joists and subfloor. The scope typically includes framing repair or replacement, subfloor and sheathing, insulation, drywall and taping, interior trim, flooring, cabinetry, and paint, plus any electrical, plumbing, or HVAC rough-in that has to be reopened and re-inspected before walls close back up. Timelines vary enormously: a single-room drywall and trim repair might run one to two weeks, while a fire damage reconstruction involving load-bearing framing, permits, and multiple trades can run several months. The work is governed by local building code, not just cosmetic matching, which is why permitting and inspection sign-off are part of the job, not an afterthought.

## Our process

1. **Scope of work and documentation review.** We walk the structure against the mitigation and demo records (moisture maps, soot or char photos, adjuster notes) so the rebuild scope reflects what was actually damaged, not just what's visible now.
2. **Permits and code review.** Structural, electrical, and plumbing changes typically require permits through the local building department. We pull what's required before framing starts so inspections don't hold up the schedule later.
3. **Framing and rough-in.** Damaged studs, joists, or subfloor are replaced to match existing structural members, and any electrical, plumbing, or HVAC lines disturbed during demo are rerun and inspected before insulation goes back in.
4. **Insulation, drywall, and finish carpentry.** Walls close up, taping and texture are matched to the surrounding surfaces, and trim, doors, and cabinetry are reinstalled or replaced to the agreed scope.
5. **Final finishes and walkthrough.** Flooring, paint, and fixtures go in last, followed by a walkthrough against the written scope so nothing gets missed before the final invoice.

## What separates a good reconstruction response from a bad one

The most common failure point is a rebuild that starts before the structure is actually dry or before hidden damage has been fully mapped, which traps moisture behind new drywall and sets up a second mold problem under warranty. A close second is mismatched materials: trim profiles, flooring transitions, or texture patterns that don't match the rest of the house because nobody measured against the original before demo. Adjusters pay close attention to whether the reconstruction scope lines up with the approved mitigation scope, whether permits were actually pulled for structural and electrical work, and whether the contractor documented pre-existing conditions versus damage-caused repairs with photos. A reconstruction crew that was present for or has access to the original drying and demo records can tell the difference between a wall that needs new insulation and one that just needs paint, which keeps the rebuild scope honest instead of padded.

## What does reconstruction services cost?

Reconstruction costs scale with square footage, the number of trades involved, and whether structural or only cosmetic repairs are needed. ## Seasonal & regional considerations

Youngstown's older housing stock, much of it framed in the early-to-mid 1900s, means reconstruction crews frequently encounter balloon framing, plaster-over-lath walls, and knob-and-tube remnants that change how a rebuild scope is written compared to newer construction. Winter rebuilds also have to account for the region's freeze-thaw cycle: open framing left exposed during a cold snap can see pipes freeze again or moisture reintroduced through snowmelt, so timing and temporary enclosure matter as much as the finish schedule.
