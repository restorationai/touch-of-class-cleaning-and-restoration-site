## Why Niles Properties See Sewage Backup Issues

The housing stock across much of Niles skews older — a significant portion of residential properties were built before the 1960s, when clay tile and cast-iron sewer laterals were standard. Those materials have a finite service life. Clay tile joints shift as soil settles, and cast iron corrodes from the inside out. The result is a lateral that looks intact from the surface but has already developed cracks or root intrusion points underground.

Trumbull County's clay-dense soil compounds the problem. Clay expands when saturated and contracts in dry periods, putting lateral pipes through a slow mechanical stress cycle year after year. During heavy spring rain events — the kind that overwhelm combined sewer systems — municipal lines can surcharge, pushing sewage back up through the lowest floor drain in a home. Basements in Niles take the hit first, and finished lower levels can sustain serious damage before a homeowner realizes what's happening.

Septic systems on properties at the edges of the city's service area face a different version of the same issue: saturated drain fields in late winter and early spring can't absorb effluent fast enough, leading to surfacing sewage or backflow into the structure.

## Our Sewage Cleanup and Sanitization Process in Niles

Raw sewage is classified as Category 3 water — the highest contamination level — and the process reflects that. Every sewage job begins with containment: the affected area is isolated to prevent cross-contamination with clean spaces, and the crew suits up in appropriate PPE before any material is touched.

Extraction comes next. Standing sewage and saturated materials are removed using truck-mounted extraction equipment. Porous materials that have absorbed Category 3 contamination — drywall, carpet, insulation, wood subfloor — are typically removed rather than dried in place, because drying alone does not eliminate the biological hazard embedded in the material.

Once the gross contamination is out, surfaces are treated with an EPA-registered disinfectant applied to all affected structural components: concrete, framing, joists, and any remaining building materials. The crew then sets drying equipment — air movers and dehumidifiers calibrated to the space — and monitors moisture readings until the structure reaches its drying goal. In Niles homes with older plaster walls or thick subfloor assemblies, that monitoring phase often runs longer than it would in newer construction because dense materials release moisture more slowly than modern drywall or OSB.

Final clearance is documented with moisture readings and a written scope, which matters when you're filing an insurance claim.
