## Why Canfield Properties Are Vulnerable After Storm and Fire Events

Mahoning County's climate is a genuine stress test for residential roofing. The region sits in the snowbelt corridor that funnels moisture from Lake Erie inland, meaning Canfield can absorb significant accumulation while areas to the south and west stay dry. That weight — combined with freeze-thaw cycling through late winter and early spring — accelerates wear on older shingle systems and flashing seals. When a storm finally breaks through, the opening is rarely small: missing shingles often come off in sections, and a gap that looks manageable from the ground can admit gallons of water per hour during a sustained rain event.

Canfield's housing stock adds another layer of complexity. The township and borough include a substantial number of homes built between the 1950s and 1980s, many with dimensional lumber roof framing rather than engineered trusses. That framing holds up well under normal loads, but once moisture gets into the rafter bays, it moves laterally through the wood faster than it would through modern OSB sheathing — meaning a modest roof breach can wet a much larger area than the visible damage suggests. Getting a tarp over the opening quickly is not just about keeping rain out; it is about limiting how far the saturation front travels before drying can begin.
