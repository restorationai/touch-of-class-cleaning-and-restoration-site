## Why Grove City Properties See Mold Issues

Mercer County's climate delivers cold, wet winters followed by warm, humid summers. That seasonal swing is hard on building envelopes, particularly in Grove City's older housing stock where many homes predate modern vapor barriers and insulated rim joists. Fieldstone and poured-concrete block foundations, common in homes built before the 1960s, are porous by nature. Ground moisture migrates through them steadily, and without a working sump or proper grading, that moisture accumulates behind finished walls and under subfloors before anyone notices the musty smell.

Crawl spaces are a recurring problem in this part of western Pennsylvania. Vented crawl spaces, once considered best practice, can actually draw in humid outdoor air during summer, condense it against cooler structural wood, and feed mold colonies on floor joists and sill plates for months without a visible sign inside the living space. By the time a homeowner notices soft flooring or a persistent earthy odor, the colony may already span a significant area of the substructure.

## Our Mold Remediation Process in Grove City

Every job starts with a thorough assessment before any material is disturbed. A technician inspects the visible growth, probes moisture levels in surrounding building materials, and identifies the moisture source driving the problem. Skipping that last step is the most common reason mold returns after a cleanup.

Once the scope is clear, the affected area is isolated using poly sheeting and negative air pressure so that spores disturbed during removal do not migrate to clean areas of the home. Porous materials that cannot be brought to an acceptable spore count through cleaning, such as heavily colonized drywall or insulation, are removed and bagged for disposal. Structural wood is treated and dried to documented moisture levels per the IICRC S520 standard.

After remediation, air scrubbers with HEPA filtration run until post-remediation air samples confirm the spore count has returned to an acceptable baseline. That clearance step matters: it gives homeowners and their insurance carriers documented evidence that the work is complete, not just visually clean.

## Local Note

In Mercer County's older borough-style homes, including many of the two-story frame houses that line Grove City's residential streets, plaster-and-lath wall construction is still common. Plaster does not absorb bulk water the way drywall does, but the wood lath behind it absolutely will, and it holds that moisture long after the surface feels dry to the touch. A moisture meter reading on the plaster face can look acceptable while the lath is still sitting at 25 percent or higher. Technicians working in these homes need to probe deeper and allow longer drying cycles before any encapsulation or reconstruction begins. Rushing that step in a plaster-wall home is how mold comes back six months later.
