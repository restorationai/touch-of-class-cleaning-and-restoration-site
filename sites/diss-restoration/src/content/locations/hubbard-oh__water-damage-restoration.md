## Why Hubbard Properties See Water Damage Issues

The Mahoning Valley's clay-heavy soils drain slowly. During heavy rain or rapid snowmelt, that saturation pushes hydrostatic pressure against basement walls and floor slabs in ways that catch homeowners off guard — especially in homes built before modern waterproofing standards were common. Hubbard's residential neighborhoods contain a significant share of mid-century construction: block foundations, original cast-iron or galvanized plumbing, and crawl spaces that were never fully conditioned. Galvanized supply lines corrode from the inside out, often showing no visible warning before they fail. Cast-iron drain lines develop cracks and root intrusion that back up into lower-level living areas. Add the region's pattern of hard winters followed by rapid spring warm-up, and the conditions for a water loss event are built into the landscape itself.

Flat or gently sloping lots common in this part of Trumbull County also mean surface water has fewer places to go. When downspouts discharge against a foundation rather than away from it, or when window wells fill faster than their drains can clear, basement water intrusion follows a predictable path — through mortar joints, around penetrations, and across the floor slab.

## Our Water Damage Restoration Process in Hubbard

Every water loss starts with a thorough inspection before any equipment is placed. Using thermal imaging cameras and calibrated moisture meters, the crew maps where water has actually traveled — not just where it's visible. In Hubbard's older homes, that often means finding moisture behind plaster and lath, inside block wall cavities, and beneath original hardwood or vinyl tile floors where the subfloor has absorbed standing water.

Once the scope is documented, extraction comes first. Truck-mounted and portable extractors pull standing water from the structure before drying equipment is staged. Then industrial-grade desiccant and refrigerant dehumidifiers, paired with high-velocity air movers, are positioned to dry the structure from the inside out — a process governed by the IICRC S500 standard for water damage restoration. Moisture readings are logged at every visit so drying progress is documented and verifiable, which matters both for structural integrity and for insurance claim support.

When materials cannot be dried in place — saturated insulation, compromised drywall, or flooring with trapped moisture beneath it — controlled demolition removes only what is necessary. The goal is to dry the structure, not to over-demo it.

## Local Note

One pattern worth knowing in Hubbard's older neighborhoods: homes built with plaster-and-lath interior walls hold moisture differently than modern drywall construction. A moisture meter reading at the surface can show acceptable levels while the lath behind the plaster is still saturated — sometimes for weeks. Drying these assemblies correctly requires extended drying times and more frequent monitoring intervals than a comparable drywall home. Rushing the process or relying on surface readings alone leads to mold growth inside the wall cavity that isn't visible until it's already a larger problem. The IICRC ASD (Applied Structural Drying) certification the team holds is specifically relevant here — it covers the science of drying non-standard assemblies, not just open-stud drywall construction.

If you are dealing with water in a Hubbard home with original plaster walls, plan for a longer drying cycle and ask the technician to show you the moisture logs at each visit so you can see the trend line, not just the final reading.
