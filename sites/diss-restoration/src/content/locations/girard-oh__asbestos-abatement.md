Girard's residential neighborhoods carry a quiet hazard that many homeowners don't think about until a renovation uncovers it: asbestos-containing materials woven into the bones of homes built during the mid-twentieth century industrial boom that shaped this corner of Trumbull County. The same era that made Girard a working-class hub also filled its housing stock with floor tiles, pipe insulation, joint compound, and textured ceilings that may still contain asbestos fibers today. Disturbing those materials without proper containment puts occupants at risk — and creates a liability that no coat of paint can fix.

## Why Girard Properties See Asbestos Risks

Girard sits in a part of northeast Ohio where the residential construction timeline closely tracks the region's steel and manufacturing peak — roughly the 1930s through the early 1970s. Homes built during that window are the most likely to contain asbestos in multiple locations simultaneously: the floor tile adhesive in the basement, the wrap on the furnace flue, the texture on the living room ceiling, and the drywall compound in the bedrooms. It's rarely just one material.

The region's climate adds pressure. Freeze-thaw cycles through Trumbull County winters cause pipe insulation to crack and flake, and older boiler systems — common in Girard's two-story frame homes — are often wrapped in asbestos-containing insulation that degrades as the pipes expand and contract season after season. A cracked wrap in a utility room is no longer a contained hazard; it becomes an active one.

Popcorn ceilings are another common finding in Girard homes built between the late 1950s and early 1980s. Homeowners planning to scrape and repaint before listing a property often don't realize that texture applied before 1980 has a meaningful probability of containing chrysotile asbestos. Testing before any scraping is not optional — it's the step that determines whether a weekend DIY project becomes a regulated abatement job.

## Our Asbestos Abatement Process in Girard

Every project starts with a formal inspection and bulk sampling of suspect materials. Samples go to an accredited laboratory, and no abatement work begins until results are in hand. That sequence matters because Ohio EPA regulations govern how asbestos-containing materials must be handled, packaged, transported, and disposed of — and the process differs depending on whether the material is friable (easily crumbled) or non-friable.

For confirmed asbestos-containing materials, the work area is isolated with negative air pressure containment before any removal begins. HEPA-filtered air scrubbers run continuously to capture airborne fibers. Crew members work in full personal protective equipment, and all removed material is wet-wrapped and sealed in labeled disposal bags that meet Ohio's hazardous waste transport requirements.

After removal, the contained area undergoes a visual clearance inspection followed by air sampling. Clearance air results must meet established fiber-count thresholds before containment is broken and the space is returned to normal use. The EPA Lead-Safe certification our team holds also covers lead paint co-occurring with asbestos in older Girard homes — a combination that's common in pre-1960 construction and requires a coordinated approach rather than two separate scopes of work.

## Local Note

One pattern that comes up repeatedly in Girard's older two-story homes: asbestos floor tile is often found layered beneath vinyl sheet goods that were installed as a "renovation" in the 1980s or 1990s. The original 9×9 floor tiles — a near-certain indicator of asbestos-containing material in homes built before 1980 — were simply covered rather than removed. Homeowners discover them when pulling up the later flooring during a kitchen remodel or basement finishing project. If you lift a corner of sheet vinyl and see small square tiles underneath, stop work and call for testing before going further. Cutting or breaking those tiles releases fibers that are otherwise stable when left undisturbed.
