## Why Wheatland Properties See Water Damage Issues

The Shenango River corridor creates a moisture environment that most inland Pennsylvania towns don't deal with at the same intensity. Spring snowmelt combines with rain to push groundwater tables up quickly, and basements in Wheatland's mid-century brick and block homes can go from dry to several inches of standing water in a matter of hours. Older clay-tile drain systems, common in homes built before the 1970s, are prone to root intrusion and collapse, which can send sewage-laced water back through floor drains just as fast as a burst supply line.

The housing stock itself adds another layer of complexity. Plaster-and-lath interior walls, which are standard in homes built before roughly 1960, absorb water more slowly than modern drywall but release it even more slowly. Surface readings on a moisture meter can show acceptable numbers while the lath behind the plaster stays saturated for days, creating exactly the conditions mold needs to establish itself. That hidden saturation is one reason water damage in Wheatland's older homes requires more monitoring time, not less, than a newer construction job.

## Our Water Damage Restoration Process in Wheatland

Every job starts with a thorough assessment using thermal imaging and calibrated moisture meters to map where water has traveled, not just where it's visible. In Wheatland's older homes, that often means tracing moisture through plaster walls, under hardwood subfloors, and into rim joist cavities that are common in pier-and-beam and block-wall basement construction.

Once the scope is mapped, standing water is removed with truck-mounted extraction equipment. Structural drying follows using industrial air movers and low-grain refrigerant dehumidifiers placed according to IICRC S500 drying science, not guesswork. Moisture readings are logged at each monitoring visit so the drying record is documented and defensible for your insurance carrier. Affected materials that cannot be dried in place, saturated insulation, buckled subfloor sections, compromised drywall, are removed and disposed of properly before the drying phase closes out.

## Local Note

Homes in Wheatland built during the steel-era construction boom of the 1940s and 1950s frequently have block-wall basements with parging, a thin mortar coating applied to the interior block face to slow moisture intrusion. When water damage occurs, that parging can trap moisture between the coating and the block, keeping the wall reading dry at the surface while the block itself stays wet. Crews familiar with this construction pattern know to check behind the parge coat before declaring a basement wall dry, a step that matters for preventing mold growth in the weeks after a loss.
