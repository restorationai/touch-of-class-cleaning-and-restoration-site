## Why Struthers Properties See Asbestos Issues

The housing stock in Struthers reflects the city's history as a mill and manufacturing community. Homes built from the 1920s through the late 1970s routinely incorporated asbestos in ways that aren't always visible: wrapped around heating pipes in basements, compressed into vinyl floor tiles beneath layers of newer flooring, mixed into the joint compound behind drywall, and sprayed onto ceilings in the textured "popcorn" finish that was popular through the mid-1980s.

Ohio's climate adds another layer of concern. Mahoning County winters are cold and wet, and decades of freeze-thaw cycling cause building materials to crack, shift, and deteriorate. Asbestos-containing insulation around older steam and hot-water heating systems — common in Struthers homes that predate forced-air HVAC — becomes friable over time, meaning it crumbles easily and releases fibers into the air. A crumbling pipe wrap in a basement that looks like ordinary deterioration can be a significant exposure hazard.

Struthers also sits within a county that follows Ohio EPA and Ohio Department of Health notification requirements for asbestos abatement projects above threshold quantities. Renovation or demolition work that disturbs regulated amounts of asbestos-containing material requires proper notification, licensed contractors, and disposal at an approved facility — not a standard dumpster.

## Our Asbestos Abatement Process in Struthers

Every project begins with a thorough inspection and bulk sampling of suspect materials. Samples are sent to an accredited laboratory for analysis before any removal work begins. That step matters: not every popcorn ceiling or old floor tile contains asbestos, and testing before demolition prevents unnecessary work and unnecessary exposure.

When asbestos-containing materials are confirmed, the work area is isolated with polyethylene sheeting and placed under negative air pressure using HEPA-filtered air scrubbers. This containment prevents fibers from migrating into adjacent rooms or HVAC systems. Workers wear full personal protective equipment, and all removed materials are wetted to suppress fiber release, then sealed in labeled disposal bags.

After removal, air clearance testing confirms that fiber levels have returned to acceptable levels before containment is removed and the space is released for renovation or occupancy. The entire process is documented — inspection reports, lab results, air clearance data, and disposal manifests — which matters for insurance claims, real estate transactions, and Ohio EPA compliance records.

One thing that comes up regularly in Struthers-area homes built during the steel era: multiple generations of flooring layered on top of each other. It's common to find modern vinyl plank over 1970s sheet vinyl over original 1940s or 1950s floor tiles — and that bottom layer of 9×9 or 12×12 floor tiles is a common asbestos-containing material.
