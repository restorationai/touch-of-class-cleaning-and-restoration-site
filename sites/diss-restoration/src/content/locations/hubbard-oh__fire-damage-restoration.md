Hubbard sits in Trumbull County's western edge, where decades of industrial-era construction left behind a housing stock that burns and smokes differently than newer builds. Older balloon-frame homes — common throughout this part of northeastern Ohio — allow fire to travel vertically inside wall cavities with little resistance, meaning a kitchen fire can deposit smoke residue in attic spaces before the flames ever reach the ceiling. When that happens, the cleanup is far more involved than it looks from the doorway, and getting it right matters for both the structure and the people living in it.

## Why Hubbard Properties Are Vulnerable to Widespread Fire Damage

The residential neighborhoods throughout Hubbard include a significant number of homes built between the 1920s and 1960s, a period when balloon framing was standard practice and insulation was minimal or absent. In a fire event, that open stud cavity acts like a chimney — heat, smoke, and soot migrate upward and laterally into spaces that look untouched. Synthetic materials common in mid-century renovations (vinyl flooring, older insulation, lead-based paint) also produce a heavier, oilier soot when they combust, which bonds more aggressively to drywall, wood trim, and HVAC ductwork than the dry soot from a purely wood fire.

Hubbard's winters add another layer of complexity. Bitter cold during fire suppression means water from hoses freezes quickly on exterior surfaces and can saturate wall cavities before it drains — so a fire loss in January or February often arrives with a secondary water damage component that needs to be addressed simultaneously.

## Our Fire Damage Restoration Process in Hubbard

Every fire loss is assessed before any cleaning or demolition begins. The IICRC FSRT (Fire and Smoke Restoration Technician) standard guides how we categorize soot type, map odor migration, and determine which materials can be restored versus which need to be removed. That distinction matters — unnecessary demolition adds cost and delays reconstruction, while leaving compromised material behind means odors return weeks later.

The process typically moves through these phases:

- **Emergency stabilization** — board-up, roof tarping if the structure is open to weather, and water extraction if suppression water is present
- **Soot and smoke mapping** — testing surfaces throughout the home, including HVAC returns and attic decking, to find where smoke traveled
- **Controlled demolition** — removing only what cannot be restored, documented for your insurance carrier
- **Dry-ice blasting or chemical sponge cleaning** — method depends on surface type and soot category
- **Odor neutralization** — hydroxyl generation or thermal fogging to address smoke molecules embedded in porous materials
- **Structural drying** — because suppression water often hides behind plaster or under subfloor, we use moisture mapping to confirm the structure is dry before reconstruction begins

Because we hold IICRC FSRT and ASD (Applied Structural Drying) certifications, both the fire and the water component are handled under one scope — no handoff between contractors, no gap in documentation.

## Local Note

Homes in this part of Trumbull County that were built with plaster-and-lath walls — still common in Hubbard's older residential blocks — absorb smoke odor more deeply than modern drywall because plaster is porous throughout its thickness, not just at the surface. Standard wiping and sealing often isn't enough; thermal fogging or hydroxyl treatment needs time to penetrate the full depth of the wall. Rushing that step is one of the most common reasons homeowners in older northeastern Ohio homes report lingering smoke smell months after a restoration is declared complete. We account for that dwell time in the project schedule rather than treating it as an afterthought.
