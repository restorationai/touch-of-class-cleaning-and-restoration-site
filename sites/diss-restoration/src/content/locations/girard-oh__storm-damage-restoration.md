## Why Girard Properties See Significant Storm Damage

Girard's housing stock skews older. A large share of the city's residential neighborhoods were built between the 1920s and the 1960s, when construction standards for wind and water resistance looked very different than they do today. Older wood-framed homes with original roof decking, aging mortar on brick chimneys, and single-pane windows are more vulnerable to wind-driven rain than newer builds. When a severe line of storms passes through, those older assemblies can let water into wall cavities, attic spaces, and basement window wells simultaneously — creating multiple entry points that are easy to miss during a quick walk-through.

The region also experiences a meaningful freeze-thaw cycle through late fall and early spring. Hail events that crack or dislodge shingles in October can leave a roof quietly leaking through winter, with the damage not becoming obvious until snowmelt saturates the insulation and ceiling drywall in February or March. That delayed discovery pattern is common in Trumbull County and tends to make claims more complicated.

## Our Storm Damage Restoration Process in Girard

Every storm loss is different, but the sequence of work follows a consistent logic: stop active intrusion, document everything before anything is moved, then dry and restore in the right order.

**Stabilization first.** If a tree has compromised a roof or a window is open to the weather, the first priority is emergency tarping and board-up to prevent additional water entry. We carry materials on every truck for exactly this reason.

**Thorough moisture mapping.** Wind-driven rain infiltrates in unexpected directions — under flashing, through soffit vents, along ridge lines. Our IICRC-certified technicians use thermal imaging and calibrated moisture meters to trace water migration behind walls and under flooring rather than relying on visible staining alone. This matters especially in Girard's older plaster-and-lath homes, where water can travel several feet laterally before showing on a ceiling.

**Structural drying to standard.** Drying is managed to the IICRC S500 and S520 standards, with daily monitoring logs that document moisture readings at each affected material. Those logs become part of your insurance claim file.

**Reconstruction.** Once materials are confirmed dry, we coordinate repairs — from roof decking and sheathing to interior drywall, insulation, and finish work — so you are working with one point of contact rather than managing separate contractors.

## Girard Insurance Coordination

Storm damage claims in Ohio involve a few moving parts that are worth understanding before you call your carrier. Insurers typically require documentation of the storm event itself — date, time, and weather service confirmation — alongside a scope of damage that separates pre-existing wear from acute storm loss. We photograph and document affected materials before any work begins, creating a defensible record that supports your adjuster's assessment. We work directly with most major carriers and can communicate the scope in the format adjusters expect, which tends to speed up the review process.

If your policy includes an ACV (actual cash value) provision rather than replacement cost, the documentation we provide can also support a supplemental claim if additional damage is found during the drying or repair phase — something that comes up more often in older Girard homes where storm access reveals deferred maintenance the carrier needs to distinguish from the acute loss.

## Local Note

Girard's older brick and block homes along the Mahoning River-adjacent streets present a specific challenge after high-wind events: mortar joints that have been softening for decades can allow wind-driven rain to penetrate the masonry itself, not just through windows or the roof. That moisture enters wall cavities from the exterior face of the brick rather than from above, which means standard roof-focused inspections miss it. When we assess storm damage in Girard's older residential areas, we include the exterior masonry envelope in our moisture survey — not just the attic and ceiling plane — because that's where the water is actually entering.
