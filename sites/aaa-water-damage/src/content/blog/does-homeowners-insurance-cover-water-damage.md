Homeowners insurance *sometimes* covers water damage — but the answer depends almost entirely on where the water came from and how fast it arrived. Sudden, accidental events (a pipe that bursts overnight, a washing machine hose that lets go) are typically covered. Slow, gradual leaks — a dripping supply line behind a cabinet that quietly soaks the subfloor over six months — almost never are. Flood water that enters from outside your home is a separate policy category altogether. Read on for a plain-English breakdown of what's covered, what's excluded, and what to do the moment you find damage.

## The Core Rule: Sudden vs. Gradual

Insurance adjusters live by one central question: *Was this sudden and accidental, or was it ongoing and preventable?*

**Events that are usually covered:**
- A supply pipe freezes and bursts (common in older Honolulu homes that weren't built with cold snaps in mind, but it does happen)
- A dishwasher or washing machine hose fails without warning
- An upstairs toilet overflows and water soaks through to the ceiling below
- A roof is damaged by a storm and rain enters through the breach
- An HVAC drain pan cracks and spills onto the ceiling of the room below

**Events that are usually NOT covered:**
- A slow drip under the sink that has been seeping for weeks or months
- Damage caused by a roof that was already in poor condition before a storm hit
- Mold that developed because a leak went unaddressed — even if the original leak was covered
- Sewer or drain backup (this is typically a separate rider; check your declarations page)
- Flooding from outside — rising groundwater, storm surge, overflowing streams

The gradual-damage exclusion is the one that catches most homeowners off guard. If an adjuster determines that the damage developed over time and a "reasonable homeowner" would have noticed it sooner, the claim can be denied even if the water source itself would otherwise be covered.

## Flood Damage Is a Completely Different Policy

This is one of the most common and costly misunderstandings in the industry. Standard homeowners insurance (HO-3 and HO-5 policies, which cover most Hawaii homes) does **not** cover flooding from an external water source — period. That includes:

- Storm surge from a hurricane or tropical storm
- Overflow from a nearby stream, canal, or drainage ditch
- Groundwater that rises into a basement or crawl space
- Sheet flooding across a yard that enters through a door or foundation crack

Flood coverage comes from the National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) or a private flood insurer, purchased as a separate policy. If you live in a low-lying area of Honolulu — near Keehi Lagoon, in parts of Kalihi, or anywhere in a FEMA-designated Special Flood Hazard Area — and you don't have a separate flood policy, you are uninsured for the most likely large-scale water event you'll face.

You can check your property's flood zone designation at FEMA's Flood Map Service Center (msc.fema.gov) using your address.

## What to Do the Moment You Discover Water Damage

How you respond in the first hour matters — both for the structure of your home and for your insurance claim.

1. **Stop the source if you safely can.** For a burst pipe or supply line, turn off the main water shutoff valve. For most Honolulu single-family homes, it's near the meter at the street. For a roof leak, you can't stop the rain, but you can move belongings and place buckets.
2. **Photograph everything before you touch it.** Walk through and shoot wide angles of each affected room, then close-ups of the water source, standing water, and any visible damage to walls, flooring, and cabinets. Do this even before you start mopping up — your insurer needs to see the pre-mitigation condition.
3. **Protect your property from further damage.** Your policy almost certainly requires you to take "reasonable steps" to prevent additional loss. Move furniture off wet carpet. Pull soaked rugs out of the room. Place plastic sheeting over a damaged roof section if it's safe to do so. Failing to mitigate can give an adjuster grounds to reduce your payout.
4. **Call your insurance company to open a claim.** You don't need to know the full scope of damage yet — just report the event and get a claim number. Ask specifically whether an adjuster will come in person or whether you'll need to submit photos and contractor estimates.
5. **Document your communication.** Note the date, time, and name of every person you speak with at your insurance company. Send follow-ups by email when possible so you have a written record.

## What NOT to Do After Water Damage

- **Don't tear out materials before the adjuster sees them.** If you rip out wet drywall before an adjuster documents it, you may lose the ability to claim that damage. If mold risk is urgent, photograph thoroughly and consult a restoration professional about the timing.
- **Don't assume the damage is minor because the surface looks dry.** In Hawaii's humid climate, moisture migrates into wall cavities, under tile, and into concrete slab floors faster than it evaporates. What looks like a small wet spot on drywall can mean saturated insulation and framing behind it. Moisture meters and thermal imaging reveal what eyes can't.
- **Don't sign a full release with your insurer before repairs are complete.** If hidden damage is found during restoration — rot, mold, compromised structural members — you want the claim still open.
- **Don't ignore the smell.** A musty or earthy odor after water intrusion is an early sign of microbial growth. Mold can begin colonizing porous materials within 24 to 48 hours in warm, humid conditions. Once it's established, remediation becomes a separate, more complex process.

## When to Call a Water Damage Restoration Professional

Some water events are genuinely DIY-manageable: a small appliance leak caught immediately, cleaned up within an hour, dried with fans over a day or two on a tile floor. Many are not.

Call a professional restoration company when:

- Standing water covers more than a small area, or has been present for more than a few hours
- Water has soaked into carpet, hardwood, drywall, or insulation
- The water source was a toilet, sewage line, or dishwasher drain (contaminated water requires different handling than clean supply-line water)
- You can smell mold or see discoloration on walls or ceilings
- The damage is in a wall cavity, under flooring, or otherwise not fully visible
- Your insurance company is sending an adjuster and you want an independent scope of damage documented

A professional assessment gives you two things: an accurate picture of the actual damage extent, and documentation that supports your insurance claim. Restoration companies use moisture meters, thermal cameras, and air movers calibrated to drying standards — equipment that gets materials genuinely dry rather than surface-dry.

## The Recovery Timeline: What Comes After the Claim

Once your claim is open and mitigation is underway, expect the process to move in phases:

- **Days 1–3:** Water extraction, structural drying with commercial air movers and dehumidifiers, moisture readings taken daily
- **Days 3–7:** Drying equipment removed once materials reach target moisture levels; damaged materials (drywall, flooring, insulation) identified for removal
- **Week 2 and beyond:** Reconstruction — new drywall, flooring, paint, cabinetry — begins after the structure is confirmed dry

The reconstruction phase is typically where insurance estimates and actual costs diverge. Material prices, labor availability, and the discovery of hidden damage during demo can all affect the final number. Keep all receipts and get itemized estimates in writing.
