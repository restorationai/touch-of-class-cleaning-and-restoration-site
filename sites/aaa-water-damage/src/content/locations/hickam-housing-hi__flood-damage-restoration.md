Hickam Housing sits on the southwestern edge of Oahu, where trade winds carry moisture inland from Pearl Harbor and the open Pacific, and where heavy Kona storm systems can drop several inches of rain in a matter of hours. When that water finds its way into a home, through a compromised slab, a overwhelmed storm drain, or wind-driven rain through a damaged lanai door, it moves fast and hides in places that look dry at the surface. Flood damage restoration here is not the same job it is on the mainland, and the conditions that make it different start the moment the water stops rising.

## Why Hickam Housing Properties Face Distinct Flood Risks

The housing stock on this part of Oahu reflects decades of military and civilian construction, with many homes built on concrete slab foundations that sit close to grade. When rainfall is heavy enough to overwhelm the surrounding drainage infrastructure, water does not have far to travel before it crosses a threshold. Slab foundations that show no visible cracking can still allow water to wick upward through the concrete itself, saturating vinyl flooring and the adhesive beneath it before a homeowner notices anything wrong.

The climate adds a layer of urgency that does not exist in drier regions. Oahu's consistently high relative humidity, often hovering between 70 and 80 percent even on clear days, means that wet building materials do not dry passively the way they might in a desert climate. Without active drying equipment, a flooded room in this environment can begin supporting microbial growth in as little as 24 to 48 hours. The warm ambient temperature accelerates that timeline. Getting drying equipment running quickly is not a best practice here, it is a necessity.

## Our Flood Damage Restoration Process in Hickam Housing

The first step after arrival is a full moisture assessment, using thermal imaging and calibrated moisture meters to map where water has traveled beyond what is visible. In slab-on-grade homes, this means checking baseboards, wall cavities, and flooring systems for hidden saturation. Water that enters through a doorway or drain often travels laterally under flooring before it surfaces elsewhere in the home.

Once the extent of the loss is mapped, standing water is extracted using truck-mounted and portable extraction equipment. After extraction, the drying phase begins with industrial air movers and refrigerant or desiccant dehumidifiers sized to the affected square footage. In Hickam Housing's climate, dehumidifier capacity matters more than it does in most of the continental United States, the equipment has to actively fight the ambient moisture in the outdoor air, not just the moisture in the structure. Readings are logged daily so that drying progress is documented and the job is not closed until moisture levels return to acceptable baselines for this region.

Materials that cannot be dried in place, including saturated drywall below the flood line, soaked insulation, and compromised flooring, are removed and properly disposed of before reconstruction begins. This step is not optional, leaving wet material inside a wall cavity in a warm, humid climate creates conditions that compound the original loss.

## Hickam Housing Insurance Coordination

Flood losses in this area often involve both standard homeowners policies and, for properties in designated flood zones, separate NFIP (National Flood Insurance Program) coverage. The documentation requirements for these two policy types differ, and having a restoration contractor who understands how to photograph, measure, and itemize a loss for adjuster review can reduce delays in the claims process. The team documents affected materials, moisture readings, and equipment placement in a format that supports the adjuster's scope of work, so the claim file reflects what actually happened to the structure.

## Local Note

Homes in this part of Oahu that were built during earlier military construction phases often used a textured plaster or stucco interior finish rather than standard paper-faced drywall. That material behaves differently during a flood loss: it is more resistant to immediate saturation but can trap moisture behind it for longer than drywall would, and it does not always show visible staining until the moisture content is already high. Thermal imaging is particularly useful in these homes because the surface can read visually dry while the wall assembly behind it is still holding significant moisture. Knowing to look for this is the difference between a complete dry-out and a callback three weeks later.
