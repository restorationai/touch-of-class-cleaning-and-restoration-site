## Why Kaneohe Base Properties See Flood Damage Differently

The windward Ko'olau corridor is one of the wettest stretches of the Hawaiian Islands. Rainfall totals here regularly outpace the leeward side of O'ahu by a significant margin, and the soil, much of it clay-heavy and slow-draining, does not absorb heavy rain quickly. When the ground is already saturated from a previous storm, even a moderate rainfall event can push water into crawl spaces, garages, and ground-floor living areas faster than most homeowners expect.

Housing on and near Kaneohe Base includes a mix of older military-era construction and post-statehood residential builds. Many of these structures use concrete masonry unit (CMU) block walls, which absorb moisture at the core even when the surface appears dry. Moisture meters calibrated for wood-framed drywall construction will read incorrectly on CMU, a detail that matters enormously when determining whether a wall cavity is actually dry before reconstruction begins.

## Our Flood Damage Restoration Process in Kaneohe Base

Flood cleanup in this environment is not a single-day event. The process moves in stages, and each stage has to be verified before the next begins.

**Water extraction** comes first. Standing water is removed using truck-mounted and portable extraction units capable of pulling water from carpet, subfloor, and low-lying structural cavities. In slab-on-grade homes common to this area, water that migrates beneath tile or vinyl flooring requires targeted extraction and, in some cases, floor covering removal to reach the concrete below.

**Structural drying** follows, using industrial air movers and dehumidifiers placed according to the layout of the affected space. High ambient humidity on the windward side of O'ahu means dehumidifiers are working against both the moisture in building materials and the moisture in the surrounding air. Drying timelines here are typically longer than they would be in a drier climate, a fact that affects both the equipment load and the monitoring schedule.

**Moisture mapping** runs throughout the drying phase. Readings are taken at consistent intervals across walls, floors, and ceilings to confirm that drying is progressing evenly and that no hidden wet pockets are being missed. Documentation from this phase is also what insurance adjusters use to verify the scope of work.

**Affected material removal** is handled when materials cannot be dried in place. Saturated insulation, swollen subfloor panels, and compromised drywall are removed and disposed of before reconstruction can begin.

## Local Note

CMU block construction, common in military-era housing in this area, creates a specific drying challenge that homeowners and even some contractors underestimate. Because the block is porous, water wicks into the core of the wall and can remain there long after the interior surface feels dry to the touch. Standard drying protocols designed for wood-frame construction will not reach that moisture. Effective drying in these structures requires extended drying times, higher air movement across wall surfaces, and monitoring equipment capable of reading moisture levels in masonry. Skipping this step is one of the more common reasons mold appears weeks after a flood cleanup that seemed complete at the time.
