## Our Water Damage Restoration Process in Aiea

Every job starts with a thorough assessment — not a visual scan, but moisture readings taken with calibrated meters at walls, subfloors, and ceiling cavities. In older plantation homes, this matters especially because the original hardwood subfloors and tongue-and-groove wall paneling common in that era absorb and hold moisture differently than modern OSB or drywall. Water can wick several feet laterally through those materials before showing a visible stain.

Once the scope is mapped, extraction comes first. Commercial-grade truck-mounted and portable extractors pull standing water from carpet, flooring, and crawl spaces. After extraction, drying equipment — high-velocity air movers and refrigerant dehumidifiers calibrated for Hawaii's ambient humidity — is positioned to create directed airflow through the structure. In Aiea's climate, ambient relative humidity rarely drops below 60–70%, which means passive drying is not a realistic option; the equipment has to do the work the climate won't.

Moisture readings are logged daily. Equipment is adjusted or repositioned based on what the numbers show, not on a fixed schedule. Drying is complete when readings return to pre-loss baselines for that specific material — not when the floor feels dry underfoot.

One detail that surprises Aiea homeowners unfamiliar with restoration work: the older homes in Waimalu and lower Aiea Heights frequently have interior walls finished with a skim-coat plaster over wood lath rather than standard drywall.
