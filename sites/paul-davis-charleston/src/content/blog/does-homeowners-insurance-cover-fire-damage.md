**TL;DR:** Standard homeowners insurance covers fire damage from sudden, accidental fires, including repairs to your home, damage to other structures like a detached garage, destroyed belongings, and additional living expenses while you're displaced. It typically excludes damage from arson, long-term vacancy, or intentional acts, but water used to extinguish the fire is covered as part of the same claim. Most policies pay replacement cost on the structure and either replacement cost or actual cash value on contents, depending on what you bought.

If you're standing in a house that still smells like smoke, waiting on an adjuster to call back, you don't need a lecture on insurance theory. You need to know what's actually going to get paid for and what you're going to have to cover yourself. Here's the breakdown, bucket by bucket, plus the two things that catch people off guard most: the replacement cost versus actual cash value distinction, and the paperwork a contractor might ask you to sign before touching anything.

## What does a standard homeowners policy actually cover after a fire?

A standard HO-3 homeowners policy pays for fire damage under four separate coverages, and understanding which bucket your loss falls into determines how the check gets written. Dwelling coverage (Coverage A) pays to repair or rebuild the structure itself, framing, drywall, roof, electrical, everything attached to the house. Other Structures coverage (Coverage B) handles a detached garage, shed, or fence if the fire spread there.

Personal Property coverage (Coverage C) pays for your belongings: furniture, clothing, electronics, anything the fire or smoke destroyed or made unusable. And Loss of Use coverage, often called Additional Living Expenses (ALE), reimburses hotel stays, temporary rentals, and the extra cost of eating out while your kitchen is unusable. According to the [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/what-does-homeowners-insurance-cover), fire and lightning are among the most commonly covered perils in a standard policy, and most carriers treat fire losses as a single claim that pulls from all four coverages as needed.

Smoke damage isn't a separate claim category. It's covered under the same dwelling and contents coverages as the fire itself, since smoke and soot migration is a direct result of the fire. That matters because insurers sometimes try to draw a line between "fire damage" and "smoke damage" when a room wasn't directly burned, and that line usually doesn't hold up under a properly documented claim.

## Replacement cost versus actual cash value: which one do you actually have?

Replacement cost value (RCV) pays what it costs to buy the item new today; actual cash value (ACV) pays replacement cost minus depreciation for age and wear. A ten-year-old sofa destroyed by smoke might cost $1,800 to replace today, but an ACV policy might only pay $700 for it, factoring in how much life the carrier believes it had left.

Most modern homeowners policies write dwelling coverage on an RCV basis, meaning the structure itself gets rebuilt at today's material and labor costs, not depreciated value. Contents coverage is where RCV versus ACV diverges most. Some policies pay RCV on contents outright; others pay ACV first and release the depreciation holdback (the difference between ACV and RCV) only after you actually replace the item and submit a receipt. Check your declarations page, the line will say "Replacement Cost" or "Actual Cash Value" next to Coverage C.

This is the single most common surprise in a fire claim. Homeowners expect a check for what things cost today and get a check for what things were worth yesterday. If your policy is RCV-with-holdback, keep every receipt for replacement items; that paperwork is what unlocks the second check.

## What's not covered, and the one exception people get wrong

Standard homeowners insurance excludes fires you set intentionally, fires in a home vacant more than the policy's stated period (often 30 to 60 days), and damage from a fire that started due to known, unaddressed hazards you failed to disclose. Arson by the policyholder voids coverage outright, and most carriers will investigate cause of origin before paying a significant claim, which is part of why fire claims take longer to settle than a simple water loss.

Vacancy clauses catch people with rental properties, seasonal homes, or homes mid-renovation. If nobody's living in the house and the policy's vacancy window has passed, standard coverage can lapse for fire, vandalism, and several other perils. If a property sits empty for any stretch, a vacant-home endorsement closes that gap.

Here's the exception people assume works against them but actually doesn't: water damage from firefighting efforts is covered. Fire departments use thousands of gallons to knock down a structure fire, and that water soaks subfloors, drops ceilings, and ruins contents the flames never touched. Insurers treat that water damage as part of the fire loss, not a separate, excluded water claim. If you're staring at soaked carpet two rooms away from where the fire actually burned, that's still your fire claim's problem to pay for. The [National Association of Insurance Commissioners](https://www.naic.org/consumer_home.htm) publishes state-specific consumer guides if you want to check how your state's insurance department treats fire-related water loss.

## What is Assignment of Benefits, and should you sign one?

An Assignment of Benefits (AOB) is a legal document that signs your right to collect insurance payment over to the contractor doing the repair work, letting them bill and negotiate with the insurer directly instead of you. In exchange for convenience, you're handing over a real right: the ability to control who gets paid, how much, and when.

AOBs aren't inherently bad. They can speed up a claim by letting the restoration company deal with the adjuster directly instead of routing every invoice through you first. The risk shows up when the document is vague about scope, lets the contractor bill the insurer for whatever amount they choose, or locks you into a relationship even if the work goes sideways. Before signing anything, read exactly what's being assigned, confirm it's limited to this specific loss and scope of work, and ask whether you can still see and approve the itemized estimate before it's submitted.

## What should you do in the first few days to protect your claim?

Document everything before you touch or discard a single item, because an adjuster who never saw the damage firsthand relies entirely on your photos and the restoration company's written scope. Photograph every room, every damaged surface, and anything you plan to throw away, from multiple angles, before cleanup starts.

Avoid tossing smoke-damaged items prematurely. Soft goods like clothing, bedding, and upholstered furniture with heavy smoke odor often can't be fully restored and do need to go, but let the restoration company document and itemize them first; a photographed, itemized loss list supports a higher payout than "we threw it all out and don't have receipts." Keep receipts for any temporary lodging, meals, or essentials purchased while displaced; those feed directly into your ALE claim.
