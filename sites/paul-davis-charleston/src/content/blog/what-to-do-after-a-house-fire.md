**TL;DR:** After a house fire, wait for the fire department or fire marshal to release the structure before entering. In the first hour, call your insurance agent and get emergency board-up if windows or doors are open. In the first 24 hours, document everything with photos before touching debris. In Charleston's humid air, firefighting water left behind starts growing mold within 24 to 48 hours, so water extraction has to happen alongside soot and smoke cleanup, not after it.

If the fire trucks just pulled away from your street in Mount Pleasant, James Island, or downtown Charleston and you're standing on the sidewalk wondering what happens next, you're not alone in that moment, but you are on a clock. A house fire never travels alone. It brings heat and structural damage, soot and smoke that migrate through the HVAC system into rooms the fire never touched, and hundreds or thousands of gallons of firefighting water sitting in your walls and under your floors. In Charleston's Lowcountry humidity, that water starts supporting mold growth faster than almost anywhere else in the country. What you do in the next 72 hours determines how much of your home and your belongings can actually be saved.

## Is it safe to go back inside your home after a house fire?

No, not until the fire department or your local fire marshal formally releases the structure, usually with a verbal or written all-clear at the scene. Even a fire that looks "out" can leave hot spots inside walls, weakened flooring, or compromised electrical systems that aren't safe to walk on or touch.

Charleston Fire Department or the county fire marshal typically inspects for structural integrity and re-ignition risk before anyone, including you, goes back in. If your insurance company or a restoration crew arrives before that release happens, they'll wait too. This isn't bureaucracy for its own sake. Charred floor joists and fire-weakened stair treads collapse under normal weight, and a fire that appears extinguished can smolder inside insulation or wall cavities for hours.

Once you're cleared, go in briefly and only to retrieve medications, documents, or pets if it's safe. Full assessment and cleanup should wait for daylight and, ideally, for someone trained to read what's structurally sound.

## What should you do in the first hour after a house fire?

In the first hour, secure the property, contact your insurance carrier, and arrange emergency board-up if the fire left doors, windows, or the roof open to the weather. That single hour sets up everything that follows.

Here's what that looks like in practice:

- Call your insurance agent or carrier's claims line and open a claim number while you're still on scene, if possible.
- Ask the fire department for a copy of the incident report; you'll need it for the claim.
- If the structure has open windows, a burned-through roof section, or a door that no longer closes, arrange board-up and tarping right away. An open house in Charleston's coastal air invites rain, looters, and wildlife within hours.
- Take a first round of photos and short video walkthroughs, from the exterior first, before anything gets moved or covered.
- Arrange somewhere to stay. Most homeowners policies include additional living expense (ALE) coverage that reimburses hotel and meal costs while the home is unlivable; ask your adjuster to confirm this early.

Don't try to run appliances, flip breakers, or test utilities yourself. Let the fire department or a licensed electrician confirm the electrical and gas systems are safe first.

## What do you need to do in the first 24 hours after a house fire?

In the first 24 hours, document the damage thoroughly, protect what's salvageable from further exposure, and avoid disturbing debris until it's been photographed and, if needed, reviewed by your adjuster. Rushing to clean up before documentation is one of the most common ways homeowners lose claim value.

Walk each room with your phone camera before touching anything, including closets, attics, and the garage. Soot travels along air currents and settles in rooms far from the fire's origin. Note water pooling on floors or dripping from ceilings; this is firefighting water, and it's now doing its own kind of damage.

Do not run box fans or open windows to "air out" the smoke smell. That circulates soot particles deeper into upholstery, drywall, and HVAC ductwork, and it's one of the reasons DIY smoke odor removal usually fails where a [professional deodorization process](/blog/smoke-odor-removal-techniques/) succeeds. Do not attempt to wash soot off walls with water or household cleaner either; wet-wiping dry soot smears it permanently into porous surfaces.

If debris removal after the house fire feels urgent, especially charred furniture or damaged flooring, wait until your insurance adjuster or restoration company has documented it in place. Once it's logged, disposal can begin.

## Why does the first 72 hours matter so much in a Charleston home?

The first 72 hours matter because three kinds of damage arrive at once after a house fire, and one of them, standing water, turns into mold in the Lowcountry's humidity faster than almost anywhere else. Heat and structural damage happen instantly. Soot and smoke residue set into surfaces within hours. Firefighting water left in flooring, drywall, and insulation starts supporting mold growth in 24 to 48 hours in Charleston's humid air, according to guidance from the [Environmental Protection Agency](https://www.epa.gov/mold) on moisture and mold timelines.

That's why fire restoration in a coastal SC home like one on James Island or in North Charleston can't treat water extraction as a separate job that happens "later." A restoration crew addressing fire and smoke damage without pulling standing water first is setting up a second disaster inside the walls of the first one. This is also why homes near the marsh or with older wood-frame construction common in Charleston's historic districts need faster response than the same fire would demand in a drier climate. For more on how quickly mold takes hold once moisture is present, see [how quickly mold grows after water damage](/blog/mold-after-water-damage/).

## What does a fire restoration company actually do?

A fire restoration company assesses structural and smoke damage, extracts any firefighting water, removes unsalvageable debris, cleans and deodorizes what can be saved, and rebuilds what can't. The work typically runs in that order, though water extraction and initial smoke assessment often happen on the same visit.

The general sequence:

1. **Assessment and documentation.** A technician walks the property, photographs damage, and writes a scope of work your insurance adjuster can review.
2. **Water extraction and drying.** Any firefighting water gets pulled out with extraction equipment, and affected materials are dried with air movers and dehumidifiers before mold has a chance to start.
3. **Debris and content removal.** Charred, unsalvageable materials go out. Salvageable contents may be packed out for off-site cleaning.
4. **Soot and smoke cleanup.** Surfaces are cleaned with techniques matched to the soot type; HEPA air scrubbers pull particulate and odor out of the air.
5. **Deodorization.** Thermal fogging or hydroxyl treatment neutralizes smoke odor at the molecular level, not just masks it.
6. **Reconstruction.** Drywall, flooring, and framing that didn't survive get rebuilt to close out the claim.

## How long does fire damage restoration take?

Most residential fire damage restoration takes anywhere from a few weeks for a contained, single-room fire to several months for a fire that caused structural damage or required full reconstruction. The timeline depends on how much of the home was affected, how far smoke traveled, and whether load-bearing framing needs rebuilding.

A small kitchen fire with contained smoke migration might be cleaned, deodorized, and repaired in two to four weeks. A fire that burned through a roof or multiple rooms, requiring structural rebuild, can run three to six months once you factor in permitting and reconstruction. Water damage from firefighting efforts, if not addressed within that first 24 to 48 hours, can add mold remediation to the timeline, which is why fast water extraction matters as much as the fire cleanup itself.

Your restoration company should give you a written scope and rough timeline once the assessment is complete, and update it as work uncovers hidden damage behind walls or under flooring.

A house fire is disorienting, and it's easy to either freeze or rush. The steps above are the same order a restoration crew follows once they're on site: safety and documentation first, water and debris second, cleanup and rebuild last.
