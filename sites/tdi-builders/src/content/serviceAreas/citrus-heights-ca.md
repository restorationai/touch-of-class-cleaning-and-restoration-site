## Restoration emergencies common in Citrus Heights

Citrus Heights sits in the Sacramento Valley's inland climate zone, where summers are long and dry and winters bring concentrated rainfall rather than gradual soaking. That pattern, months of drought followed by atmospheric river events, puts real stress on roofing, gutters, and aging drainage systems all at once. When a storm drops two inches of rain in a few hours on soil that has baked hard through October, water moves fast across surfaces and finds every gap in a foundation stem wall or a cracked driveway apron before it can percolate.

The city's housing stock is dominated by single-family homes built from the late 1950s through the 1980s, a period when tract construction moved quickly and moisture barriers were minimal by today's standards. Slab-on-grade foundations are common, and when a supply line or drain fails inside a slab, water migrates silently under flooring for days before a homeowner notices warped laminate or a musty smell near a baseboard. The Sacramento Valley's summer heat also creates ideal attic conditions for mold colonization after any roof leak goes unaddressed, mold can begin establishing within 24 to 48 hours of sustained moisture.

## Building stock, site conditions, and permits in Citrus Heights

Most of Citrus Heights was developed between the late 1950s and the mid-1980s, which means the dominant construction is wood-frame on slab-on-grade, crawlspace foundations are far less common here than in older Sacramento neighborhoods. Homes of that era frequently used galvanized steel supply lines and cast-iron drain lines, both of which corrode from the inside out and can fail with little visible warning. Any restoration scope on a pre-1980 home should account for the possibility of asbestos-containing materials in floor tile, joint compound, and duct insulation, as well as lead-based paint on interior and exterior surfaces.

The soils across much of Sacramento County, including Citrus Heights, include expansive clay fractions that swell when wet and shrink in dry summers. That movement stresses slab edges and can open hairline cracks that admit moisture during wet winters. Drainage across the city is generally sheet-flow to the street rather than deep percolation, so low spots against foundations collect water quickly in heavy rain.
