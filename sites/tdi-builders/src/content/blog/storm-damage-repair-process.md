After a storm tears through your property, the path from soaked insulation and missing shingles to a finished, code-compliant rebuild follows a predictable sequence — even when the damage itself feels chaotic. Understanding each phase helps you ask the right questions, avoid costly missteps, and move through the insurance process without getting stuck. Here is what that process actually looks like, from the first inspection through the final walkthrough.

## Step 1: The Initial Inspection — What Gets Documented and Why

Before any work begins, a thorough inspection establishes the full scope of damage. This is not just a visual sweep. A qualified inspector will probe roofing materials for soft spots, check attic insulation for moisture intrusion, look behind fascia boards for rot that started before the storm, and assess whether structural members — rafters, ridge boards, wall framing — took any load impact.

In Sacramento, storms tend to arrive as atmospheric river events: multi-day rain at volume rather than brief violent squalls. That pattern matters because water that enters through a compromised roof on day one can travel laterally through insulation and wall cavities by day three, showing up as a ceiling stain in a room nowhere near the original breach. An inspection that only looks at the obvious entry point will miss that migration.

Document everything before anything is moved or dried. Photograph the roof from multiple angles, the interior ceiling and walls, any pooling on floors, and the attic if accessible. This documentation is the foundation of your insurance claim. If you have a contractor on-site before an adjuster visits, ask them to hold off on removing materials until the adjuster has seen the damage in place — or until you have confirmed your carrier will accept contractor documentation in lieu of an in-person visit.

## Step 2: Emergency Stabilization — Stopping the Loss Before It Grows

Once the inspection is complete and documented, the priority shifts to stopping active damage. This phase is sometimes called "mitigation" and it typically happens within the first 24 to 72 hours.

For storm damage, stabilization usually means:

1. **Tarping the roof** — Heavy-duty polyethylene tarps are secured over any area where shingles, underlayment, or decking are missing or compromised. The tarp needs to extend past the ridge and be fastened in a way that won't pull free in the next wind event.
2. **Board-up or temporary glazing** — Broken windows or damaged siding openings get covered to prevent additional water intrusion and security exposure.
3. **Water extraction and drying** — If water has entered living spaces, it needs to come out quickly. Wet insulation, wet drywall, and wet subfloor materials that sit for more than 24 to 48 hours begin to support mold growth. Industrial air movers and dehumidifiers are not optional on a job with significant interior moisture.
4. **Debris removal from drainage paths** — Gutters packed with debris from the storm, or downspouts disconnected by wind, will redirect the next rainfall straight against the foundation or back under the eaves.

What you should not do during this phase: do not apply roofing cement, caulk, or consumer-grade sealant over damaged areas and call it fixed. These materials fail under thermal cycling and UV exposure, and they can trap moisture against wood decking in a way that accelerates rot. They also create complications when a roofer later tries to assess the true extent of damage.

## Step 3: The Insurance Claim — How It Runs Parallel to the Work

Most storm damage repairs involve a homeowner's insurance claim, and the claim process runs alongside — not after — the physical repair work. A few things to understand:

**Your adjuster's estimate is a starting point, not a final number.** Adjusters work from software databases that price materials and labor at regional averages. If your roof has a steep pitch, requires specialized materials to match existing, or reveals additional damage once the first layer is removed, the initial estimate will need to be supplemented. Contractors experienced in insurance work know how to write and submit supplements.

**Depreciation and your deductible are separate things.** Actual cash value policies pay out replacement cost minus depreciation. Recoverable depreciation can often be claimed once work is complete and receipts are submitted. Read your policy's loss settlement language before assuming what you will receive.

**Keep every receipt, every photo, every communication.** If you hire someone for emergency tarping before your adjuster arrives, that cost is typically covered — but you need documentation of who did the work, when, and what they charged.

## Step 4: Structural Repair and Roofing Replacement

Once the claim scope is agreed upon — or at least enough of it to proceed — the rebuild phase begins. For most residential storm damage in the Sacramento area, this centers on roofing work, though it frequently extends to:

- **Roof decking replacement** — Sheathing that has delaminated or rotted needs to come out before new underlayment and shingles go down. Skipping this step and roofing over compromised decking is a code violation and a warranty void.
- **Underlayment upgrade** — Many older Sacramento homes were built with 15-lb felt. Current code and most manufacturer warranties require synthetic underlayment or, in certain applications, self-adhering membrane at eaves and valleys.
- **Fascia, soffit, and gutter work** — Wind-driven rain frequently damages these components even when the roof field survives intact. Replacing shingles without addressing rotted fascia means the new roof is draining against deteriorating wood.
- **Interior finish work** — Drywall, insulation, paint, and flooring that were water-damaged need to be replaced after the structure is dry and the moisture readings confirm the framing is back to acceptable levels. Closing up walls before the wood is dry traps moisture and guarantees a mold problem.

Permits are required for most structural roofing work in Sacramento County. A contractor who suggests skipping the permit to save time is creating a liability that will surface at resale or at the next insurance claim.

## Step 5: Final Inspection and Closeout

The last phase is often underestimated. A final inspection — either a city inspection if permits were pulled, or a thorough contractor walkthrough — confirms that the work was completed to code and that no secondary issues were introduced during construction.

For roofing work, this means verifying flashing at all penetrations (vents, pipes, chimneys, skylights), checking valley detail, and confirming that ridge and hip caps are properly nailed and sealed. For interior work, it means a final moisture reading of any framing that was wet, confirmation that insulation is properly installed and vapor barriers are correct for the climate zone, and a visual check of all finished surfaces.

Once the work passes inspection, your contractor should provide you with warranty documentation for both materials and labor, and any manufacturer warranty registration that requires homeowner action.
