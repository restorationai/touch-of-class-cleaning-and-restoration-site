Most mold you find in a home is not the infamous "black mold" — but the color alone won't tell you which is which. Here's the short answer: *Stachybotrys chartarum*, the species commonly called black mold, is dark greenish-black, slimy when wet, and almost always grows on materials that have stayed wet for at least a week or two. Common household molds — *Cladosporium*, *Penicillium*, *Aspergillus* — can also appear black, gray, green, or brown. You cannot reliably identify a mold species by sight. What you *can* assess at home is the size of the problem, where it's growing, and whether it's time to stop guessing and call a professional.

## Why Color Is a Poor Diagnostic Tool

The cultural fixation on "black mold" comes from legitimate concern — *Stachybotrys* produces mycotoxins that can cause serious respiratory symptoms in some people, particularly those with asthma, allergies, or compromised immune systems. But the anxiety around it has created a misleading shortcut: if it's black, it must be toxic; if it's green or white, it's probably fine.

Neither assumption holds up.

- *Aspergillus niger* is jet black and extremely common in bathrooms and on food.
- *Cladosporium* frequently appears olive-green to black on window sills and HVAC grilles.
- *Stachybotrys* is almost never the first mold to colonize a wet surface — it typically arrives weeks after other species have already established themselves.
- Some *Penicillium* species are blue-green and can produce mycotoxins as well.

The only way to confirm a species is laboratory analysis — either an air quality test or a tape-lift sample sent to a certified lab. Visual identification, including by contractors, is always preliminary.

### What *Stachybotrys* Actually Looks Like

If you want a practical description: *Stachybotrys* tends to grow in dense, irregular patches. When the colony is actively wet, it has a slightly gelatinous or slimy sheen. When it dries out, it becomes powdery and can look almost charcoal-gray. It almost exclusively grows on cellulose-rich materials — drywall paper, ceiling tiles, wood framing, cardboard — that have experienced prolonged saturation, not just surface condensation. You typically won't find it on tile grout or painted concrete.

If you're seeing dark mold on the back of drywall after opening a wall following a slow leak, that is a scenario where *Stachybotrys* is plausible and professional testing is worth the cost.

## How to Assess What You're Looking At

Before you touch anything, take a few minutes to gather information. You're trying to answer three questions:

1. **How large is the affected area?** EPA guidance generally treats anything under 10 square feet as potentially manageable by a careful homeowner. Anything larger — or anything that suggests the mold has spread inside walls, under flooring, or into HVAC systems — warrants professional assessment.
2. **What is it growing on?** Mold on non-porous surfaces (ceramic tile, glass, metal) can usually be cleaned. Mold that has penetrated porous materials (drywall, insulation, wood framing, carpet backing) typically means those materials need to be removed, not just wiped down.
3. **Is there an active or unresolved moisture source?** Mold is a symptom. If the leak, condensation problem, or flooding that caused it hasn't been fixed, cleaning the mold is temporary at best.

Take photographs before disturbing anything. If you do end up filing an insurance claim or bringing in a remediation contractor, documentation of the original condition is useful.

## What Not to Do

A few common mistakes that make the situation worse:

**Don't bleach it and call it done.** Bleach kills surface mold on non-porous materials, but it doesn't penetrate drywall or wood deeply enough to address the root colony. More importantly, it doesn't remove the dead spores and mycotoxins — and those can still trigger reactions in sensitive individuals.

**Don't run a fan directly on a moldy area.** Disturbing mold with airflow sends spores into the rest of the house. If you need to ventilate the space, open a window and use negative pressure (a fan blowing *out*) rather than circulating air through the home.

**Don't tear out drywall without containment.** Cutting into mold-affected drywall without plastic sheeting and proper respiratory protection is one of the fastest ways to spread contamination to adjacent rooms. This is one reason professional remediation uses negative-pressure containment chambers.

**Don't rely on mold-killing paint as a fix.** Encapsulating mold under paint is not remediation. It may pass a visual inspection temporarily, but it doesn't address structural damage or ongoing moisture, and it can complicate future testing.

**Don't ignore symptoms.** If anyone in the household is experiencing persistent coughing, unexplained headaches, eye irritation, or worsening asthma that correlates with time spent at home, take that seriously and consult a physician — not a contractor. A contractor can fix the building; a doctor evaluates health.

## When to Call a Professional

Call a mold remediation professional when:

- The affected area is larger than roughly 10 square feet, or you can't determine where the growth ends.
- The mold is inside walls, under flooring, in a crawl space, or in an HVAC system.
- You've had a slow or hidden leak — a supply line dripping inside a wall, a roof leak that went unnoticed for weeks — rather than a quick, obvious spill that dried fast.
- Someone in the home has a respiratory condition, is immunocompromised, or is an infant.
- You've cleaned the visible mold more than once and it keeps returning.
- You want species identification before deciding how to proceed.

## The Longer Picture: Remediation and Rebuilding

Remediation removes the mold and the conditions that allowed it to grow. Restoration puts the structure back together — new drywall, insulation, framing if needed, paint, and whatever finish materials were removed during containment. These are two distinct phases, and not every contractor does both.

If you're going through insurance, the adjuster will typically want a scope of work that separates the remediation cost from the reconstruction cost. Keeping those phases with a single contractor who can document both can simplify the claims process considerably.

Once the work is done, a clearance test — air sampling or surface sampling performed by an independent industrial hygienist — confirms that spore counts have returned to normal background levels. Don't skip this step. It's the only objective confirmation that the remediation was complete.
