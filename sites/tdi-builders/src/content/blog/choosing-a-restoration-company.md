Choosing a restoration company after a flood, fire, or mold discovery is one of the few decisions you'll make while already in crisis mode. The short answer: vet the contractor *before* you sign anything, confirm they work directly with your insurance carrier, and never let urgency pressure you into skipping basic due diligence. The rest of this guide walks you through exactly how to do that — even when the water is still on the floor.

## Why the Restoration Industry Attracts Bad Actors

Water damage, fire damage, and mold are emotionally loaded events. You're displaced, stressed, and often fielding calls from your insurance adjuster before the smoke has cleared. That pressure creates the perfect conditions for what the industry calls "storm chasing" — contractors who follow major weather events into affected areas, knock on doors, and push homeowners to sign assignment-of-benefits agreements before a legitimate estimate has been written.

Sacramento's wet winters and periodic atmospheric river events bring this pattern to the region reliably. After a significant storm rolls through the Central Valley, unlicensed or out-of-area crews sometimes appear in neighborhoods like Pocket, Natomas, and Elk Grove before local contractors have even gotten their trucks loaded. That doesn't mean every contractor who shows up quickly is suspect — response speed matters in restoration — but it does mean you should know what to look for before you hand anyone a signature.

## Five Things to Verify Before You Hire Anyone

Think of this as your pre-hire checklist. Run through it even if you're in a hurry.

1. **California Contractor's License Board (CSLB) status.** Every restoration contractor working in California must hold a valid CSLB license. Look them up at cslb.ca.gov before you sign. An unlicensed crew performing structural drying or demolition on your property creates liability that falls back on you.

2. **Proof of general liability and workers' compensation insurance.** Ask for a certificate of insurance naming you as an additional insured for the duration of the project. If a worker is injured on your property and the contractor carries no workers' comp, your homeowner's policy may be on the hook.

3. **IICRC certification.** The Institute of Inspection, Cleaning and Restoration Certification sets the technical standards the industry runs on — the S500 for water damage, the S520 for mold remediation. A company whose technicians hold IICRC credentials has been trained to those standards. Ask which certifications their field crew holds, not just the owner.

4. **Direct insurance billing experience.** A contractor who has worked with your carrier before knows how to document losses in the format adjusters expect — moisture readings, photo logs, scope-of-loss reports. That documentation gap is often what causes claim disputes, not the damage itself.

5. **A local, verifiable address.** Not a P.O. box, not a suite number at a UPS Store. A physical location you can visit if something goes wrong. Out-of-area contractors who arrive after a storm and leave after the job offer little recourse if the work is substandard.

## Red Flags During the Estimate

The estimate conversation tells you almost as much as the paperwork. Watch for these:

- **Pressure to sign an assignment of benefits (AOB) immediately.** An AOB transfers your insurance claim rights directly to the contractor. That's not always wrong, but signing before you understand what you're authorizing — or before your adjuster has been notified — can complicate your claim significantly.
- **A verbal estimate with no written scope.** Legitimate restoration work is documented line by line. If a contractor quotes you a number without walking the damage and producing a written scope, that number will change.
- **Vague drying timelines.** Water behind plaster can read dry at the surface while the lath and framing stay saturated for weeks. A contractor who says "we'll be done in two days" without taking baseline moisture readings hasn't actually assessed your loss.
- **No mention of industrial drying equipment.** Fans from a hardware store are not restoration equipment. Ask what they're bringing: commercial dehumidifiers, air movers, thermal imaging cameras, moisture meters. The answer tells you whether they're set up to do the job correctly.
- **Mold guarantees.** No legitimate contractor guarantees mold will never return after remediation. Mold is a moisture problem; if the moisture source isn't fixed, mold follows. Anyone who promises otherwise is selling you something.

## What the Restoration Process Actually Looks Like

Understanding the sequence helps you evaluate whether a contractor's plan makes sense.

**Water damage** follows a three-phase arc: emergency mitigation (stopping the source, extracting standing water), structural drying (typically measured in days, not hours, using calibrated equipment and daily moisture readings), and rebuild (replacing materials that couldn't be dried in place). Skipping or rushing the drying phase is the most common cause of mold problems that appear six weeks after a "completed" job.

**Fire and smoke damage** is more chemically complex than it looks. Smoke residue penetrates porous materials — drywall, insulation, wood framing, fabric — and the odor compounds bond at a molecular level. Effective fire restoration involves controlled demolition of unsalvageable materials, HEPA filtration, specialized chemical neutralization, and often ozone or hydroxyl treatment for odor. A coat of Kilz over smoke-stained drywall is not remediation.

**Mold remediation** in Sacramento homes — many of which were built in the postwar tract-housing boom with wood-frame construction and older plumbing — often involves more square footage than the visible staining suggests. Mold colonizes in as little as 24 to 48 hours after moisture intrusion, and it spreads behind walls and under flooring before it's visible. A proper remediation includes containment, HEPA air scrubbing, removal of affected materials to a defined clearance standard, and post-remediation verification testing by a third party.

## How to Handle the Insurance Side

Your contractor and your adjuster are not adversaries, but they're also not on the same team. Here's how to navigate it:

- **Notify your carrier before work begins** whenever possible. Emergency mitigation (stopping active water, boarding a broken window) is generally covered without prior approval, but large-scope demolition or rebuild should be authorized.
- **Keep every receipt and photograph everything** before any material is removed. Your adjuster needs documentation of what existed before the loss.
- **Request a copy of the contractor's scope of loss** and compare it to your adjuster's estimate. Discrepancies are normal and negotiable; large gaps may require a public adjuster or attorney.
- **Understand your policy's actual cash value vs. replacement cost value language.** ACV policies depreciate materials; RCV policies pay to restore to pre-loss condition. That difference can run into tens of thousands of dollars on a significant loss.

## Making the Call

If you're reading this because something just happened — a pipe burst, a fire, a smell that turned out to be mold — the most important thing you can do right now is slow down enough to make a good decision. The damage that's already occurred is not going to get dramatically worse in the hour it takes to verify a license, check a certificate of insurance, and read a scope before signing.
