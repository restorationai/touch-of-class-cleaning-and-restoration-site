Stop the water first. Everything else comes second. If a pipe burst, find your main shutoff valve — usually near the water meter, in a utility closet, or along the foundation wall in older Sacramento homes — and turn it off. If the source is a roof leak or appliance overflow, cut power to the affected area at the breaker before you step into standing water. Once the water is no longer spreading, you have a narrow window to limit the damage. Here is how to use it.

## Why the First 24 Hours Are the Most Critical

Water moves in ways that aren't obvious. It follows framing cavities downward, wicks into drywall from the bottom up, and saturates subfloor materials that feel firm underfoot. In Sacramento's climate — warm, dry summers and wet winters — homes built before the 1980s often have original hardwood floors and plaster walls that absorb moisture quickly and release it slowly. Mold can begin colonizing wet organic material within 24 to 48 hours under the right temperature and humidity conditions. That's not a scare tactic; it's the reason the timeline matters.

The goal of the first day is not to fully dry your home. It's to stop the spread, document what happened, and protect the structure from the damage that compounds silently overnight.

## Immediate Steps to Take Right Now

Work through these in order. Don't skip to the cleanup before the source is controlled.

1. **Stop the water source.** Shut off the main supply valve, close the valve behind the affected appliance, or divert roof drainage away from the breach. If you can't locate the source, call your utility or a plumber before doing anything else.

2. **Cut power to wet areas.** Water and live circuits are a lethal combination. If you're uncertain whether an outlet or fixture is in the affected zone, turn off the breaker for that room. Don't re-energize until a licensed electrician has inspected.

3. **Document everything before you touch it.** Walk the perimeter and take photos and video on your phone — wide shots showing the room, close-ups of the water line on walls, and shots of any damaged belongings. Your insurance adjuster will want this, and it's nearly impossible to recreate once cleanup begins.

4. **Remove standing water if you can do so safely.** A wet/dry shop vac handles small volumes well. For larger accumulations, a submersible pump moves water faster. Mop up what remains and wring towels into a drain — not into another room.

5. **Move salvageable belongings to a dry area.** Furniture sitting in water wicks moisture up through legs and into upholstery. Move rugs, cushions, and wood furniture out of the wet zone. Place aluminum foil or plastic sheeting under furniture legs that can't be moved.

6. **Ventilate, but carefully.** Open windows and interior doors to increase airflow — unless outdoor humidity is higher than indoor humidity, which can make things worse. In Sacramento's dry summer months, outside air is usually your ally. In January and February, check the forecast before opening up.

7. **Call your insurance carrier.** Most homeowner policies require prompt notice of a loss. A brief call to open a claim protects your coverage; you don't need a full damage estimate yet.

## What Not to Do

Some common instincts actually make water damage worse.

- **Don't use a standard household vacuum on standing water.** Most are not designed for liquids and can be damaged or create an electrocution hazard.
- **Don't run central heat to speed drying.** Raising air temperature without controlling humidity can accelerate mold growth. Dehumidification and airflow together dry a structure; heat alone does not.
- **Don't pull up wet flooring immediately without checking what's underneath.** Hardwood floors can sometimes be dried in place if addressed quickly. Pulling them prematurely may be unnecessary and complicates the insurance claim.
- **Don't discard damaged materials before your adjuster has seen them.** Waterlogged drywall, flooring, and insulation are evidence of the scope of loss. Stack removed materials in a dry area and photograph them before any disposal.
- **Don't assume the surface reading tells the whole story.** A wall that feels dry to the touch 12 hours after a leak may still have wet framing or insulation behind it. Moisture meters and thermal imaging cameras reveal what hands can't.

## When to Call a Professional

You can handle a contained spill — a dishwasher overflow that soaked a few square feet of vinyl flooring, caught within the hour. The situations below are a different category:

- **The water source was a sewer line, toilet backup, or any drain.** Contaminated water (Category 2 or Category 3 in industry classification) carries bacteria and pathogens. It requires protective equipment, antimicrobial treatment, and in many cases, disposal of porous materials that absorbed it. This is not a DIY cleanup.
- **The affected area is larger than roughly 10 square feet, or you can't identify the full extent.** If water traveled behind walls or under a subfloor, you need moisture mapping equipment to find the boundaries.
- **The building is older and may contain lead paint or asbestos.** Many Sacramento homes built before 1978 have lead-based paint; homes from earlier decades may have asbestos in floor tiles, insulation, or joint compound. Disturbing these materials during demo requires specific handling protocols.
- **Mold is already visible, or the loss happened more than 24 hours ago.** At that point, remediation protocols apply, not just drying.
- **You're filing an insurance claim.** A professional restoration contractor can document the scope of damage in the format adjusters expect, which often results in a more complete claim settlement.

## What Happens After the First Day

Professional drying typically takes three to five days for a contained loss, longer if structural materials are deeply saturated. The process involves industrial air movers and dehumidifiers running continuously, with moisture readings taken daily to confirm drying progress. Walls may need to be opened to dry cavities behind them — a step that looks alarming but is far less costly than replacing framing damaged by mold.

Once materials reach their target moisture levels, reconstruction begins: new drywall, flooring, paint, and any cabinetry or trim that couldn't be saved. If the loss is covered, your insurance carrier typically pays for both the mitigation (drying) and the rebuild, minus your deductible.

The homeowners who come out of a water loss with the least disruption are almost always the ones who acted in the first few hours — not because they had special knowledge, but because they stopped the spread early and called for help before the damage compounded.
