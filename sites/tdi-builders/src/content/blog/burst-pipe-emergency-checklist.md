If a pipe just burst in your home, skip to the numbered list below — you have minutes, not hours, before water migrates into walls, subfloor, and insulation. If you're reading this before an emergency happens, good. Knowing the steps in advance can mean the difference between a contained cleanup and a full gut-and-rebuild. This checklist walks you through the first 30 minutes, the mistakes that make things worse, the signs you need professional help, and what the recovery process actually looks like after the water stops flowing.

## Immediate Steps: The First 30 Minutes

Work through this list in order. Every minute of active water flow adds square footage to the affected area.

1. **Shut off the main water supply.** In most Sacramento-area homes, the main shutoff is either at the meter box near the curb or on an exterior wall near the front of the house. Turn it clockwise until it stops. If the valve hasn't been turned in years, it may be stiff — use a wrench if needed, but don't force it past resistance.
2. **Turn off electricity to affected areas.** If water is near an outlet, a panel, or any appliance, go to your breaker box and kill power to those circuits before you step into standing water. If you're not sure which breakers cover the wet zone, shut off the main breaker.
3. **Open faucets to drain remaining pressure.** After the main is off, open the lowest faucet in the house — usually a hose bib or a utility sink — to drain residual water from the lines and reduce pressure on the break.
4. **Locate the burst.** Listen for dripping or rushing sounds. If the leak is behind drywall, you'll often see the paint bubbling, a soft spot when you press the wall, or a dark stain spreading from a seam. Wet carpet over a subfloor can feel spongy before it looks visibly wet.
5. **Move valuables and electronics off the floor.** Water travels along the path of least resistance — it will follow floor joists, run under baseboards, and appear in rooms that seem nowhere near the break. Get anything irreplaceable elevated.
6. **Document everything before you touch it.** Take photos and video of standing water, affected walls, ceilings, flooring, and any personal property. Your insurance adjuster will need this, and so will any restoration contractor.
7. **Start removing standing water if you can do so safely.** A wet/dry vacuum, mop, or even towels help — but only if the electricity is off or you're certain the wet area is on a separate circuit. Don't use a household vacuum cleaner.

## What NOT to Do After a Pipe Bursts

Some instinctive responses make the damage significantly worse.

- **Don't use a hair dryer or space heater to dry walls.** Surface heat evaporates moisture into the air and drives it deeper into framing and insulation without actually drying the structural material. Moisture meters will still read elevated days later.
- **Don't assume it's dry because the surface feels dry.** Plaster and drywall can read dry at the surface while the lath, framing, or subfloor behind them stays saturated. Mold can begin colonizing damp organic material within 24 to 48 hours of a water event — often before you'd notice any visible sign.
- **Don't immediately repaint or re-drywall.** Sealing over wet material traps moisture and almost guarantees mold growth. Any repair to walls or ceilings needs to wait until moisture readings confirm the structure is dry.
- **Don't throw away damaged materials before documenting them.** Wet carpet, ruined drywall, and damaged personal property all need to be photographed and itemized for your insurance claim. Disposing of them early can complicate or reduce your payout.
- **Don't delay calling your insurance company.** Most homeowner policies require prompt notice of a loss. Waiting several days to report can create a coverage dispute, even if the damage is clearly covered.

## When to Call a Professional Restoration Contractor

You can handle some of the immediate steps above on your own. What comes after them is where DIY typically falls short.

Call a water damage restoration professional if any of the following apply:

- The water has been sitting for more than an hour before you found it
- The affected area is larger than a small bathroom (roughly 10 square feet of flooring)
- Water has reached walls, subfloor, or ceiling — not just the surface floor
- The source was a supply line rather than a drain (supply lines carry clean water under pressure; the volume of intrusion is typically much higher)
- You see or smell anything that suggests the water contacted sewage
- The home was built before 1980 (Sacramento has a large stock of mid-century homes where older plumbing, lead paint, and asbestos-containing materials may be present in walls and floors)

A certified restoration crew brings equipment that matters here: industrial air movers, desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers, and moisture meters that read behind surfaces rather than just at them. The drying process is measured in days, not hours, and it requires monitoring to confirm the structure actually reaches dry standard — not just feels dry.

For appliance-related failures — a washing machine supply hose, a refrigerator ice-maker line, or a dishwasher connection — the source is often small-diameter tubing under constant pressure. These can run undetected for hours if the appliance is in a closed cabinet or utility room. Appliance leak cleanup follows the same protocol as a burst pipe, but the affected area is frequently concentrated under cabinetry and into the subfloor directly beneath.

## The Recovery Process: What Happens After the Water Stops

Expect the full restoration process to take longer than the emergency phase. Here's what a typical sequence looks like:

**Days 1–2: Extraction and drying setup.** Standing water is extracted, wet materials that can't be dried in place (saturated drywall, carpet padding) are removed, and drying equipment is positioned. Moisture readings are taken at multiple points to establish a baseline.

**Days 2–5: Active drying.** Air movers and dehumidifiers run continuously. A technician returns daily to check moisture readings and adjust equipment placement. The goal is to reach the dry standard for each material type — wood framing, concrete slab, and gypsum board all have different acceptable moisture levels.

**After drying is confirmed: Reconstruction.** Once moisture readings confirm the structure is dry, the rebuild phase begins — new drywall, insulation, flooring, paint, and any finish carpentry that was removed. This is also when a contractor can address the pipe repair itself if it wasn't already handled by a plumber.
