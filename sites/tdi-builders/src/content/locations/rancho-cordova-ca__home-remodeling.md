## Why Rancho Cordova's Housing Stock Shapes Every Remodel

Rancho Cordova's residential neighborhoods span several distinct construction eras, and each era carries its own set of remodeling considerations. Homes built in the 1950s and 1960s — many of them modest ranch-style houses originally constructed to house workers at what is now Mather Airport — frequently have original single-pane aluminum windows, galvanized supply lines, and load-bearing walls that don't show up on any permit record. Opening a wall in one of these homes without probing for structure first is how a bathroom remodel becomes a structural repair.

Homes from the 1970s and 1980s introduce a different set of issues: polybutylene plumbing in some tracts, original HVAC systems undersized for today's insulation standards, and electrical panels that predate arc-fault requirements. If your remodel involves expanding a kitchen footprint or adding a bathroom, the existing infrastructure often needs upgrading before finish work can begin. California's Title 24 energy code also applies to permitted remodels, which means new windows, insulation, and HVAC work must meet current efficiency standards — not the standards from when the house was built.

## Local Note

Rancho Cordova's climate runs hot and dry through summer, with valley heat regularly pushing above 100°F for weeks at a time. That matters for remodeling in a specific way: homes in this area often have attic temperatures that exceed 150°F during peak summer, which accelerates the off-gassing of older insulation materials and can affect adhesive cure times for flooring and tile. We schedule finish work in temperature-sensitive phases during summer projects, and we factor attic ventilation into any remodel that touches the ceiling plane — because in Rancho Cordova's climate, an under-ventilated attic is an ongoing energy and comfort problem, not just a code checkbox.
