## Why Loomis Properties See Elevated Storm Damage Risk

The foothills climate that makes Loomis attractive — warm summers, dramatic skies, mature tree canopy — is also what makes storm season genuinely hazardous for local homes. Placer County's foothill zone sits at the convergence of cold air draining off the Sierra and warm, moisture-laden systems pushing in from the Pacific. That collision produces intense, localized rainfall that flat valley towns rarely see at the same intensity.

The housing stock adds another layer of complexity. Many properties in the Loomis area were built in the 1960s through the 1980s, when roofing underlayment standards were less stringent and attic ventilation details were often minimal. Aged composition shingles lose their granule coating over time, and once a storm lifts even a small section of flashing, water finds the plywood decking quickly. Homes on larger lots with significant tree cover face a compounding risk: a single downed oak limb can breach a roof, block a drainage swale, and introduce debris into crawl spaces in a single event.

Soil conditions in the foothills — a mix of decomposed granite and clay-heavy subsoils — drain unevenly. During a heavy atmospheric river, clay layers saturate and shed water laterally, which can drive moisture against foundation walls and into crawl spaces that stayed dry for years before.

## Our Storm Damage Restoration Process in Loomis

The first priority after any storm loss is stopping active water intrusion before secondary damage compounds the claim. Our crew arrives with tarping materials, board-up supplies, and moisture meters calibrated for both wood-frame and older stucco construction common throughout the Loomis area.

Once the structure is stabilized, we conduct a systematic assessment: roof decking, attic insulation, wall cavities, and subfloor — because water in a foothill home rarely stops at the ceiling. Thermal imaging helps locate moisture that has migrated behind plaster or into wall bays that read dry at the surface. Structural debris from fallen trees or limbs is removed and documented photographically before any repairs begin, because your insurance adjuster will need that record.

Drying equipment — commercial-grade dehumidifiers and air movers — is placed based on the moisture map, not a one-size-fits-all layout. In homes with original hardwood floors or older lath-and-plaster walls, drying timelines are managed carefully; aggressive drying can cause secondary cracking in plaster that is already stressed. We monitor daily and adjust equipment until readings confirm the structure has returned to baseline moisture levels.

Reconstruction — framing, roofing, drywall, finish work — is handled in-house. There is no handoff to a second contractor mid-project.

## Loomis Insurance Coordination

Storm damage claims in Placer County tend to involve multiple line items: roofing, structural framing, interior finishes, and sometimes landscaping features that affected drainage. Carriers vary in how they scope foothill storm losses, and documentation quality at the outset shapes the outcome of the claim.

Our team photographs and measures every affected area before any material is removed, produces a written scope of damage, and communicates directly with your adjuster throughout the process. We are licensed and insured, which most carriers require before authorizing repair work. If a supplement is needed — because hidden damage appears once demo begins — we handle that conversation with the carrier so you are not caught between the insurance timeline and your contractor's schedule.

## Local Note

One pattern that comes up repeatedly in foothill properties is the gap between how a roof looks from the ground after a storm and what is actually happening at the decking level. Loomis and the surrounding Placer County foothill communities have a significant number of homes with T1-11 or board-and-batten siding and shallow-pitch rooflines that were popular in the 1970s and 1980s. These low-slope sections are particularly vulnerable to wind-driven rain infiltration even when no shingles are visibly missing — water enters at the ridge cap or around vent penetrations and travels horizontally along the decking before showing up as a ceiling stain. If your interior damage seems disproportionate to what you can see on the roof, that is the likely explanation, and it requires opening the ceiling to trace the path rather than patching from above.
