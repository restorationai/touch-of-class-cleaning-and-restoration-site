## Building New in Citrus Heights: What the Local Conditions Demand

Citrus Heights is largely built out, which means most new home construction today involves infill lots, teardown-and-rebuild projects, or the occasional undeveloped parcel tucked between established neighborhoods. That context matters. Infill construction in a mature suburban city comes with tighter site constraints — neighboring structures, existing utility laterals that may need to be relocated, and setback requirements that leave less margin for error than a greenfield subdivision would.

The climate adds another layer. The Sacramento Valley's hot, dry summers and mild but wet winters mean that construction scheduling has to account for a relatively short window of ideal framing weather. Concrete pours during peak summer heat require careful curing management to prevent surface cracking. Conversely, the rainy season — typically November through March — can slow grading and foundation work if the schedule isn't planned around it. A builder who hasn't worked this climate learns these lessons expensively; one who has been operating in the greater Sacramento area for decades has already absorbed them.

Soil composition across much of Citrus Heights trends toward expansive clay, particularly in areas that haven't been previously graded. Expansive soils move — they swell when wet and shrink when dry — and that movement can compromise a foundation that wasn't engineered with it in mind. Proper site investigation, compaction testing, and foundation design calibrated to the actual soil report on your specific lot are non-negotiable steps, not optional upgrades.

## Navigating Sacramento County Permits and California Energy Code

New home construction in Citrus Heights falls under Sacramento County's jurisdiction for permitting and inspections. California's Title 24 energy code — one of the most stringent residential energy standards in the country — governs insulation values, window performance, HVAC efficiency, and increasingly, solar readiness requirements. These aren't details to sort out after framing begins; they have to be baked into the design from the start or the plan check process will send the drawings back for revision.

Builders who work primarily outside California sometimes underestimate the documentation burden Title 24 compliance creates. The CF1R and CF2R forms, third-party HERS rater verification, and duct leakage testing are standard parts of a California new home build — not surprises. TDI Builders has been working within California's regulatory environment long enough that compliance is part of the workflow, not a last-minute scramble.
