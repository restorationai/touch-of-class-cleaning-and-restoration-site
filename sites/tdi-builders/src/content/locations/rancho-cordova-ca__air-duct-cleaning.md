Rancho Cordova sits in the Sacramento Valley where summer temperatures routinely push past 100°F, and HVAC systems run hard from May through September without much of a break. That sustained heat load — combined with the fine Sacramento Valley dust that works its way into return vents during dry, windy stretches — means ducts in this city accumulate debris faster than in cooler, more humid climates. If your system has been pushing that same recycled air since last cooling season, it's worth taking a closer look at what's traveling through your vents.

## Why Rancho Cordova Properties See Duct Issues

The Sacramento Valley's dry summers create a particular dust profile: fine particulate from the surrounding agricultural areas and the region's sandy loam soils gets drawn into return air intakes, especially on single-story homes where vents sit low to the ground. When monsoon moisture occasionally spikes in late summer, that dust can compact inside ductwork and create a surface that holds allergens, mold spores, and odors.

Rancho Cordova also has a wide range of housing stock. Neighborhoods developed in the 1950s and 1960s — built out when the city was growing rapidly as a hub for aerospace and government contractors — often have original sheet-metal duct systems with joints sealed by mastic that has dried and cracked over decades. Those gaps allow conditioned air to leak into attic or crawl space before it ever reaches a room, and they also let unconditioned attic air — loaded with insulation fibers and dust — migrate back into the supply side. Newer subdivisions built in the 1990s and 2000s are more likely to have flex duct, which can sag, kink, or collect debris in low spots if it wasn't installed with enough support.

## Our Air Duct Cleaning Process in Rancho Cordova

The process begins with a visual inspection of your supply and return registers, the air handler, and accessible duct runs. For Rancho Cordova homes with attic-mounted air handlers — common in the single-story tract homes throughout the area — we pay particular attention to the plenum connections and the first few feet of supply trunk, where dust tends to settle when the system cycles off.

Cleaning uses negative-pressure equipment: a high-powered vacuum unit attaches to the main trunk line while technicians work branch runs with rotary brush and compressed-air tools, dislodging built-up debris and moving it toward the collection point rather than back into the living space. Supply and return sides are cleaned separately. After mechanical cleaning, we offer air duct sanitization using an EPA-registered antimicrobial treatment applied as a fine mist — particularly useful in homes where musty odors suggest microbial growth on duct liner surfaces.

Dryer vent cleaning is available as an add-on and is worth considering in Rancho Cordova's older housing stock, where original dryer vent runs are sometimes longer than current best practices recommend, or where vents terminate in a side-wall location that has become partially obstructed by landscaping.

## Equipment & Methods We Use for Air Duct Cleaning

The core tool is a truck-mounted or portable negative-air machine capable of creating sufficient static pressure to capture dislodged debris before it re-enters the living space — not a shop vac adapted for duct work. Rotary brush systems are sized to the duct diameter, and technicians change brush heads between supply and return runs to avoid cross-contamination. For fiberglass-lined ductwork, which appears in some Rancho Cordova commercial and multifamily buildings, we use softer brush configurations that clean without abrading the liner surface.

All work is performed by IICRC certified technicians, and TDI Builders, Inc. is licensed and insured. With over 40 years of construction and restoration experience operating out of Sacramento, the team understands the regional building patterns that affect how duct systems were installed and how they age.

## Local Note

Rancho Cordova's hot, dry summers mean that attic temperatures can exceed 150°F on peak days — a condition that accelerates the breakdown of flex duct's inner liner and outer jacket. When we inspect attic duct runs in homes built between roughly 1985 and 2005, we frequently find flex duct that has become brittle at the connection collars, which are the points most likely to separate and allow attic air to infiltrate the supply side. Homeowners who notice a persistent "hot attic" smell from their vents during the first weeks of cooling season should have those collar connections inspected before investing in a full cleaning — a disconnected section will recontaminate a freshly cleaned system almost immediately.
