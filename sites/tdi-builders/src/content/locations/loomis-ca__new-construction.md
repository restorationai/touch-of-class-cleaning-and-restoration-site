Loomis sits in that particular stretch of Placer County where the Sierra Nevada foothills begin to assert themselves — rolling terrain, clay-heavy soils, and a climate that swings from wet, frost-edged winters to dry summers that crack exposed earth. Building a new home here isn't the same exercise as grading a flat Sacramento Valley lot and pouring a slab. The land has opinions, and a builder who doesn't account for them will spend the back half of a project correcting the front half. TDI Builders, Inc. has been doing ground-up residential construction since 1985, and the foothills conditions around Loomis are part of what we plan for before a shovel breaks ground.

## Why Building in Loomis Demands Site-Specific Planning

Placer County's western foothill corridor — the band that includes Loomis and its surrounding unincorporated parcels — sits on a mix of decomposed granite and expansive clay soils. Expansive clay is the detail that changes a foundation design. When the rainy season saturates that soil, it swells; when the dry season bakes it, it contracts. A foundation engineered for flat, stable Sacramento-area soil and transplanted to a Loomis hillside parcel is an invitation to cracking and differential settlement over time.

Soil reports and geotechnical analysis aren't optional steps here — they're the foundation of the foundation. Depending on slope and soil composition, your engineer may specify a post-tension slab, a raised perimeter foundation, or pier-and-grade-beam construction. The choice affects framing timelines, utility rough-in sequencing, and ultimately the long-term performance of the structure. We coordinate with licensed geotechnical engineers and structural engineers early in the design phase so those decisions are made before they become expensive mid-project pivots.

Placer County also enforces its own fire hazard severity zone designations across much of the Loomis area, which affects exterior material selections, vent screening requirements, and defensible space setbacks. These aren't suggestions — they're code-enforced conditions of approval that shape what a new home can look like and how it must be built.

## Our New Home Construction Process in Loomis

Every ground-up build we manage moves through a structured sequence, but the specifics are calibrated to the site, not copied from a template.

**Pre-construction and permitting** begins with a site assessment that accounts for Loomis's topography, access, and utility connections. Placer County's Building Services department handles permit issuance for most Loomis addresses, and the county's review timelines and inspection requirements shape the project schedule from day one. We prepare and submit permit packages, coordinate with the county's plan check process, and track approval status so the project doesn't stall waiting on paperwork.

**Site preparation and foundation work** follows approval. On hillside or sloped parcels common to the Loomis area, grading plans must account for drainage — both to protect the structure and to comply with county grading ordinances that govern how water is directed off a disturbed site. Erosion control measures are required during construction, and we treat them as a genuine site-management priority, not a checkbox.

**Framing, mechanical rough-in, and envelope** proceed in sequence with county inspections at each required phase. We use licensed subcontractors for electrical, plumbing, and HVAC, and we manage the inspection schedule to keep trades coordinated and the project moving.

**Finish work and final inspection** close out the permit and produce a certificate of occupancy. We walk every finish with the homeowner before that final call is made.

## Local Note

One thing that catches builders unfamiliar with this part of Placer County: many Loomis-area parcels are served by private wells and septic systems rather than municipal water and sewer. If you're building on a lot that isn't connected to a public water district, the well permit, drilling, and water quality testing run on a separate track from the building permit — and septic system design requires a percolation test and county Environmental Health approval before the building department will issue a full permit. These aren't insurmountable steps, but they add lead time that needs to be in the schedule from the start. We've navigated this process enough times to know where the delays tend to accumulate and how to front-load the work that prevents them.
