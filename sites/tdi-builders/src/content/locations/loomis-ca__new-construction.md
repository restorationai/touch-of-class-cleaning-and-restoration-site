## What Makes New Home Construction in Loomis Different

Placer County's soil profile in the Loomis area is dominated by expansive clay and decomposed granite — a combination that behaves very differently depending on moisture content. During the wet season, clay-rich soil swells; in the dry summer months, it contracts. A foundation engineered without accounting for that movement will show it within a few years in the form of cracked slabs, sticking doors, and uneven floors. Proper site investigation, soil testing, and engineered foundation design aren't optional steps here — they're the difference between a home that settles gracefully and one that requires costly remediation.

Beyond soil, Loomis is in a Wildland-Urban Interface zone. California's building codes impose specific requirements on homes in WUI areas — ignition-resistant construction materials, ember-resistant vents, Class A roof assemblies — and Placer County enforces them closely. Navigating those requirements from the design phase forward, rather than discovering them at inspection, keeps a project on schedule.

## Our New Home Construction Process in Loomis

Every ground-up build starts with the land itself. Before a shovel breaks soil, the site needs to be evaluated: slope, drainage patterns, utility access, and setback requirements relative to Placer County's parcel rules. Loomis properties on larger rural lots often require private well and septic systems rather than municipal hookups, which adds coordination with county environmental health to the early permitting process.

From there, the process moves through design coordination, plan check submission with Placer County Building Services, grading permits where required, and phased inspections at foundation, framing, rough mechanical, and final. Each inspection gate is a schedule dependency, and knowing the county's typical review timelines — which can vary seasonally — is part of managing a realistic build schedule for clients.

Framing in the Loomis area also means thinking about thermal performance. The climate swings hard: summer afternoons in the foothills routinely exceed 100°F, while winter nights can drop well below freezing. Wall assemblies, attic insulation, and window specifications that meet California's Title 24 energy code while actually performing in that thermal range require deliberate material selection, not just code-minimum compliance.

## Local Note

One thing that catches first-time builders in the Loomis area off guard is the interaction between rural lot sizes and Placer County's grading ordinance. Parcels with any meaningful slope — common in the foothills east of town — often trigger grading permit requirements and, in some cases, a geotechnical report before the county will accept a foundation plan. Builders who don't flag this early can find themselves two months into a project timeline before the actual permit clock starts.
