El Dorado Hills sits at the edge of the Sierra Nevada foothills, where the transition from valley floor to oak-studded hillside creates a building environment unlike anything closer to Sacramento. Expansive soils, steep lot grades, and one of El Dorado County's more active HOA landscapes mean that ground-up residential construction here demands a level of site-specific planning that generic tract builders rarely bring. TDI Builders, Inc. has been doing this kind of work since 1985, and the foothill terrain is not a surprise to us — it's a starting point.

## Why Building in El Dorado Hills Requires Site-Specific Planning

The geology beneath El Dorado Hills is not uniform. Much of the area sits on decomposed granite and clay-heavy soils that behave differently across a single lot — stable in dry months, prone to movement and drainage complications after the winter rains that roll in off the Sierra. A home sited on a ridge-line parcel faces different grading and foundation requirements than one tucked into a lower-elevation cul-de-sac. Get the soils report wrong, or skip the geotechnical step to save time, and you may be correcting drainage problems for years after move-in.

El Dorado County's building department enforces its own permitting process, and new residential construction in the unincorporated areas around El Dorado Hills goes through county review rather than a city building department — a distinction that affects timeline and submittal requirements. Fire-resistive construction standards are also a real consideration here: the area's Wildland-Urban Interface designation means roofing materials, vents, and exterior assemblies are subject to stricter requirements than you'd encounter on a flat Sacramento infill lot.

## Our New Home Construction Process in El Dorado Hills

Every project starts with the land itself. Before a single design line is drawn, we assess the parcel — slope, drainage patterns, utility access, and any easements or setback constraints that El Dorado County has recorded against the property. That site analysis shapes the foundation type, the driveway approach, and where the structure can realistically sit.

From there, the process moves through design coordination, permit submittal to El Dorado County, and a construction sequence built around the local climate calendar. Framing and roofing are scheduled to close in the structure before the wet season arrives; finish work and mechanical rough-ins follow in the drier months. We coordinate directly with PG&E for utility connections and work with the county inspector at each required milestone — foundation, framing, rough mechanical, and final — so there are no surprises at the end of the job.

Because many parcels in this part of the foothills are served by private septic rather than municipal sewer, we also manage the coordination with El Dorado County Environmental Management when a new system or expansion is part of the scope.

## HOA Requirements and Architectural Review in El Dorado Hills

El Dorado Hills has a significant HOA footprint. Developments throughout the area — particularly in the master-planned communities that expanded through the late 1990s and 2000s — maintain Architectural Review Committees that must approve exterior materials, roof pitch, color palettes, and sometimes even driveway materials before a permit application can move forward. Skipping that step or submitting to the county before the HOA signs off is a common source of project delays.

We build the HOA submittal into our pre-construction timeline as a standard step, not an afterthought. That means preparing the elevation drawings, material callouts, and site plans the ARC typically requires, and building in realistic review windows before the county application goes in.

## Local Note

One thing that catches builders unfamiliar with El Dorado Hills off guard: the combination of steep lot grades and clay subsoils means that compaction testing during grading is not just a code checkbox — it's genuinely predictive of how the finished site will drain over the first several winters. Lots that look well-graded in August can pond against foundation walls by January if the compaction work wasn't done in lifts and verified. We treat the soils report and the compaction log as foundational documents, not paperwork to file and forget.
