## Why Citrus Heights Homes Require a Thoughtful Addition Approach

The dominant housing stock in Citrus Heights leans heavily on single-story ranch homes with slab-on-grade foundations. These slabs perform well in Sacramento County's climate, but they present specific challenges when you're extending a footprint. A bump-out addition or a full room addition requires careful attention to where the existing slab ends and new concrete begins, the joint between old and new must be engineered to move together rather than independently, or you'll see cracking at the seam within a few years.

Beyond the foundation, older homes in this area were built under earlier California building codes that permitted narrower hallways, lower ceiling heights, and electrical panels that weren't sized for modern loads. Adding a bedroom, a home office, or a family room often surfaces these legacy conditions, a 100-amp panel that needs upgrading before the addition can be permitted, or framing that doesn't meet current Title 24 energy requirements. TDI Builders has been navigating California's evolving construction codes since 1985, so these discoveries during pre-construction don't stall the project; they get folded into the plan.

Not every neighborhood in Citrus Heights is governed by a homeowners association, but a meaningful portion of the planned communities developed from the 1980s onward do carry CC&Rs that regulate exterior additions. ## Local Note

One pattern that comes up repeatedly in Citrus Heights ranch homes built in the 1960s: the original attic insulation is often compressed fiberglass batts that no longer meet current R-value requirements, and the attic access is sized for the era, sometimes barely large enough for a person to enter. When a room addition raises the roofline or ties into the existing attic space, that's the right moment to address the insulation and access together.
