New home construction in Antelope almost always starts with the same question: what's actually under this lot? Much of Antelope was built out from open rangeland starting in the 1980s and 90s, and the clay-heavy soil that made it good grazing ground is the same soil that makes foundation engineering non-negotiable today. Whether you're building on one of the community's remaining undeveloped parcels or tearing down to rebuild, the ground itself shapes the plan before the framing ever starts.

## Why Antelope Lots Need Engineered Foundations

Sacramento County sits on expansive clay soils that swell when winter rain soaks in and shrink hard during the dry Central Valley summer. That cycle puts real stress on a slab that wasn't designed for it, and it's a bigger factor in Antelope than in older, more established Sacramento neighborhoods because so much of the housing stock here is only a few decades old and still settling into its soil profile. Before we pour anything, we get a geotechnical read on the lot and size the foundation system to the actual soil behavior, not a generic spec.

## Our New Home Construction Process in Antelope

Ground-up work in Antelope runs through Sacramento County's planning and building departments for permitting, grading, and inspections, and we manage that process start to finish rather than handing homeowners a stack of paperwork to chase down themselves. After the soils report and permit set are approved, the sequence is standard but site-adjusted: grading and compaction, foundation, framing, rough mechanical and electrical, insulation, drywall, and finish work, with county inspections at each hold point. We schedule long-lead items like windows and trusses early because supply timing, more than labor, is usually what determines whether a project holds its calendar.

## Antelope HOA Coordination

A good share of Antelope's residential tracts were developed under homeowners association covenants that control exterior materials, roof pitch, setbacks, and sometimes paint palettes. If your lot falls inside one of these communities, the architectural review process runs parallel to county permitting, not after it, and submitting both at once avoids a month of dead time waiting on a committee that only meets periodically. We pull the HOA's design guidelines before we finalize drawings so the plan doesn't get bounced back after it's already been engineered.

## Local note

Antelope's clay soil doesn't just move with the seasons, it moves unevenly across a single lot depending on where the original grading cut or filled. We've seen slabs crack along one edge while the rest of the pad stays flat because the fill depth wasn't consistent from corner to corner. A proper compaction test across the full building pad, not just a sample point, catches this before the foundation goes in rather than after drywall is already cracking.
