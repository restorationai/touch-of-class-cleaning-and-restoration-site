A storm doesn't announce the full extent of its damage when it leaves. The obvious stuff — a cracked window, a torn soffit, shingles scattered across the lawn — gets photographed and filed. What gets missed is the water that followed the wind inside, sitting behind drywall or under roofing felt, quietly setting the conditions for mold within 24 to 48 hours. Storm damage restoration is the work of finding all of it, not just the visible damage, and repairing the structure before secondary damage compounds the loss.

## What storm damage restoration actually involves

Storm events in the Sacramento Valley can arrive as atmospheric river systems that saturate roofs and foundations over days, or as fast-moving wind events that strip flashing, crack stucco, and drive debris through windows in minutes. The work that follows depends heavily on what the storm actually did.

A wind event typically means structural assessment first — roof decking, ridge caps, fascia, and any penetrations where water can enter. A sustained rain event means tracing water intrusion paths from the point of entry down through wall cavities and subfloor assemblies. Hail leaves damage that looks cosmetic on the surface but compromises shingle granule coverage in ways that accelerate wear and void manufacturer warranties. Tree strikes are their own category: a fallen limb on a roof can compromise structural members that aren't visible until the debris is cleared and the decking is probed.

Equipment on a storm job typically includes moisture meters and thermal imaging cameras to map wet building materials, tarping and board-up supplies for immediate weatherproofing, extraction equipment if standing water is present, and industrial drying systems (desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers, high-velocity air movers) when wall cavities or floor assemblies have absorbed water. IICRC-certified technicians follow the S500 standard for water intrusion and the S110 standard for fire and smoke where applicable — both of which apply when a storm causes both wind damage and electrical or fire events.

## Our process

1. **Emergency stabilization.** Before any documentation or scoping, the structure needs to stop taking on additional damage. That means tarping compromised roof sections, boarding broken windows and doors, and extracting any standing water. TDI Builders responds 24/7 — a call at 2 a.m. after a tree comes through a roof gets the same response as a daytime call.

2. **Damage mapping and moisture assessment.** Once the structure is stabilized, the full scope of damage is documented — photographically and with moisture readings at every affected surface. Thermal imaging identifies wet insulation and saturated wall cavities that read dry at the surface. This documentation is the foundation of the insurance claim.

3. **Structural drying and debris removal.** Wet building materials are dried in place where possible, using calibrated drying systems monitored over multiple days. Debris — roofing material, insulation, damaged drywall — is removed and disposed of. Drying logs are maintained throughout, which insurance adjusters require to validate the scope of mitigation.

4. **Scope of work and reconstruction.** With 40+ years of construction and restoration experience, TDI Builders scopes and performs the rebuild — framing repairs, roofing, drywall, insulation, exterior cladding, windows — rather than handing the project to a second contractor. A written scope is produced before reconstruction begins.

5. **Final inspection and closeout.** Moisture readings are taken at closeout to confirm all affected assemblies have returned to normal levels. Documentation is compiled for the insurance carrier.

## What separates a good storm damage response from a bad one

The most common failure in storm damage work is treating the visible damage as the complete picture. A contractor who patches the roof and repaints the water-stained ceiling without checking the wall cavity below the intrusion point leaves saturated framing in place. That framing will support mold growth, and the homeowner won't know until it's a larger remediation project.

A second common failure is inadequate documentation before work begins. Insurance adjusters need photographs of damage in its original condition, moisture readings with date and time stamps, and a written scope that ties each line item to a specific cause of loss. Restoration contractors who skip this step create disputes at the claim stage that delay payment and sometimes leave homeowners holding costs that should have been covered.

What good operators do differently: they probe roof decking before calling it sound, they pull insulation to check the sheathing behind it, they use thermal imaging rather than relying on visual inspection alone, and they produce drying logs that show the progression from wet to dry — not just a final reading.

Typical costs for storm damage restoration vary significantly based on the type of event, the size of the affected area, and how much water intrusion occurred before mitigation began. Most homeowners pay somewhere in the ranges below — these are industry-typical figures, not TDI Builders quotes.

Homeowners insurance typically covers sudden and accidental storm damage — wind, hail, falling trees — subject to your deductible and policy exclusions; gradual water intrusion or pre-existing maintenance issues are generally not covered, which is why thorough documentation of the storm event matters.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Sacramento's storm season runs roughly November through March, driven by Pacific systems that can deliver several inches of rain in 24 hours after months of dry conditions. Roofs, gutters, and flashing that have dried and contracted over summer are often the first to fail when the first heavy rain arrives. The region's older housing stock — much of it built before modern weather-resistant barriers were standard — is particularly vulnerable to wind-driven rain getting behind siding and stucco. The clay soils common in the Sacramento Valley also drain slowly, which means prolonged saturation of foundations and crawlspaces after sustained rain events.
