Smoke doesn't stop at the burn line. After a fire, soot migrates through HVAC ducts, settles into wall cavities, and bonds to surfaces in rooms the flames never touched — and the odor that seems to fade in the first week often resurfaces months later when humidity rises. Rebuilding after a fire means more than replacing what burned. It means finding every surface that absorbed combustion byproducts, treating them correctly, and reconstructing the structure so it's safe, code-compliant, and livable again.

## What fire and smoke damage rebuilding actually involves

Fire losses fall into two overlapping problems: the physical destruction from heat and flame, and the chemical contamination left by smoke. Both have to be resolved before reconstruction begins — and the contamination side is where most corners get cut.

Soot comes in different forms depending on what burned. Protein soot from kitchen fires is nearly invisible but leaves a pungent, greasy film that ordinary cleaning spreads rather than removes. Synthetic soot from burning plastics, wiring insulation, or composite materials is oily, acidic, and corrosive — it will continue etching metal fixtures and discoloring surfaces if it isn't neutralized. Wet soot from a fire that was suppressed quickly with water has its own chemistry. Each type requires a different cleaning agent and technique.

Beyond soot, structural assessment after a fire looks at char depth in framing members, whether engineered lumber (LVL beams, OSB sheathing, I-joists) has delaminated or lost load capacity, and whether masonry or concrete has spalled from thermal shock. Electrical systems in fire-affected zones are treated as compromised until inspected, because heat degrades wire insulation even when the wires themselves don't ignite.

Timeline from loss to move-back varies with the scope of damage. A single-room fire with contained smoke spread might be resolved in three to six weeks. A whole-floor or whole-structure loss with full gut-and-rebuild typically runs three to six months, depending on permit lead times and material availability.

## Our process

1. **Damage assessment and soot characterization.** Before any cleaning or demolition begins, the affected area is documented — photographs, moisture readings, air quality samples where indicated — and the type of soot present is identified. This determines which cleaning protocols apply and informs the written scope of work.

2. **Contents pack-out and protection.** Salvageable contents are inventoried and removed to a clean staging area before demolition dust and cleaning agents can cause secondary damage. Items that can be restored are catalogued; items that cannot are documented for the insurance claim.

3. **Controlled demolition and structural evaluation.** Charred and smoke-saturated materials — drywall, insulation, flooring, cabinetry — are removed to the extent the damage requires. Structural framing is evaluated for char depth and load integrity. Any framing that has lost more than a code-allowable margin of its cross-section is flagged for replacement.

4. **Smoke and odor treatment.** Exposed framing and structural surfaces are cleaned and sealed with an appropriate encapsulant. Thermal fogging or ozone treatment is used to neutralize odor molecules that have penetrated porous materials. HVAC ducts serving the affected zone are cleaned and inspected — smoke travels through ductwork and will re-contaminate a rebuilt space if the system isn't addressed.

5. **Reconstruction and finish work.** Once the structure is clean, dry, and inspected, rebuilding begins: framing repairs, insulation, drywall, electrical and plumbing rough-in, and all finish trades. Work is permitted and inspected per Sacramento-area building department requirements. The goal is a finished space that matches or improves on the pre-loss condition.

## What separates a good fire damage response from a bad one

The most common failure in fire damage rebuilding is treating it like a cosmetic project. Painting over smoke-stained drywall without cleaning and sealing the substrate first traps odor that bleeds back through the new paint within months. Replacing flooring without addressing smoke penetration in the subfloor leaves the same problem one layer down.

A second common mistake is incomplete duct cleaning. Smoke aerosols are small enough to travel the entire duct system during a fire, and a contractor who only cleans the rooms that burned is leaving contamination in the system.

Insurance adjusters on fire claims look for documentation of soot type and cleaning method, because the appropriate method varies and the cost difference is significant. A scope that just says "clean affected surfaces" without specifying the protocol will often be underpaid. Detailed, method-specific documentation — what was cleaned, with what agent, to what standard — protects the claim.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Sacramento's dry summers and the surrounding foothill communities face elevated wildfire risk from June through October, and the smoke from regional fires — even fires that don't directly threaten a structure — can deposit fine particulate inside homes through gaps in the building envelope. After a nearby wildfire event, HVAC filters and attic spaces are worth inspecting even if the structure itself was never threatened. The Valley's low humidity in summer can cause smoke-damaged wood to check and crack more rapidly than in wetter climates, which affects how quickly structural assessment needs to happen after a loss.
