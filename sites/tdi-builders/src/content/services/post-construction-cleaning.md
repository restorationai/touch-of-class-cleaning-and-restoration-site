The contractor has packed up, the dumpster is gone, and the project looks finished — but walk across the floor and you can feel the grit under your shoes. Look up at the light fixtures and you'll see a fine white haze of drywall dust settled on every horizontal surface. Construction and renovation leave behind a category of residue that standard janitorial work isn't equipped to handle: silica dust embedded in grout lines, caulk smears on glass, adhesive overspray on trim, and paint mist on hardware. Post-construction cleaning is the final step that makes a finished project actually livable.

## What post-construction and specialty cleaning actually involves

Post-construction cleaning is not mopping and wiping down counters. It is a systematic removal of construction residue — dust, debris, adhesives, sealers, paint overspray, protective film, and packaging material — from every surface in a newly built or renovated space.

The work typically happens in two or three passes. A rough clean removes bulk debris: scrap lumber, drywall offcuts, packaging foam, and heavy dust accumulation. A final clean addresses every finished surface — windows, cabinetry interiors, light fixtures, HVAC registers, grout joints, and hardware. A touch-up clean, if scheduled after trades return for punch-list items, corrects anything disturbed in the final days before occupancy.

Equipment matters here. HEPA-filtered vacuums are standard because standard shop vacs recirculate fine silica and drywall dust back into the air rather than capturing it. Microfiber systems, non-abrasive glass scrapers, and solvent-specific cleaners for adhesive residue are part of a professional kit. On larger commercial projects, air scrubbers run continuously to reduce airborne particulate before surfaces are wiped.

Timeline depends on scope. A single-room bathroom remodel may need four to six hours of final clean. A full gut-renovation of a 2,000-square-foot home typically requires one to two full days of crew time across rough and final passes. New commercial construction is scoped by square footage and finish complexity.

## Our process

1. **Pre-clean walkthrough and surface inventory.** Before a crew touches anything, a lead technician walks the space with the general contractor or property owner to identify sensitive finishes — polished concrete, natural stone, specialty glass coatings, custom cabinetry — that require specific products or techniques. Damage from the wrong cleaner on a sealed travertine floor or a low-e window coating is expensive to correct.

2. **Rough clean and debris removal.** All bulk construction waste — scrap material, packaging, tape, protective sheeting — is removed and disposed of properly. HVAC registers are temporarily sealed or cleaned to prevent dust from recirculating once the system runs.

3. **Detail clean from ceiling to floor.** Work proceeds top-down: light fixtures, ceiling fans, and smoke detectors first; then walls, windows, and cabinetry; then countertops, appliances, and plumbing fixtures; floors last. Window glass is scraped of paint and sticker adhesive, then cleaned with appropriate glass cleaner. Cabinet interiors are vacuumed and wiped. Grout lines are detailed with appropriate brushes.

4. **Floor care by surface type.** Hardwood, LVP, tile, polished concrete, and carpet each require a different approach. New hardwood may need only dust mopping and a pH-neutral wipe; tile grout may need a light scrub to remove grout haze. Carpet installed before other trades finished gets a HEPA vacuum pass and may require spot treatment for adhesive or paint transfer.

5. **Final inspection and sign-off.** A walkthrough with the client or GC confirms every surface meets the agreed standard before the crew leaves. Any items flagged during inspection are corrected on-site.

## What separates a good post-construction clean from a bad one

The most common failure is treating post-construction cleaning like routine janitorial work. A standard mop and bucket drags drywall slurry across a new tile floor and leaves a haze that requires re-cleaning. Dry dusting with cotton cloths on wood surfaces can scratch a finish that hasn't fully cured.

Silica dust is the detail most crews underestimate. Fine drywall and concrete dust settles into HVAC systems, inside cabinetry, and on top of door frames. If it isn't captured with HEPA filtration before the system runs, it recirculates for weeks and coats every surface the homeowner just cleaned.

On renovation projects in older Sacramento housing stock — homes built before the 1980s are common in neighborhoods like Land Park, Curtis Park, and East Sacramento — lead paint disturbance during demolition is a real consideration. Work on pre-1978 surfaces may require lead-safe practices under EPA RRP guidelines. TDI Builders, Inc. is licensed and insured, and our team is equipped to identify when those protocols apply.

For commercial projects, general contractors often need a certificate of completion for the cleaning scope before a certificate of occupancy is issued. Documenting the clean with photos and a signed inspection sheet protects everyone.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Sacramento's dry summers mean construction seasons run long, and many renovation projects wrap up in the fall when temperatures drop and windows can finally be opened for ventilation during cleaning. That same dry heat, however, means drywall dust becomes airborne easily and travels further through an open floor plan — HEPA filtration during the clean matters more in a dry climate than in a humid one.

The Valley's agricultural dust and pollen load also means that HVAC systems on a newly cleaned property can accumulate particulate quickly if registers weren't properly sealed during construction. Confirming that the HVAC filter was replaced and registers were cleaned is a standard part of a thorough post-construction clean in this region.
