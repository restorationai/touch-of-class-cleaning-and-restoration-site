The fire is out — but the damage isn't done. Smoke keeps penetrating porous materials for days after flames are extinguished, soot begins etching metal surfaces within hours, and the acidic residue left by burning synthetics can permanently stain walls, corrode fixtures, and embed itself in HVAC ductwork before a crew ever arrives. Fire damage restoration is a race against secondary destruction, and the window to reverse it is shorter than most homeowners realize.

## What fire damage restoration actually involves

Fire losses are chemically complex in a way that water damage isn't. A structure fire produces multiple soot types simultaneously — wet, oily residue from burning plastics and synthetics; dry, powdery char from wood and paper; and protein residue from food or organic material that leaves almost no visible trace but generates an overwhelming odor. Each type responds to different cleaning chemistry, and misidentifying them is one of the most common reasons a restoration job fails.

Beyond surfaces, smoke migrates. It follows air currents, infiltrates wall cavities, settles into insulation, and coats the inside of ductwork. A room two doors from the fire origin can test positive for smoke contamination even when it looks clean. Thermal imaging and air quality sampling are how technicians find what the eye misses.

Structural assessment runs parallel to cleaning. Fire weakens framing members, can compromise load-bearing walls, and may damage electrical systems in ways that aren't visible from the surface. Restoration work that skips a structural evaluation — or defers it — creates a liability that shows up months later.

Timeline: emergency board-up and stabilization happen same-day. Soot and odor mitigation typically spans several days to two weeks depending on fire size and material involvement. Reconstruction, if needed, follows once the structure is confirmed dry and clean.

## Our process

1. **Emergency stabilization and safety assessment** — Before any cleaning begins, the structure is evaluated for electrical hazards, compromised framing, and unsafe air quality. Openings are boarded or tarped to prevent weather intrusion and unauthorized entry. Utilities are confirmed safe or isolated.

2. **Soot characterization and surface pre-testing** — Technicians identify soot types present in each area of the structure and test cleaning agents on inconspicuous surfaces before committing to a method. Wet-sponge cleaning, dry-chemical sponges, and solvent-based products are not interchangeable — using the wrong one can drive soot deeper into drywall or permanently set a stain.

3. **Contents pack-out and inventory** — Salvageable contents are documented, inventoried, and moved to a controlled environment for cleaning. Electronics, textiles, documents, and artwork each require different restoration methods. Items that cannot be restored are catalogued for insurance purposes before disposal.

4. **Structural cleaning, thermal fogging, and ozone treatment** — Hard surfaces are cleaned using chemistry matched to the soot type present. Odor neutralization goes beyond surface treatment: thermal fogging disperses a deodorizing agent that follows the same pathways smoke traveled, reaching voids and cavities that manual cleaning cannot. For severe odor cases, ozone treatment is used in unoccupied spaces to oxidize residual smoke compounds at the molecular level.

5. **Clearance verification and reconstruction scope** — Air quality and surface testing confirm that contamination levels are within acceptable limits before reconstruction begins. If structural repairs are needed — framing, drywall, insulation, electrical, or finish work — a written scope is prepared and permitted work proceeds through licensed subcontractors.

## What separates a good fire damage response from a bad one

The most common failure in fire restoration is treating it like a cleaning job rather than a chemical remediation. Crews that apply all-purpose cleaners to protein soot, skip duct cleaning, or close up wall cavities before verifying odor levels are setting the homeowner up for a callback — or a failed air quality test months later.

Insurance adjusters look for documentation at every stage: pre-cleaning photographs, soot type notes, cleaning product logs, contents inventories, and post-cleaning verification. A restoration contractor who doesn't produce this documentation creates gaps in the claim record that can delay or reduce the payout. IICRC-certified technicians are trained to generate this documentation as a standard part of the job, not as an afterthought.

Another missed step: HVAC systems. Smoke circulates through ductwork during and after a fire. Restoring a structure without cleaning the duct system means the first time the heat or AC runs, smoke odor redistributes through every room. Sacramento's Central Valley summers make air conditioning non-optional — duct cleaning is not a line item to value-engineer out.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Sacramento's dry summers and the surrounding foothills create a fire season that runs roughly May through November, though the risk extends year-round with the right wind conditions. Homes in the wildland-urban interface — Elk Grove's eastern edges, the Folsom foothills, areas near the American River Parkway — face a distinct fire profile from urban structure fires: ash and ember intrusion, exterior char with heavy smoke infiltration, and HVAC systems that pulled outside air during the event. These losses often look less dramatic than a kitchen fire but involve contamination across a much larger area of the home.

Older Sacramento housing stock — bungalows and Craftsman-era homes in Curtis Park, Land Park, and East Sacramento — frequently has original plaster walls, knob-and-tube wiring, and uninsulated wall cavities. Smoke penetrates and migrates differently in these structures than in modern framed construction, and cleaning protocols need to account for that.
