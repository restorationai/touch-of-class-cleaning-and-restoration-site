When a fire chars the kitchen or a burst pipe soaks the living room, the structure gets the headlines — but it's the contents that carry the memories and the dollar value. A dining table warped by standing water, a wool rug saturated with smoke odor, a box of photographs coated in fine soot: each one has a narrow recovery window measured in hours and days, not weeks. Contents restoration and storage is the discipline of intercepting that clock — inventorying, packing, cleaning, and storing your belongings while the structure is being rebuilt around them.

## What Contents Restoration and Storage actually involves

Contents work begins the moment a pack-out crew arrives on-site. Every item that can be moved is photographed in place, barcoded, and logged into a digital inventory before a single box is sealed. That documentation trail is not optional — it is the foundation of your insurance claim and your protection against disputes over what was present and what condition it was in.

From there, items are triaged into three categories: restorable, possibly restorable, and non-restorable. Restorable contents go to a climate-controlled contents cleaning facility. Possibly restorable items are evaluated under better lighting and with the right equipment — ultrasonic cleaning tanks for hard goods, ozone or hydroxyl chambers for smoke-saturated soft goods, freeze-drying units for water-damaged documents and photographs. Non-restorable items are documented thoroughly before disposal, because that documentation is what the adjuster uses to calculate replacement cost.

Storage is not simply stacking boxes in a warehouse. Contents should be held in a facility with controlled temperature and humidity, off the ground, and secured against secondary damage. The goal is to hand your belongings back in the same condition they left — or better.

## Our process

1. **On-site inventory and photo documentation.** Every item is photographed in its original location, assigned a unique identifier, and entered into a line-item inventory. This creates the evidentiary record that supports your insurance claim from day one.

2. **Condition triage and pack-out.** Items are sorted by material type and damage category — fabric versus hard goods, wet versus smoke-affected, fragile versus stable — and packed accordingly. Wet items are separated immediately to prevent cross-contamination and secondary mold growth during transit.

3. **Specialty cleaning and deodorization.** Hard surfaces go through ultrasonic cleaning or hand-cleaning with appropriate chemistry matched to the material. Textiles and upholstered furniture are treated for smoke odor using hydroxyl or ozone technology. Electronics are evaluated by a qualified technician before any cleaning attempt. Documents and photographs with water damage may be candidates for freeze-drying, which halts deterioration and allows controlled drying without further distortion.

4. **Climate-controlled storage.** Cleaned and dried contents are stored in a secured, climate-controlled facility until the structure is ready for move-back. Items remain inventoried and accessible for adjuster inspection throughout the process.

5. **Inventoried move-back and final walkthrough.** When the rebuild is complete, contents are returned, unpacked, and checked against the original inventory. Any item that did not survive the cleaning process is already documented for claim purposes.

## What separates a good contents response from a bad one

The most common failure in contents work is speed without documentation. A crew that packs a house in four hours without photographing items in place creates a claim dispute waiting to happen. Adjusters require pre-loss condition evidence; without it, depreciation schedules favor the carrier.

A second common problem is using the wrong cleaning chemistry on the wrong material. Protein soot from a kitchen fire — the kind that comes from burning food and grease — is oily, dense, and bonds to surfaces differently than the dry, powdery soot from burning synthetic materials. Applying a dry-cleaning sponge to a protein-soot-coated surface smears the residue deeper. Experienced contents technicians characterize the soot type before selecting a method.

Water-damaged contents present a different trap: items that appear dry at the surface but retain moisture in joints, stuffing, or paper cores. A mattress, a hardcover book, a wood-framed painting — each holds water in ways that a surface reading misses. Releasing those items back to a repaired home without confirming they are fully dry reintroduces a moisture source into a freshly dried structure.

Finally, electronics require a separate evaluation track. Smoke residue is conductive. A television or appliance that powers on after a fire is not necessarily safe to return to service — corrosive residue on circuit boards can cause failure weeks later. A qualified electronics evaluation, documented in the claim file, protects both the homeowner and the carrier.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Sacramento's dry summers create a specific post-fire contents challenge: smoke residue and ash that have baked onto surfaces in 95-degree heat bond more aggressively than residue in cooler, humid conditions. Contents that sit in a hot, closed structure for even a day or two before pack-out are measurably harder to restore. In winter, the Sacramento Valley's tule fog and overnight condensation can accelerate mold colonization on water-damaged contents that are not packed out promptly — mold can begin colonizing porous materials within 24 to 48 hours of saturation under the right humidity conditions.
