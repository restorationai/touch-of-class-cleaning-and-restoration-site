A broken window or a hole in your roof doesn't wait for business hours. Within hours of a fire, storm, or forced entry, rain, wind, and opportunistic theft can turn a contained loss into a far larger one. Emergency board-up and tarping is the work that stops the clock on secondary damage — sealing openings before the next weather event, before vandals notice an unguarded property, and before your insurance adjuster walks in to find a loss that has grown well beyond the original incident.

## What emergency board-up and tarping actually involves

Board-up and tarping is structural first aid. On the board-up side, that means measuring and cutting plywood panels — typically ¾-inch CDX — to cover broken windows, compromised door frames, garage openings, and any wall breach left by fire or impact. Panels are secured with screws or lag bolts into the surrounding framing, not just taped to the exterior, so they hold against wind load and resist removal.

Roof tarping is a different discipline. A tarp laid flat over a hole does almost nothing; water pools, the tarp shifts, and the opening stays exposed at the edges. Proper tarping involves running the material over the ridge line, securing it with weighted battens or screw-down strips, and extending coverage well past the damaged area so wind-driven rain cannot wick underneath. On a steep-pitch roof or after a fire that has weakened the decking, this work requires fall-protection equipment and an experienced crew that understands compromised structural loads.

Timeline matters here. Most insurers and restoration standards recognize that secondary damage — water intrusion, additional smoke migration, theft of copper or fixtures — that occurs after a loss event but before reasonable protective measures are in place can complicate or reduce a claim. Getting openings sealed the same day as the loss is not just good practice; it is often a condition of coverage.

## Our process

1. **Damage assessment and hazard check.** Before any material goes up, the crew walks the perimeter and interior (where safe) to identify every opening, assess roof load and structural stability, and flag hazards like live utilities, weakened floors, or residual smoke conditions. This step determines what materials are needed and in what sequence.

2. **Measurement and material staging.** Openings are measured precisely. Plywood is cut on-site to fit — oversized panels that flex or gap at the edges provide poor protection and photograph poorly for insurance documentation. Tarps are sized to the roof section, not just the visible hole.

3. **Board-up installation.** Panels are installed from the interior where possible to maximize holding strength and minimize exterior damage to siding or brick. Hardware is chosen based on substrate — concrete anchors for masonry, structural screws for wood framing.

4. **Roof tarping and ridge-line anchoring.** Tarps are run over the peak and secured with battens screwed through the tarp into undamaged decking. Edges are sealed or weighted to prevent wind uplift. On roofs with fire-damaged decking, crew members work from ladders or use roof jacks rather than walking the field.

5. **Photo documentation for the insurance file.** Every opening is photographed before and after. Measurements, materials used, and installation method are recorded in writing. This documentation package is handed directly to the homeowner or adjuster and supports the claim for protective-measures coverage.

## What separates a good board-up response from a bad one

The most common failure is speed without thoroughness. A crew that nails a single sheet over a broken window and leaves misses the attic vent that is now open to weather, the door frame that is racked and no longer closes, or the garage side door that was forced during a break-in. Every opening needs to be identified, not just the obvious one.

A second common problem is inadequate roof tarping. Tarps that are not run over the ridge line, not anchored with battens, or not extended past the damaged area will shift or lift in the first wind event. Insurance adjusters see this regularly — a tarp that failed overnight means a second water-intrusion event on top of the original loss, and that conversation with the carrier is difficult.

On fire losses specifically, board-up crews need to account for the fact that fire weakens roof decking, rafters, and wall framing in ways that are not always visible from the exterior. Walking a fire-damaged roof without assessing structural integrity first is a safety failure, not just a liability one. Experienced crews treat every fire-damaged roof as compromised until they have confirmed otherwise.

Insurance adjusters also look for documentation quality. A claim for protective measures that arrives with no photos, no material list, and no written scope is harder to approve than one with a complete package. Documentation is part of the work, not an afterthought.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Sacramento's dry summers and wet winters create two distinct board-up seasons. From roughly November through March, the Central Valley sees the bulk of its rainfall, and an unprotected roof opening during an atmospheric river event can admit hundreds of gallons of water in a single night. Response time is most critical during this window.

Summer brings a different risk: the fire season across the foothills and valley edges means fire-damaged structures are most common from June through October. Post-fire board-up in this period also needs to account for high temperatures that accelerate mold colonization in any moisture that enters before the structure is sealed — another reason same-day response matters.
