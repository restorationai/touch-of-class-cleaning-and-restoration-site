Building a house from the ground up means making hundreds of decisions before a single nail is driven — lot grading, foundation type, framing system, mechanical rough-ins, insulation strategy, finish sequencing — and a misstep at any stage can cost months and tens of thousands of dollars to correct. The difference between a home that performs well for decades and one that develops chronic moisture, structural, or code problems usually comes down to what happened in the first few phases, before the walls were closed. TDI Builders, Inc. has been managing that complexity across the Sacramento region for more than 40 years.

## What new home construction actually involves

Ground-up residential construction is a sequenced, permit-driven process. Every phase depends on the one before it: the slab cannot be poured until the soil is compacted and inspected; framing cannot begin until the foundation cures; mechanical rough-ins must be inspected before insulation goes in; drywall cannot be hung until framing, plumbing, electrical, and HVAC have all passed rough inspection.

In the Sacramento Valley, that sequencing is shaped by local conditions. The region's expansive clay soils — common across much of the valley floor and the surrounding foothills — require engineered foundations designed specifically for soil movement. Summer heat regularly exceeds 100°F, which affects concrete cure schedules, framing moisture content, and the performance of building envelope systems if they are not specified correctly. Winter rains, though seasonal, can saturate unprotected framing and subfloor assemblies quickly if a project is not properly staged and covered.

A well-managed new home build in this region typically runs from pre-construction planning through final inspection over a timeline that varies with design complexity, lot conditions, and current permit-office workload — but the quality of coordination at each phase is what determines whether that timeline holds.

## Our process

1. **Pre-construction planning and site evaluation.** Before permits are pulled, the lot is evaluated for drainage, soil conditions, utility access, and any grading requirements. Architectural plans are reviewed for constructability, and a detailed scope of work is developed so that every subcontractor and material order is coordinated before mobilization.

2. **Permitting and approvals.** Building permits for new residential construction in Sacramento County and the surrounding jurisdictions require plan-check review by the local building department. TDI Builders manages the permit application, responds to plan-check comments, and tracks approval status so the project can move to groundbreaking without unnecessary delays.

3. **Foundation and framing.** Site prep, grading, and foundation work establish the geometry everything else depends on. For Sacramento-area soils, this often means post-tension or engineered slab systems. Once the foundation passes inspection, framing proceeds — walls, floor systems, roof structure — followed by sheathing and weather-resistant barrier installation before any mechanical trades begin their rough-in work.

4. **Mechanical rough-ins, insulation, and inspections.** Plumbing, electrical, and HVAC are roughed in and inspected before any wall cavities are closed. Insulation is installed to meet California's Title 24 energy code requirements, which are among the most stringent in the country and directly affect long-term utility costs. Each phase is inspected and signed off before the next begins.

5. **Finish work, final inspections, and certificate of occupancy.** Drywall, interior finishes, cabinetry, flooring, fixtures, and exterior work are completed in sequence. Final inspections cover structural, mechanical, electrical, and life-safety systems. The project is not complete until the certificate of occupancy is issued and the home is delivered with all documentation, warranties, and as-built records in hand.

## What separates a good build from a costly one

The most common problems in new home construction are not dramatic failures — they are quiet ones that don't surface until after move-in. Improperly compacted subgrade leads to slab settlement. Framing lumber installed above acceptable moisture content traps water vapor behind drywall. Flashing details at windows, roof penetrations, and wall-to-deck transitions that look fine at inspection can allow bulk water intrusion for years before it becomes visible.

Experienced builders manage these risks through sequencing discipline and inspection culture — not rushing a phase to stay on schedule, not closing walls until moisture readings confirm the framing is within spec, and not treating inspections as a formality. In California, Title 24 energy compliance is not optional, and errors in the building envelope or mechanical system design can require costly corrections at final inspection or trigger issues at resale.

Subcontractor coordination is where many projects lose time and money. When trades are not properly scheduled and their work is not inspected before the next phase begins, problems get buried. A general contractor's job is to prevent that — not just to manage a schedule, but to own the quality of every phase.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Sacramento's climate creates specific construction windows worth planning around. Concrete flatwork and foundation pours are best scheduled outside the peak summer heat to manage cure rates; late spring and early fall are generally optimal. Winter rains — typically November through March — require active site drainage management and temporary weatherproofing of open framing. Lumber delivered during the wet season should be moisture-tested before installation, and framing should be allowed to dry to acceptable levels before insulation and drywall begin. Building in the foothills east of Sacramento introduces additional considerations: steeper grades, different soil profiles, and in some areas, defensible-space and fire-resistant construction requirements that affect material selection and exterior detailing.
