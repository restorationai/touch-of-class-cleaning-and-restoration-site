## What room additions and home additions actually involve

A room addition is a permanent structural expansion of your existing home — new foundation, new framing, new roof tie-in, new exterior cladding, and interior finishes that match or complement what's already there. That's meaningfully different from finishing a basement or converting a garage. You're breaking through an exterior wall, extending the footprint of the house, and tying new construction into an existing structure that may be decades old.

In Sacramento, that typically means working with wood-frame construction on slab or raised foundations, navigating City of Sacramento or county permitting, and accounting for the Central Valley's climate swings — 100°F summers that demand proper insulation and HVAC sizing, and wet winters where drainage and waterproofing details matter at the foundation level. Additions range from a modest bump-out that expands a kitchen by 120 square feet to a full second-story addition that doubles the home's livable area. The scope determines the timeline: smaller single-room additions often run 8–14 weeks from permit approval to punch list; larger multi-room or second-story projects can run 4–6 months.

## Our process

1. **Design and scope development.** Before any permit application, we walk the site with you, assess the existing structure (foundation capacity, roof pitch, load-bearing wall locations, utility routing), and develop a written scope of work. For second-story additions, this includes a structural assessment of whether the existing foundation and framing can carry the new load — a step that gets skipped by less thorough operators and causes expensive surprises mid-project.

2. **Permitting and plan approval.** We prepare and submit plans to the applicable jurisdiction — City of Sacramento, Sacramento County, or the relevant municipality — and manage the back-and-forth with the building department. Permit timelines vary; we build realistic schedules that account for plan check cycles rather than promising a start date before approvals are in hand.

3. **Site preparation and foundation work.** Once permits are issued, we excavate and pour the new foundation, tie it into the existing slab or stem wall, and waterproof below-grade surfaces where required. This phase also includes any utility extensions — electrical panels, gas lines, plumbing rough-ins — that need to be stubbed in before framing begins.

4. **Framing, roof tie-in, and weathertight envelope.** Framing goes up, the roof is extended or reconfigured to cover the new space, and sheathing, housewrap, windows, and exterior doors are installed so the addition is weathertight before interior work begins. The roof tie-in is one of the most detail-sensitive phases — a poorly flashed valley or ridge connection is where water intrusion problems start.

5. **MEP rough-ins, insulation, drywall, and finishes.** Mechanical, electrical, and plumbing systems are roughed in and inspected, insulation is installed to current Title 24 energy code requirements, and drywall, paint, flooring, trim, and fixtures bring the space to finished condition. Final inspections close the permit.

## What separates a good addition from a problem project

The most common failure point in room additions isn't the finish work — it's what happens at the connections. Where new framing meets old framing, where the new roof ties into the existing roof, where new concrete meets old concrete: these are the joints that leak, settle, or crack if they're not detailed correctly.

A second common issue is undersized or improperly integrated HVAC. Adding 400 square feet to a house and extending one duct from the existing system rarely works. A proper addition includes a Manual J load calculation and, often, a dedicated mini-split or zone extension sized for the new space.

Permitting is not optional, and skipping it creates real problems at resale. Unpermitted additions surface in title searches and appraisals, and buyers' lenders routinely require them to be legalized or removed. Every addition TDI Builders, Inc. builds is permitted and inspected through final.

Finally, structural assessment before a second-story addition is non-negotiable. Adding a full floor to a home built in the 1960s or 1970s requires verifying that the existing foundation, mudsill, and wall framing meet current load requirements — or engineering the reinforcements before framing begins.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Sacramento's building season is long compared to most of California — mild winters mean framing and exterior work can proceed through December and January in most years. That said, the rainy season (roughly November through March) creates real risk during the window between demolishing an exterior wall and getting the new addition weathertight. Scheduling that transition during a dry stretch, and having a plan for temporary weather protection if rain arrives early, is part of responsible project management here.

Summer heat affects concrete curing schedules and worker productivity during exterior phases. Projects that begin foundation work in June or July need to account for both.
