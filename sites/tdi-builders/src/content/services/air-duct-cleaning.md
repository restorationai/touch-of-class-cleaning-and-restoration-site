When you run your HVAC system and notice a faint musty smell, a film of dust resettling on surfaces within days of cleaning, or family members sneezing more than usual indoors, the ductwork itself is often the source. Over years of operation, Sacramento's forced-air systems accumulate dust, pollen, rodent debris, and in some cases mold spores — all of it recirculated every time the system kicks on. Air duct cleaning removes that buildup at the source, not just at the filter.

## What air duct cleaning actually involves

Duct cleaning is not a shop-vac job. Professional HVAC duct cleaning uses truck-mounted or high-powered portable negative-air machines that pull 4,000–8,000 CFM of suction through the entire duct system, creating negative pressure so loosened debris travels toward the collection unit rather than back into living spaces. Rotating brush systems or pneumatic whips agitate buildup from duct walls before it's extracted. Supply registers, return grilles, the air handler cabinet, evaporator coil housing, and blower compartment are all addressed — not just the visible register openings.

For Sacramento homes, which skew heavily toward single-story ranch construction from the 1950s through 1980s with sheet-metal duct runs in unconditioned attic spaces, technicians also inspect for disconnected duct sections, pest intrusion points, and insulation degradation that can introduce fiberglass particulate into the airstream. Dryer vent cleaning is often performed at the same visit, since lint-clogged dryer vents are a leading cause of residential fires and the access point is already staged.

A typical residential duct cleaning visit runs two to four hours depending on system size and the degree of contamination found.

## Our process

1. **System inspection and pre-cleaning assessment.** Before any equipment is connected, technicians photograph register conditions, check static pressure, and identify any duct damage, disconnections, or mold-suspect areas. This establishes a baseline and determines whether sanitization will be needed after mechanical cleaning.

2. **Negative-pressure setup.** A high-CFM collection unit is connected directly to the main trunk line or a large return plenum. All supply and return registers are sealed with covers so the entire system operates under negative pressure — debris moves toward the machine, not your furniture.

3. **Mechanical agitation and extraction.** Rotating brushes or compressed-air whips are run through each duct branch from the register back toward the trunk. Dislodged material is captured by the negative-pressure unit. The air handler cabinet, blower wheel, and evaporator coil housing are cleaned separately with appropriate tools.

4. **Air duct sanitization (when indicated).** If microbial growth, rodent contamination, or persistent odor is confirmed, an EPA-registered antimicrobial agent is fogged or applied by hand to duct interior surfaces. This step is not automatic — it's applied when the inspection warrants it, not as an upsell on every job.

5. **Post-cleaning verification and documentation.** Registers are re-photographed, airflow at each supply register is checked, and the collection unit filter is shown to the homeowner as evidence of what was removed. Any structural duct issues found during the job are documented in writing.

## What separates a good duct cleaning response from a bad one

The most common shortcut in this industry is skipping negative pressure entirely — running a brush into a register with a shop vacuum held nearby. Without a sealed negative-pressure system, agitation simply redistributes debris into the living space. A second common failure is cleaning only the accessible register openings while leaving the air handler cabinet, blower wheel, and coil housing untouched. Those components harbor the densest contamination and are the first place mold establishes when there's any moisture in the system.

On sanitization, the mistake runs in both directions: some operators skip it when microbial contamination is genuinely present; others apply it on every job regardless of findings, which adds cost without benefit. Proper practice is to confirm the need visually or with surface sampling before applying any antimicrobial.

For insurance-related claims — typically triggered when duct contamination follows a water loss or fire — adjusters look for pre- and post-cleaning photographs, documentation of what was found in the air handler, and a written scope that distinguishes mechanical cleaning from sanitization line items. Vague invoices slow claims.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Sacramento's long, dry summers push HVAC systems to run for months without interruption, accelerating dust and debris accumulation in duct runs. Fall is when that buildup is at its peak — and when wildfire smoke events can deposit fine particulate directly into return air intakes if systems are running during an air quality alert. Homes with whole-house fans, common in older Sacramento neighborhoods, pull unfiltered attic air through the living space and can introduce insulation fibers and rodent debris into duct systems over time. If your system ran during a Spare the Air event or a nearby fire, a post-event inspection is worth scheduling before the heating season begins.
