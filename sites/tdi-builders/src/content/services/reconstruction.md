After the water is extracted, the fire is out, or the mold is remediated, you're left staring at what remains — missing drywall, charred framing, buckled subfloor, or a ceiling that no longer exists. Mitigation stops the damage; reconstruction is what makes the space livable again. That gap between a dried-out shell and a finished room is where projects stall, budgets balloon, and homeowners discover their contractor wasn't equipped to handle both sides of a loss. TDI Builders, Inc. bridges that gap with 40+ years of construction and restoration experience, handling post-damage reconstruction from structural framing through final finishes.

## What reconstruction services actually involve

Post-damage rebuilding is not the same as a standard remodel. The work begins inside walls and under floors — places a general contractor might never look — and has to account for what the damage did to the structure, not just what's visibly missing.

Depending on the loss, reconstruction can mean sistering compromised floor joists, replacing smoke-saturated insulation, re-sheathing exterior walls, or rebuilding a load-bearing partition that was removed during emergency mitigation. It can also mean coordinating with mechanical trades to restore HVAC ductwork, electrical circuits, or plumbing runs that were damaged or deliberately cut during the remediation phase.

Timeline varies significantly by scope. A single room with drywall and paint work can move from permitted scope to final walk-through in two to four weeks. A multi-room structural reconstruction — particularly one involving roof framing, exterior sheathing, or load-bearing walls — typically runs eight to sixteen weeks depending on permit turnaround, material lead times, and inspection scheduling. Sacramento's building department processing times are a real variable here; scopes that require plan check add time that has nothing to do with the crew.

## Our process

**1. Scope of work and documentation**
Before a nail goes in, every affected area is documented — photographs, measurements, material specifications, and a written line-item scope. This document is the backbone of the insurance claim and the construction contract. Vague scopes are where disputes begin; a detailed scope protects both the homeowner and the carrier.

**2. Permitting and code compliance**
Any structural work, electrical, plumbing, or mechanical replacement in Sacramento requires permits. TDI Builders, Inc. manages the permit application, coordinates plan check when required, and schedules inspections at each phase. Work done without permits can void insurance coverage and create title problems when the property sells.

**3. Structural and rough-in work**
Framing, sheathing, blocking, and any load-bearing repairs come first. Once the structure is sound and inspected, mechanical trades — electrical, plumbing, HVAC — complete their rough-in work before walls are closed. Skipping or rushing this sequence is one of the most common mistakes in post-disaster rebuilding, and it's expensive to undo.

**4. Insulation, drywall, and moisture barrier**
In a post-water or post-fire loss, insulation is almost always replaced rather than dried in place. New vapor barriers and insulation are installed to current code before drywall goes up. This is also the stage where any remaining moisture readings are confirmed — drywall should never close over materials that haven't reached equilibrium moisture content.

**5. Finish work and final inspection**
Taping, texturing, paint, flooring, trim, cabinetry, and fixture reinstallation bring the space back to pre-loss condition — or better, when the homeowner chooses to upgrade during the rebuild. A final inspection by the building department closes the permit and documents the work for the insurance file.

## What separates a good reconstruction response from a bad one

The most common failure point in fire damage reconstruction and post-water rebuilding is closing walls before the structure is genuinely dry and inspected. Moisture trapped behind new drywall creates mold within days in Sacramento's warmer months — and that secondary loss is rarely covered the same way the original claim was.

A second failure is scope creep in the wrong direction: contractors who underscope to win the bid and then present change orders once walls are open. A thorough initial scope, including exploratory demolition where necessary, prevents this. Insurance adjusters are trained to look for supplemental claims that should have been in the original scope — a pattern that slows payment and strains the homeowner's relationship with their carrier.

Permit avoidance is the third and most legally consequential mistake. Unpermitted structural reconstruction can trigger stop-work orders, require demolition of completed work, and create disclosure obligations that affect property value. Licensed and insured contractors who pull permits protect the homeowner, not just themselves.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Sacramento's dry summers and wet winters create two distinct reconstruction windows. Winter storms — particularly the atmospheric river events that have become more frequent across the Central Valley — can cause roof and wall damage that requires immediate temporary protection before permanent reconstruction can begin. Starting a rebuild during active rain season means sequencing exterior work carefully around weather.

Summer heat accelerates drying of framing and sheathing, which is an advantage, but it also means adhesives, caulks, and some coatings have shorter working times. For fire damage reconstruction specifically, the dry season also means fire-resistive assemblies and ember-resistant venting are increasingly relevant in Sacramento's foothill-adjacent neighborhoods, where defensible space and construction standards continue to evolve.
