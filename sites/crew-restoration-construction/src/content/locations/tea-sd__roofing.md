## Why Tea Properties See Roofing Problems Earlier Than Expected

The Sioux Falls metro sits in a climate corridor that delivers some of the most punishing roofing conditions in the country. Tea gets the same punishment: freeze-thaw cycles that can run thirty or more times between November and March, hailstorms that roll through the corridor between I-29 and Highway 11 with little warning, and sustained winds that exploit any unsealed seam or lifted shingle tab. Ice dams are a particular problem on the steeper-pitched homes common in newer Tea subdivisions — warm air escaping through inadequate attic insulation melts snow at the ridge, and that water refreezes at the cold eave, forcing moisture under the first course of shingles.

Beyond weather, the rapid pace of construction in Tea during the 2010s boom means some homes were roofed quickly with entry-level materials to hit builder price points. ## Our Roofing Installation and Replacement Process in Tea

Every project starts with a thorough inspection — not a quick walk-around, but a close look at the decking condition, attic ventilation, flashing at every penetration, and the condition of the drip edge. In Tea's newer construction zones, we frequently find that ridge vents were installed but soffit vents were blocked during insulation, creating the pressure imbalance that accelerates shingle aging. We document all of this before a single shingle is pulled.

Once the scope is agreed on, the process moves in a logical sequence: full tear-off of existing layers (South Dakota building code limits the number of overlay layers, and most Tea homes are already at or near that limit), inspection and replacement of any soft or delaminated decking, installation of ice-and-water shield along all eaves and valleys — a step that is non-negotiable in a climate that sees the freeze-thaw cycles Tea does — followed by synthetic underlayment, new drip edge, and then the shingle installation itself. We close out every job with a debris sweep using a rolling magnet to pull fasteners from the lawn and driveway.

Material selection matters here. Architectural shingles rated for 130 mph wind uplift are the practical minimum for this part of South Dakota.
