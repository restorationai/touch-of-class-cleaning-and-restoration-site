When the Big Sioux River swells after a late-spring thaw or a summer thunderstorm rolls across the prairie and overwhelms a sump pump, water finds its way into Sioux Falls homes faster than most homeowners expect. The city's mix of clay-heavy soils — which shed water instead of absorbing it — means that even a moderate rain event can push groundwater against foundation walls and into basements within hours. If you're standing in a wet basement or watching a ceiling bubble from a burst pipe, the clock is already running: mold can begin colonizing saturated drywall in as little as 24 to 48 hours.

## Why Sioux Falls Properties See Water Damage Issues

Sioux Falls sits on a relatively flat glacial plain with expansive clay soils that drain poorly. During freeze-thaw cycles — common from late February through April — the ground shifts, and older foundation walls develop hairline cracks that let snowmelt seep in. Neighborhoods like McKennan Park and the Cathedral Historic District are filled with homes built before 1960, many with stone or brick foundations that were never designed to handle modern hydrostatic pressure. Those older foundations can absorb significant moisture before a homeowner notices any visible water, meaning the damage is often deeper than it looks.

On the newer end of the city, developments near Prairie Green and the 57108 corridor were built on lots where topsoil was stripped during construction, leaving compacted clay close to the surface. Improper grading — or grading that has settled over time — channels runoff directly toward foundations instead of away from them. Add in aging municipal infrastructure in some central neighborhoods, and the combination of exterior water intrusion and interior plumbing failures makes water damage one of the most common calls in the metro.

## Our Water Damage Restoration Process in Sioux Falls

Every job starts with a thorough moisture assessment using thermal imaging cameras and calibrated moisture meters. This step matters especially in Sioux Falls' older housing stock, where water can travel behind plaster walls or under original hardwood floors without leaving an obvious wet spot on the surface.

Once we've mapped the full extent of the damage, we move to water extraction — removing standing water with truck-mounted and portable extraction units before it migrates further into subfloor assemblies or wall cavities. After extraction, we set industrial-grade desiccant dehumidifiers and axial air movers calibrated to the room's cubic footage and the materials present. Drying is monitored daily with moisture readings logged at each check, so you have a documented record of progress — something your insurance adjuster will want to see.

If structural materials like subfloor sheathing or wall framing have absorbed enough water to compromise their integrity, we handle the controlled demolition and reconstruction as well. That means one point of contact from the wet carpet to the finished wall — no handoff gap where work falls through.

## Sioux Falls Insurance Coordination

Most standard homeowner policies in South Dakota cover sudden and accidental water damage — a burst pipe, a washing machine supply line failure, an ice dam backing water under shingles. What they typically exclude is long-term seepage or flooding from surface water, which requires separate flood coverage through the National Flood Insurance Program.

We document every affected area with photographs, moisture readings, and written scope notes formatted the way adjusters expect to receive them. That documentation package reduces back-and-forth with carriers and helps move claims forward. If your policy includes direct repair billing, we can work within that framework; if you're paying out of pocket first, we'll make sure the paperwork supports your reimbursement claim.

## Local Note: What Sioux Falls Basements Tell Us Before We Start

Crew works across the metro — from the historic bungalows near All Saints to newer construction east of Sanford USD Medical Center — and one pattern shows up repeatedly in pre-1970 homes: the original drain tile systems (perimeter drains installed around the foundation footing) are often clay tile, not modern perforated plastic pipe. Clay tile joints separate over decades, and when they fail, the drain tile that was supposed to carry water away from the foundation instead becomes a pathway for soil and water to enter. When we see a basement that floods repeatedly despite a working sump pump, failed drain tile is usually the culprit. We flag it in our assessment so the homeowner can address the root cause — not just the symptom — before the next heavy rain.
