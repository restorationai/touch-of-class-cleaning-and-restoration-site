## Why Worthington Properties See Mold Issues

Southwestern Minnesota's climate is more aggressive than its latitude suggests. Worthington experiences some of the widest humidity swings in the state — dry, bitter winters followed by humid summers that push dew points into the uncomfortable range. That seasonal whiplash stresses building envelopes. Condensation forms on cold basement walls when warm summer air moves in; vapor barriers that were installed decades ago degrade and lose effectiveness.

Homes in the Lake Okabena area face a compounding challenge. Lots close to the lake sit on soils with high clay content that holds groundwater near the surface. After heavy spring rains or snowmelt, that water presses against foundation walls through hydrostatic pressure. Small seepage events that seem minor — a damp corner, a musty smell after a wet week — are often the beginning of a mold problem that's already spreading inside the wall cavity by the time it's noticed.

Older housing stock in and around Downtown Worthington adds another layer of complexity. Many of these homes were built with materials and ventilation standards that predate modern moisture management. Attic bypasses, uninsulated crawl spaces, and original single-pane windows all create cold surfaces where condensation accumulates. Mold in these structures often isn't the result of a single dramatic event — it's the product of years of marginal moisture conditions finally tipping past a threshold.

## Our Mold Remediation Process in Worthington

Every job starts with a thorough assessment — not a visual scan, but a systematic inspection using moisture meters and thermal imaging to map where water has traveled beyond what's visible. In Worthington homes with older plaster or original wood-framed walls, moisture readings can be deceptive; the material holds water differently than modern drywall, and drying timelines need to be adjusted accordingly.

Containment comes next. Affected areas are isolated with polyethylene sheeting and negative air pressure to prevent spores from spreading to unaffected rooms during the removal process. This step is especially important in older homes where HVAC systems may not be zoned — a forced-air furnace can distribute spores throughout an entire house if the work area isn't properly sealed.

Physical removal of contaminated materials follows: affected drywall, insulation, and porous substrates are bagged, sealed, and removed from the structure. Structural framing that can be salvaged is treated with EPA-registered antimicrobial agents. HEPA air scrubbers run continuously throughout the process to capture airborne particulates. Once the space is dry and treated, we document conditions before any reconstruction begins — a step that matters both for your peace of mind and for insurance documentation.
