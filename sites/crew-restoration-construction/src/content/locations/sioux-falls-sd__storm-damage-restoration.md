## Why Sioux Falls Properties Take Storm Damage Hard

Sioux Falls sits in a corridor where cold Canadian air masses collide with warm, moisture-laden air pushing north from the Gulf — a recipe for violent, fast-moving storms from late April through September. The city averages roughly 45 days of severe thunderstorm activity per year, and hail is the most common culprit behind insurance claims in Minnehaha County.

The housing stock adds another layer of complexity. Older homes in the All Saints and Whittier neighborhoods were built with dimensional lumber framing, wood shake or asphalt shingles layered over original cedar, and in many cases, original single-pane windows. Those materials absorb impact differently than modern construction — a hailstone that dents a newer architectural shingle can crack a 1960s tab shingle outright, opening the roof deck to water infiltration within hours. Meanwhile, the expansive clay soils common across much of the metro mean that water pooling around a foundation after a storm doesn't drain quickly; it sits, and it finds its way inside.

## What Storm Damage Restoration Looks Like Here

Every storm job starts with a systematic exterior-to-interior assessment before any debris is moved. That sequence matters because disturbing a tree that's partially resting on a damaged roof can shift load and worsen structural compromise. Our process:

**1. Stabilization and emergency tarping.** Exposed roof decking gets covered fast to stop active water intrusion. We document every damaged surface with photos and measurements before tarping — that documentation is your insurance file.

**2. Structural and envelope inspection.** We check fascia, soffits, gutters, siding, and window frames for impact damage, then move inside to assess ceilings, walls, and subfloors for moisture infiltration. In homes near Prairie Green and other areas with finished basements, we pay particular attention to window wells and egress openings, which collect debris and water during heavy rain events.

**3. Water extraction and drying.** If rain entered through a compromised roof or broken windows, standing water comes out first with truck-mounted extraction, followed by industrial air movers and desiccant dehumidifiers calibrated for Sioux Falls's humidity levels. South Dakota's continental climate means indoor humidity can swing dramatically — equipment settings that work in July require adjustment in September.

**4. Debris removal and structural repair.** Tree limbs, damaged roofing materials, and destroyed siding come off the property. Structural repairs — decking, framing, roofing, siding, windows — follow once the envelope is dry and stable.

## Local Note

One pattern we see repeatedly in the Cathedral Historic District and surrounding older neighborhoods: homes built before 1960 often have original plank roof sheathing rather than plywood or OSB. When hail or wind damage allows water past the shingles, that plank sheathing absorbs moisture unevenly — boards swell at different rates, which can push roofing nails and create secondary leak points that don't show up until the next rain. If your home is pre-1960 and you've had any roof penetration, ask specifically about a sheathing inspection before the roofing crew closes everything up. It's a step that gets skipped when contractors are moving fast after a big storm event, and it's one of the more common sources of callbacks we see from work done by others.
