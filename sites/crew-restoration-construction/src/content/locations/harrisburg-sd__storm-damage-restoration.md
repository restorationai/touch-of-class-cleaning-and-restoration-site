## Why Harrisburg Properties See Disproportionate Storm Damage

The housing stock here tells a specific story. A large share of Harrisburg's homes were built in the last fifteen years — quality construction, but with shallow root systems on newly planted trees and landscaping that hasn't yet knit together. During a high-wind event, those younger trees are far more likely to fail than a 40-year-old oak, and when they go, they tend to land on roofs, fences, and vehicles rather than open lawn. Willow Creek, which runs through the heart of the community, also creates natural low-lying drainage corridors that can back up fast when a storm drops two or three inches of rain in under an hour. Homes near those drainage paths — including parts of the Willow Run neighborhood — can see water intrusion at foundation walls and window wells even when the roof is completely intact.

The region also sits in a hail belt that extends up through eastern South Dakota. Golf-ball-sized hail is not unusual during peak severe weather season (April through August), and it punishes asphalt shingles, gutters, siding, and HVAC equipment in ways that aren't always visible from the ground. A roof that looks passable from the street may have enough granule loss and micro-fractures to fail within the next rain cycle.

## Our Storm Damage Restoration Process in Harrisburg

The first thing we do on any storm job is a full exterior and interior assessment before any debris is moved. That sequencing matters for insurance documentation — adjusters need to see the damage in context, not after a crew has already tarped, cut, and hauled. We photograph every affected surface, note water intrusion points, and flag any structural concerns before work begins.

From there, the process follows the damage:

- **Emergency stabilization** — roof tarping, board-up, and temporary weatherproofing to stop additional water entry while a permanent repair plan is developed.
- **Tree and debris removal** — we coordinate safe removal of downed trees, including any that have contacted the structure, before reconstruction begins. For trees that have breached the roof deck, we sequence the lift carefully to avoid secondary structural damage.
- **Water mitigation** — storm-driven water intrusion is treated the same way as any water loss: extraction, drying, and monitoring until affected materials reach acceptable moisture levels. Mold colonization can begin within 24 to 48 hours of a moisture event, so this phase moves quickly.
- **Structural repair and reconstruction** — once the structure is dry and stable, we move into full reconstruction: roofing, siding, windows, framing, drywall, and interior finishes as needed.

Having restoration and construction under one roof means you're not coordinating two separate contractors or waiting for a handoff that adds days to the timeline.

## Local Note

One pattern we've noticed specifically in newer Harrisburg subdivisions: the engineered wood and OSB sheathing used in homes built over the last decade can absorb storm-driven water quickly once the weather barrier is compromised — faster than older plywood or dimensional lumber. If a roof breach or failed window seal lets water behind the exterior cladding, that sheathing can begin to swell and delaminate within a few days. It's not always visible from inside the home until the damage is more extensive. On any storm job where we suspect envelope penetration, we use moisture meters at the sheathing layer — not just at drywall — before signing off on a dry structure.
