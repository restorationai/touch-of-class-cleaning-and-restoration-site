## Why Sibley Properties See Roofing Problems Earlier Than Expected

Osceola County sits in a climate band that delivers some of the most punishing roof conditions in the state. Average annual snowfall, combined with sustained sub-zero stretches, creates ice dam conditions on low-slope roofs that are common in Sibley's older residential neighborhoods. When ice dams form at the eave line, meltwater backs up under shingles and into the roof deck — often without any visible exterior sign until the damage is already significant.

The housing stock around Downtown Sibley skews older, with a meaningful share of homes built before 1970. Many of those roofs were originally installed with 3-tab asphalt shingles rated for 20 to 25 years. If a roof hasn't been replaced in the last two decades, it's likely working on borrowed time regardless of how it looks from the street. Older decking in these homes can also be board sheathing rather than plywood, which responds differently to moisture intrusion and requires careful inspection before new materials go down.

Hail is another consistent factor. Sibley sits in a hail corridor that sees damaging storm events most summers, and the granule loss from even moderate hail accelerates shingle aging by years. A roof that looks intact from the ground may be missing 30 to 40 percent of its protective granule layer after a single storm.

## Our Roofing Installation and Replacement Process in Sibley

Every project starts with a full roof inspection — not a sales walk, but a documented assessment of the decking, flashing, ventilation, and shingle condition. We photograph existing damage, measure the roof planes accurately, and identify any underlying issues that need to be addressed before new materials go on. Skipping that step is how contractors end up installing a new roof over a soft deck that fails within a few years.

For most Sibley residential replacements, we remove the existing shingle layers down to the deck, inspect and replace any compromised sheathing, install an ice-and-water shield along the eaves and valleys — critical given the ice dam risk in this climate — and then apply synthetic underlayment across the full field before any finish material goes down. We use architectural shingles rated for high-wind and impact resistance as our standard recommendation for this region; the upgrade cost is modest relative to the performance difference in Iowa weather.

Flashing at chimneys, skylights, and wall intersections gets replaced as a standard part of the job, not an add-on. That's where most roof leaks originate, and leaving old flashing under new shingles is a shortcut that creates callbacks.
