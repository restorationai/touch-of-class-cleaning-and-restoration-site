## Why Akron Properties See Elevated Fire Damage Challenges

Akron is a small Plymouth County town, and much of its residential and commercial building stock reflects that history. Many homes in the Downtown Akron area were built in the mid-20th century or earlier, which means balloon-frame construction is common. In a balloon-frame house, wall cavities run uninterrupted from the foundation to the roof — a design that lets smoke and heat travel vertically through the structure far more aggressively than in modern platform-frame builds. That means a kitchen fire can leave smoke residue in attic insulation and second-floor closets even when the flames never left the first floor.

The region's climate adds another layer of complexity. Northwest Iowa winters are cold and dry, and heating systems — whether older forced-air furnaces or wood-burning stoves still found in some rural-adjacent properties — run hard from October through April. Overworked heating equipment and aging electrical wiring are among the most common ignition sources in homes like these. After a fire, the same cold-dry air that accelerates soot migration also causes porous materials like brick, mortar, and unfinished wood to absorb odor compounds more deeply, making thorough cleaning and deodorization more technically demanding than it would be in a milder climate.

## Our Fire Damage Restoration Process in Akron

Every fire loss is different, but the sequence of work follows a disciplined order that prevents one phase from undoing another.

**1. Damage assessment and stabilization.** Before any cleaning begins, the structure is evaluated for safety — compromised load-bearing elements, electrical hazards, and roof or wall openings that need board-up or tarping to prevent weather intrusion. In Akron's climate, an open roof in February can introduce ice and moisture damage on top of fire damage within 24 hours.

**2. Soot and smoke removal.** Dry soot from fast-burning fires and wet, oily soot from slow smoldering fires require different cleaning chemistries. We identify the fire type and match the method — dry sponging for light smoke, alkaline cleaners for protein-based residue from kitchen fires, and thermal fogging or hydroxyl generation for embedded odor in porous materials like wood framing and drywall.

**3. Structural drying if water is present.** Firefighting efforts leave water. In older homes common to the Downtown Akron area, subfloor assemblies and plaster walls hold moisture longer than modern materials. Moisture mapping with thermal imaging guides drying equipment placement so no wet pocket is missed.

**4. Odor elimination and air quality.** Smoke odor that isn't fully neutralized at the molecular level will return when temperatures rise. We use hydroxyl generators and ozone treatment in unoccupied spaces to break down odor compounds rather than masking them.

**5. Reconstruction.** Once the structure is clean, dry, and documented, rebuild work begins — framing, drywall, insulation, cabinetry, and finish work coordinated to match what was there before.

## Local Note

Homes in Akron's older residential blocks — particularly those built before 1960 — frequently have plaster-and-lath interior walls rather than drywall. Plaster is denser and more alkaline than gypsum board, which changes how soot bonds to the surface and how cleaning chemistry needs to be applied. Aggressive scrubbing that works on drywall can permanently smear oily soot into plaster. Crews familiar with this distinction test a small section before committing to a cleaning method across an entire room — a step that saves walls that might otherwise be written off as unsalvageable.
