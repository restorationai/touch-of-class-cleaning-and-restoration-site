## Why George Properties See Siding and Gutter Issues

George sits close to the Lyon County line in Iowa's northwest corner, where the landscape is flat and wind has nowhere to slow down. That exposure accelerates wear on vinyl and wood siding in ways that are less common in more sheltered communities. Wind-driven rain finds its way behind lap siding at the seams; repeated freeze-thaw cycles push those seams apart further each winter. Gutters on homes near Downtown George — many of them built in the mid-twentieth century — were often sized for standard rainfall, not for the intense short-duration storms that have become more frequent in the region. Undersized or partially clogged gutters overflow at the fascia, and that standing moisture is one of the more reliable paths to rot behind the trim board.

## Our Siding and Gutter Process in George

Every project starts with a full exterior walk-through. On siding work, that means checking not just the visible face of each panel but the condition of the housewrap or felt paper underneath, the integrity of corner trim and window flashings, and whether any prior repairs created moisture traps. On gutter work, we measure the roof's drainage area and calculate the correct gutter width and downspout count for the actual runoff load — a step that gets skipped on quick-turnaround jobs but matters a great deal on a flat-site property where there's no grade to carry overflow away from the foundation.

For siding replacement, we remove damaged sections back to solid substrate, inspect sheathing for soft spots, and install new material with manufacturer-specified fasteners and sealants rated for Iowa temperature swings. Gutter installation includes setting proper pitch toward downspouts, securing hangers at intervals that account for ice load, and extending downspout runs far enough from the foundation to actually redirect water — not just move it six inches from the wall.

## Local Note

One thing that comes up specifically on older George-area homes: the original wood siding on mid-century farmhouses and in-town bungalows was often installed over skip sheathing — horizontal boards with gaps between them rather than solid plywood or OSB. That's a normal construction method for the era, but it changes how new siding gets installed. Solid sheathing needs to be added before installing modern vinyl or fiber cement, both for structural backing and to meet current energy code requirements. Skipping that step and fastening new siding directly to skip sheathing is a shortcut that shows up as wavy, loose panels within a few seasons. We flag this during the initial inspection so there are no surprises in the project scope.
