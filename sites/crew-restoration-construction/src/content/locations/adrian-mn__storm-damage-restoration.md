## Why Adrian Properties See Significant Storm Damage

The flat, treeless terrain that defines this part of Nobles County is beautiful on a clear day and genuinely dangerous during a severe weather event. Without hills or dense tree cover to dissipate wind energy, gusts that might clock 60 mph in a more sheltered area can hit Adrian structures at 75 mph or higher. That translates directly into lifted shingles, compromised fascia, and — in the worst cases — structural racking on older wood-frame homes.

Adrian's housing stock skews older. Many homes near Downtown Adrian and along the residential streets feeding off Main Street were built in the mid-20th century, when roofing standards and sheathing requirements were less stringent than current Minnesota code. Those roofs are more likely to fail at the decking layer rather than just losing surface shingles, which means water intrusion can reach attic insulation and ceiling framing before a homeowner realizes the damage goes deeper than it looks from the street. Hail is a separate problem: the large-stone hail events that track through southwest Minnesota in late spring and early summer can dent aluminum siding, crack asphalt shingles, and damage HVAC equipment in a single pass.

## Our Storm Damage Restoration Process in Adrian

The first step after a storm is an honest damage assessment — not an estimate shaped by what insurance might cover, but a thorough inspection of the roof, exterior envelope, attic space, and any interior areas where water has already moved. We document everything photographically before a single piece of material is removed, because that documentation is what drives a clean insurance claim.

From there, the process moves in a logical sequence: emergency tarping or board-up to stop ongoing weather exposure, controlled demolition of materials that can't be dried or salvaged, structural drying if water has entered the building, and then reconstruction. On storm jobs in Adrian, reconstruction often means working with materials that match the existing character of the home — older neighborhoods don't always accept a modern architectural shingle that looks out of place against the surrounding houses, and we source materials accordingly.

Tree strikes deserve their own attention. When a large tree limb or full trunk comes down on a roof, the visible hole is rarely the full extent of the damage. The impact load can crack rafters, shift ridge boards, and compress ceiling joists in ways that aren't obvious until the debris is cleared and the framing is inspected from inside the attic. We do that inspection on every tree-strike job before writing a scope of work.

## Local Note

One thing that comes up on Adrian storm jobs that doesn't always appear elsewhere: older homes in this part of southwest Minnesota were frequently built with a combination of wood lap siding and minimal house wrap, or no house wrap at all. When wind-driven rain gets behind that siding during a storm, it can saturate the wall cavity insulation — often original fiberglass batts — without producing obvious interior water stains right away. By the time a homeowner notices a musty smell or soft drywall, the insulation has been wet long enough to support mold growth inside the wall. On any job where wind-driven rain was significant, we probe the wall cavities before closing out the exterior work, because finding wet insulation early is far less expensive than finding mold six weeks later.
