## Why Sioux Falls Properties See Mold Issues

The Big Sioux River corridor and the low-lying areas near Falls Park aren't the only places where moisture accumulates — it happens quietly in crawl spaces, behind finished basement walls, and inside HVAC chases across the metro. Sioux Falls receives an average of nearly 25 inches of precipitation annually, much of it concentrated in late spring and early summer. That rain arrives fast, and older storm drainage infrastructure in neighborhoods like Whittier and All Saints can struggle to redirect it quickly enough to prevent water from pressing against foundation walls.

The city's housing stock adds another layer of complexity. Homes built before 1970 — common throughout the Cathedral Historic District and the blocks surrounding Sanford USD Medical Center — frequently have original fiberglass or cellulose insulation packed into wall cavities. That material holds moisture for weeks without any visible sign on the surface. By the time a homeowner notices a musty odor or a dark stain on drywall, mold colonies may already be well established behind the finish layer.

South Dakota's freeze-thaw cycle also drives mold risk in ways that aren't immediately obvious. Water that infiltrates masonry during a January thaw can refreeze, expand, and widen micro-cracks — then melt again in March and wick inward. Repeated over several winters, this process can saturate the interior face of a block foundation wall without a single visible leak event.

## Our Mold Remediation Process in Sioux Falls

Every job starts with a thorough assessment — not a visual walk-through, but a moisture-mapping inspection using thermal imaging and calibrated moisture meters to locate hidden saturation behind walls and under flooring. We identify the moisture source first, because mold remediation without addressing the source is a temporary fix.

Once the scope is defined, we establish containment using negative-air pressure chambers and 6-mil poly barriers to prevent cross-contamination to unaffected areas of the home. HEPA-filtered air scrubbers run continuously throughout the work. Affected porous materials — drywall, insulation, wood framing where necessary — are carefully removed and bagged for disposal in accordance with South Dakota Department of Environment and Natural Resources guidelines.

Surfaces are then treated with EPA-registered antimicrobial agents, and structural components are dried to manufacturer-specified moisture content before any reconstruction begins. We don't hand off the rebuild to a separate contractor — our construction division handles framing, insulation, and drywall in-house, which means one point of contact from the first call through the final coat of paint.

## Local Note

Homes in the Cathedral Historic District and parts of All Saints were frequently built with plaster-and-lath wall systems rather than modern drywall. Plaster is denser and slower to release absorbed moisture than gypite board — which means that even after the visible mold is removed and the surface looks clean, the substrate behind it can remain damp enough to support regrowth for weeks. On these jobs, we extend the drying phase and take additional moisture readings at the lath layer before closing the wall. It adds time, but it's the difference between a remediation that holds and one that fails a post-clearance air test.
