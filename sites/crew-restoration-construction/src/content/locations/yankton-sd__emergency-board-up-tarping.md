## Why Yankton Properties See Board-Up and Tarping Needs More Than You'd Expect

Yankton sits in a climate corridor that delivers genuine extremes: hard freezes that can crack masonry and buckle siding, spring deluges that overwhelm drainage, and summer hailstorms that strip shingles in minutes. Properties along the Riverside corridor and closer to Gavins Point Dam are particularly exposed — the Missouri River floodplain creates a humidity baseline that means any opening in the building envelope starts admitting moisture almost immediately.

The housing stock adds another layer of complexity. A significant share of homes in the 57078 ZIP code were built before modern sheathing standards, which means older board-and-batten or skip-sheathed roofs don't hold a tarp anchor the same way a modern OSB deck does. Window openings in pre-1970 construction are often non-standard sizes, so off-the-shelf plywood panels rarely fit without cutting on site. These are not problems you want to discover at the start of a job — they're problems a crew needs to anticipate before they pull into the driveway.

## Our Emergency Board-Up and Tarping Process in Yankton

The first thing we do on arrival is a rapid structural assessment — not just of the obvious damage, but of secondary vulnerabilities. A fire-damaged wall in Downtown Yankton might have a compromised header that makes standard panel attachment unsafe; a hail-punched roof in the Westside might have hidden sheathing rot that changes how we anchor the tarp. We document everything with photographs before touching a single piece of material, because that documentation is what your insurance adjuster will use to scope the claim.

For board-up work, we use minimum ½-inch plywood secured with construction screws rather than nails — screws hold shear load better and are easier to remove cleanly when the permanent repair crew arrives. For roof tarping, we use heavy-duty polyethylene tarps rated for the wind speeds common to southeast South Dakota, weighted and secured at the ridge rather than just draped over the peak. A tarp that lifts in a 40-mph gust does more damage than no tarp at all.

When fire damage is involved, we coordinate the board-up with any active smoke or odor containment already in place. Sealing a fire-damaged opening incorrectly can pressurize residual smoke odor into unaffected areas of the home — something we're careful to avoid.

Most homeowners' policies cover emergency protective measures — board-up and tarping are generally classified as "reasonable steps to prevent further damage," which is language that appears in the majority of standard policies. What matters is documentation: photos of the damage before work begins, photos during installation, and an itemized scope of what was installed and how.
