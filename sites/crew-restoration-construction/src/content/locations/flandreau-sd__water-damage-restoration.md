## Why Flandreau Properties Are Vulnerable to Water Damage

Flandreau's geography puts it at the intersection of two risk factors that don't always appear together. The Big Sioux River corridor creates a high water table through much of town, meaning that even a modest rainfall event can saturate the soil faster than it drains — and that saturated ground pushes hydrostatic pressure against foundation walls and slab edges. Homes near the river, particularly older properties closer to Downtown Flandreau, often have block or poured-concrete foundations that were built before modern waterproofing membranes were standard. Hairline cracks that look cosmetic in July become active water intrusion points by April.

South Dakota's freeze-thaw cycle compounds this. When temperatures swing from well below zero in January to the 40s by late February, the soil contracts and expands repeatedly, widening foundation gaps and stressing supply lines that run through uninsulated crawl spaces. Pipe bursts in Flandreau tend to cluster in late winter, when the hardest cold has passed but the ground hasn't fully stabilized — a pattern that catches homeowners off guard because the worst of winter feels over.

## Our Water Damage Restoration Process in Flandreau

When we arrive on-site, the first priority is stopping active water movement — shutting off the source if it's a burst pipe, or deploying temporary barriers if the intrusion is ground-driven. From there, the process follows a structured sequence:

**Moisture mapping and documentation.** We use thermal imaging and calibrated moisture meters to map exactly where water has traveled, including inside wall cavities and beneath flooring. This documentation also becomes the foundation of your insurance claim file.

**Water extraction.** Truck-mounted and portable extraction units pull standing water from affected rooms. In basements common to Flandreau's older housing stock, we often encounter water that has wicked into concrete block cores — extraction alone won't resolve that, which is why the drying phase matters as much as the initial removal.

**Structural drying.** Industrial desiccant and refrigerant dehumidifiers, paired with high-velocity air movers, drive moisture out of framing, subfloor, and wall assemblies. We monitor readings daily and adjust equipment placement until materials reach target dryness levels — typically over three to five days depending on the extent of saturation and the time of year.

**Controlled demolition when necessary.** Saturated drywall, insulation, and flooring that cannot be dried in place are removed to prevent mold from taking hold. In Flandreau homes with older plaster walls, we assess each section individually — plaster holds moisture longer than modern drywall, so we extend monitoring periods rather than assume a standard timeline.

## Local Note: What Flandreau's Older Housing Stock Means for Drying Timelines

A meaningful share of homes in and around Downtown Flandreau were built before 1970, and many of them have original subfloor assemblies — tongue-and-groove boards over dimensional lumber joists rather than the OSB panels common in newer construction. That older subfloor material absorbs water more deeply and releases it more slowly than engineered panels. When we're working in these homes, we extend our drying monitoring by one to two days beyond what a standard residential timeline would suggest, and we position air movers to direct airflow along the joist bays rather than just across the surface. Skipping that step is how salvageable floors get written off unnecessarily — or worse, get closed up with residual moisture still present.
