## Why Hawarden Properties See Roofing Problems Earlier Than Expected

Hawarden sits in a climatic crossroads. Cold Arctic air masses push down from the north while moisture-laden systems track up from the south, and the result is a freeze-thaw cycle that repeats dozens of times between October and March. Water infiltrates small cracks in shingles or around flashing, freezes overnight, expands, and widens those gaps by spring. On homes near the Big Sioux River corridor, ambient moisture levels stay elevated longer after rain events, which accelerates granule loss on asphalt shingles and shortens their effective lifespan.

The housing stock in and around Downtown Hawarden adds another layer of complexity. A significant share of the residential properties here were built in the mid-20th century or earlier, meaning original roof decks may be 1×6 skip sheathing or board sheathing rather than modern plywood or OSB. That matters during replacement — a new synthetic underlayment and architectural shingle system installed over deteriorated board sheathing won't perform the way the manufacturer's warranty assumes. Identifying and addressing deck condition before the first new shingle goes down is part of doing the job correctly.

## Our Roofing Installation and Replacement Process in Hawarden

Every project starts with a thorough inspection — not a quick visual from the driveway, but a physical assessment of the deck, existing underlayment, ridge and hip conditions, all penetration flashings, and the drip edge detail at the eaves. In Hawarden, we pay particular attention to valley flashing, because the steep pitch common on older homes in the Central Avenue district channels significant water volume through those valleys during heavy rain.

Once scope is established, we walk through material options with the property owner. Architectural asphalt shingles remain the most cost-effective choice for most residential applications here, but for homes with lower-slope sections or outbuildings, we discuss metal roofing systems that handle standing water and ice damming more reliably. Deck repairs are completed before any new material is installed. Ice-and-water shield goes down at eaves and in all valleys — a non-negotiable step given Hawarden's winter conditions. Synthetic underlayment covers the remaining deck, starter strips are set, and shingles are installed to manufacturer specifications for wind resistance, which in this part of Iowa means proper fastener placement matters as much as material quality.

Flashing details — around chimneys, skylights, and roof-to-wall transitions — are done in step flashing and counter-flashing where applicable, not caulk-dependent shortcuts. Ridge cap and ventilation are the last pieces, and we confirm that attic airflow meets the intake-to-exhaust ratio the system needs to prevent moisture buildup from below.
