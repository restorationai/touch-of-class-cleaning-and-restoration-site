## Why Worthington Properties See Sewage Backup Issues

Worthington's sewer and septic challenges are shaped by a combination of factors that don't apply everywhere. The city's clay-heavy soils expand significantly during spring thaw, which puts lateral pressure on older clay-tile and cast-iron sewer lines — the kind still found under many homes built before 1970 in and around Downtown Worthington. When those pipes shift or crack, tree roots find the gaps, and the first sign a homeowner notices is often a slow drain or a gurgling toilet that suddenly becomes a flooded basement.

The Lake Okabena area adds another layer of complexity. Properties near the lake sit at lower elevations and are already prone to seasonal water intrusion from snowmelt and spring rain. When the municipal storm system gets overwhelmed during a heavy melt event, the pressure can reverse flow in residential sewer laterals — pushing raw sewage back through floor drains and utility sinks. That combination of saturated soil and aging pipe infrastructure means sewage events in this part of Worthington tend to affect more square footage than a typical backup elsewhere.

Septic systems on the edges of town face their own seasonal stress. Ground that stays frozen well into April doesn't absorb effluent normally, and septic fields that drain slowly all winter can reach capacity fast once temperatures rise — leading to surface overflow or backflow into the home.

## Our Sewage Cleanup and Sanitization Process in Worthington

Sewage cleanup is a category 3 water loss — the most contaminated classification — and the process reflects that. When we arrive at a Worthington property, the first step is containment: sealing off affected areas to prevent cross-contamination to unaffected rooms. We identify the source of the backup and confirm it's been stopped before extraction begins.

Extraction equipment removes standing sewage and saturated material. In Worthington's older housing stock, that often means pulling up vinyl or tile flooring that has absorbed contaminated water from below, and removing the bottom courses of drywall where sewage has wicked upward. Concrete basement floors are scrubbed and treated with EPA-registered disinfectants rated for category 3 contamination. Porous materials that cannot be adequately sanitized — carpet padding, certain insulation types, particleboard subfloor — are bagged, documented, and removed from the property.

After extraction and removal, we run commercial-grade air movers and dehumidifiers to bring structural moisture levels down before any sanitization product is applied to remaining surfaces. Applying disinfectant to wet materials is ineffective; the drying step is not optional. Once surfaces are dry and treated, we document the work with photos and moisture readings for your insurance file.
