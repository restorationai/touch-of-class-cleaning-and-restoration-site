Mold growth behind walls and under fixtures is one of the more involved environmental cleanup scenarios a homeowner can face. When affected areas span multiple rooms and require fixture removal to access contaminated materials, a methodical, contained approach is essential. This case study documents the work Crew Restoration & Construction completed for Ryan in Brandon, SD in May 2025.

> "In the lower bathroom, fixtures were detached, including the vanity top and P-trap, to allow full access to affected areas. The vanity was removed and disposed of. Contaminated drywall was cut out and discarded, while the exposed studs were sanded and treated with antimicrobial solution to eliminate mold growth. In the lower laundry room, the laundry tub and washer/dryer were detached to access the impacted wall area. Approximately 2' x 4' of mold-affected drywall was cut out and removed. Exposed framing members were cleaned, sanded where necessary, and treated with antimicrobial solution to ensure proper remediation. Containment was established during all work to prevent cross-contamination, and equipment was deployed to maintain air quality throughout the process."

## Why Fixture Removal Comes First

On a typical environmental cleanup involving mold behind walls, the first challenge is access. Mold rarely stops at the surface you can see. It follows moisture pathways into wall cavities, along framing members, and behind cabinetry or plumbing fixtures. Detaching vanities, P-traps, laundry tubs, and appliances is not optional prep work; it is the only way to confirm the full extent of contamination and ensure nothing is left untreated behind a fixture that gets reinstalled.

In a standard remediation workflow, any material that cannot be adequately cleaned and treated (typically porous materials like drywall) is removed and discarded rather than treated in place. Framing lumber, being less porous, can often be retained after thorough sanding to remove surface growth and treatment with an EPA-registered antimicrobial solution. Sanding is important because it removes the superficial layer where spores are concentrated, giving the antimicrobial agent direct contact with the wood surface.

## Containment and Air Quality Control

One of the most important principles in mold remediation is preventing the spread of spores to unaffected areas of the home during the work itself. On a standard loss like this, remediation professionals establish physical containment barriers, typically using heavy polyethylene sheeting sealed at doorways and penetrations, to isolate the work zone from the rest of the living space.

Air quality control runs alongside containment. Negative air machines or air scrubbers equipped with HEPA filtration are commonly deployed inside the contained area. These units draw air through fine filters that capture mold spores and other particulates, exhausting cleaner air and maintaining lower air pressure inside the work zone relative to surrounding rooms. That pressure differential means any air movement at the containment boundary flows inward rather than outward, reducing the risk of cross-contamination. Both measures together protect the occupants and the unaffected portions of the structure while remediation is underway.

## Treating Multiple Affected Rooms

When mold is found in more than one area of a home, each space is typically treated as its own contained work zone, with the same access, removal, cleaning, and treatment steps applied consistently. The goal is not simply to address what is visible but to verify that the underlying structure is clean and stable before any reconstruction begins. Skipping a room or treating it less thoroughly than another creates the risk that remaining mold will recolonize the remediated areas once conditions allow.

After remediation is complete, a clearance inspection (performed by a third-party industrial hygienist in many cases) can confirm that spore counts have returned to normal background levels before new drywall and fixtures are installed.
