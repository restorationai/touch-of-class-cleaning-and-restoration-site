Widespread moisture intrusion affecting multiple rooms of a home presents one of the more complex scenarios a restoration crew can face. When saturation reaches drywall, flooring, subfloor, and structural cavities simultaneously, the mitigation process demands careful sequencing, continuous monitoring, and coordination with adjusters and homeowners alike. This project in Sioux Falls, SD illustrates exactly that kind of multi-room, multi-material water loss.

> "Upon arrival, Crew assessed the loss and identified widespread moisture intrusion affecting multiple areas of the home. While no standing water extraction was required, debris was present on the main floor, and moisture was detected in drywall, flooring, and subfloor materials across the kitchen, dining room, living area, bathroom, and basement. Wet drywall was noted in the ceiling and walls, and several flooring materials (tile and wood) were saturated. The kitchen and dining room flooring extended beneath cabinetry, limiting full removal at this stage... Moisture readings indicated ongoing saturation in drywall, wood flooring, and subfloor, with some areas showing warping... Crew initiated mitigation and drying efforts immediately. Debris was removed from the affected floor areas, and drying equipment was strategically placed throughout the structure. A total of 4 dehumidifiers, 8 air movers, and 2 air scrubbers were installed to control moisture and maintain air quality... Affected drywall and insulation were removed from the kitchen and dining room areas. Temporary containment was installed along the kitchen counters until a determination could be made regarding cabinet and countertop removal. Flood cuts were performed, including 2-foot cuts on one wall and 4-foot cuts on an adjacent wall, to address moisture migration... Drying mats were placed in the dining room in an attempt to salvage portions of the wood flooring. Self-leveling material was removed due to warped and wet subfloor conditions, with additional removal still required. Subfloor replacement is anticipated in select areas."

## How Structural Drying Works on a Multi-Room Loss

When moisture spreads across several rooms and material types, a restoration team cannot simply place a few fans and wait. On a typical loss like this, technicians take moisture readings throughout the structure to map exactly where saturation exists and at what levels. Those readings establish a baseline, and subsequent readings taken over the following days track whether materials are trending toward drying targets or whether additional intervention is needed.

The combination of dehumidifiers, air movers, and air scrubbers serves distinct purposes. Air movers accelerate evaporation by moving humid air away from wet surfaces. Dehumidifiers then pull that moisture-laden air and extract the water vapor, exhausting drier air back into the space. Air scrubbers filter airborne particulates that become disturbed during demolition and drying activity. Repositioning equipment as drying progresses, as the project notes describe, is standard practice: once one area reaches acceptable moisture levels, units are redirected toward zones that are still wet, maximizing efficiency across the whole structure.

Cold temperatures add a layer of complexity in South Dakota winters. Dehumidifiers that retain residual water can freeze during transport or in under-heated spaces, which is why operational considerations like keeping basement lighting on and planning equipment pickup with appropriate vehicles are part of a thorough mitigation plan.

## Flood Cuts, Flooring Removal, and Access Constraints

Flood cuts are a standard technique in water damage mitigation. When drywall absorbs moisture, the affected section is cut away at a height above the visible damage line, typically 12 inches, 2 feet, or 4 feet depending on how far moisture has wicked upward. This exposes the wall cavity so air movers can dry the framing and insulation behind the surface. Leaving wet drywall in place traps moisture against wood framing and creates conditions favorable to mold growth, which is why removal is generally preferred over attempting to dry through the surface alone.

Flooring removal follows similar logic, but access constraints are common on real jobs. Tile and wood flooring that runs beneath cabinetry, or flooring that cannot be reached until a plumbing fixture is removed, may need to be addressed in phases. On a typical loss, crews document what has been removed, what remains, and why, so that the insurance adjuster and homeowner have a clear record for planning subsequent work. Drying mats offer one option for attempting to salvage wood flooring in place when full removal is not immediately possible, though whether salvage is achievable depends on moisture levels and how long the material has been wet.

Subfloor conditions often determine the scope of reconstruction. When self-leveling compound or subfloor panels are warped or saturated, replacement is frequently more practical than drying in place, particularly if the material has lost structural integrity.

## Working Alongside the Insurance Adjuster

On larger losses, having the adjuster present during a site visit allows for real-time decisions about scope. Items like HVAC ductwork and area rugs, which may be candidates for cleaning rather than replacement, can be evaluated and documented together. This kind of coordination helps avoid disputes later and keeps the project moving efficiently. On a typical claim, the restoration contractor documents conditions with moisture logs, photographs, and written scope notes so the adjuster has the information needed to authorize each phase of work.
