## How Much Does Mold Remediation Cost in South Dakota?

Most residential mold remediation jobs in South Dakota fall between $1,500 and $6,000, with the national average landing around $2,200 to $3,500 for a mid-size affected area. Small, contained jobs (a single bathroom or a section of crawl space wall) can come in under $1,500. Large jobs involving finished basements, HVAC systems, or structural framing regularly exceed $8,000 to $12,000.

The table below shows typical ranges by scenario. These are industry-typical figures based on scope and materials, not quotes or guarantees from any specific contractor.

| Scenario | Typical Cost Range |
|---|---|
| Single bathroom, surface mold on tile/grout | $500 - $1,500 |
| Small area (under 10 sq ft), drywall affected | $1,200 - $2,500 |
| Unfinished basement or crawl space, moderate growth | $2,000 - $4,500 |
| Finished basement, drywall and framing affected | $4,000 - $9,000 |
| Attic mold from roof leak or poor ventilation | $1,500 - $5,000 |
| HVAC system contamination | $3,000 - $10,000 |
| Whole-house or severe structural involvement | $10,000 - $30,000+ |

Mold inspection and testing is typically a separate line item: professional air sampling and surface testing in Sioux Falls generally runs $300 to $700. ## What Makes Mold Remediation Cost More (or Less) in Sioux Falls?

Several factors move the price significantly, and Sioux Falls has a few local conditions that tend to push costs toward the higher end of national ranges.

**Location in the home.** Mold in an unfinished utility room is far cheaper to remediate than mold inside a finished basement with drywall, insulation, and flooring. In Sioux Falls, a large share of homes have finished lower levels, which means more materials to remove and replace when moisture gets in.

**How long the moisture was present.** Mold can begin colonizing within 24 to 48 hours of a water event, according to the [EPA's mold cleanup guidance](https://www.epa.gov/mold/mold-cleanup-your-home). A slow leak behind a wall that went undetected for months will produce far deeper contamination than a pipe burst caught the same day. If you suspect mold has been growing quietly, our post on [signs of hidden mold](/blog/signs-of-hidden-mold/) can help you identify what to look for before you call.

**Category of the original water source.** Mold that grew from a clean water pipe leak (Category 1) is different from mold that developed after sewage backup or floodwater intrusion (Category 3, also called black water). Category 3 source contamination requires more aggressive containment and disposal protocols, which adds cost.

**South Dakota's winter humidity swings.** Sioux Falls winters are cold and dry outdoors but often humid indoors from heating systems, cooking, and poor ventilation in tightly sealed homes. That indoor humidity, combined with cold exterior walls in older neighborhoods like McKennan Park and Cathedral Historic District, creates condensation conditions that feed mold growth even without a visible leak. Crawl spaces under older homes in the All Saints and Whittier areas are particularly prone to this pattern.

**Structural materials affected.** Mold on drywall is removed by cutting out and replacing the drywall. Mold on framing lumber is treated differently, sometimes with HEPA vacuuming, antimicrobial application, and sanding, and sometimes with full replacement if the wood is structurally compromised. Framing remediation is more labor-intensive and more expensive.

**Containment and air filtration requirements.** IICRC-certified remediation (the [IICRC](https://www.iicrc.org) sets the industry standard for mold remediation through its S520 standard) requires setting up physical containment barriers and running HEPA air scrubbers during the work to prevent spores from spreading to unaffected areas. This is non-negotiable for a proper job, but it does add time and equipment cost compared to a contractor who skips these steps.

## What Does the Mold Remediation Process Actually Involve?

A proper mold remediation job follows a defined sequence. Knowing the steps helps you evaluate whether a contractor's scope of work is complete.

1. **Assessment and testing.** A certified inspector identifies the extent of growth, moisture sources, and the species present (if relevant to the scope). Air and surface samples may be collected.
2. **Containment setup.** Poly sheeting barriers isolate the work area. Negative air pressure machines exhaust contaminated air outside through HEPA filtration so spores don't migrate.
3. **Removal of affected materials.** Porous materials (drywall, insulation, carpet) that are contaminated beyond surface level are removed and bagged for disposal. Semi-porous materials like framing lumber are treated in place when structurally sound.
4. **HEPA vacuuming and antimicrobial treatment.** All surfaces in the containment zone are HEPA-vacuumed and treated with an EPA-registered antimicrobial agent.
5. **Drying and moisture correction.** The underlying moisture source must be fixed before remediation is complete. Running dehumidifiers to bring the area to acceptable moisture levels is part of this phase.
6. **Post-remediation verification (clearance testing).** An independent inspector (or the remediating company, with documentation) confirms spore counts have returned to normal levels. A written clearance report is the standard deliverable.
7. **Reconstruction.** Replacing removed drywall, insulation, and finishes is often a separate contract. Crew Restoration & Construction handles both remediation and reconstruction, which simplifies the process for homeowners who don't want to manage two contractors.

## Does Homeowners Insurance Cover Mold Remediation?

Homeowners insurance sometimes covers mold remediation, but the coverage depends entirely on what caused the mold. Most standard policies cover mold that resulted from a sudden, accidental water event that is itself covered, such as a burst pipe or an appliance failure. They typically exclude mold that resulted from long-term neglect, gradual leaks, or flooding (which requires a separate flood policy).

In practical terms: if your washing machine supply line failed and mold developed in the wall behind it, your policy likely covers it. If a slow drip under a sink went unnoticed for a year, your claim will probably be denied on grounds of maintenance neglect.

Documentation matters enormously. A written scope of work, moisture readings taken at the start of the job, and photos of the affected area before any work begins are the records that protect your claim. Make sure your contractor provides all of these before demolition starts.

## What Should You Do Next?

If you can see or smell mold in your Sioux Falls home, the right next step is a professional assessment, not a DIY cleaning attempt. Surface cleaning with bleach does not address mold inside drywall cavities or framing, and disturbing a mold colony without containment can spread spores to unaffected rooms.

Get a written scope of work before any contractor starts. The scope should identify the affected area in square footage, list the materials to be removed, specify the containment method, and include a plan for post-remediation verification. Any contractor unwilling to provide this in writing before starting is a contractor to avoid.
