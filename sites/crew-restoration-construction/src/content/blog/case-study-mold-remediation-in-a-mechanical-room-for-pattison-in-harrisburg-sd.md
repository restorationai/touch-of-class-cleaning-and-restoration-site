## Why Containment Comes First

On a typical mold remediation job, the first priority before any cleaning begins is establishing containment. The purpose is straightforward: prevent mold spores disturbed during remediation from migrating into other areas of the structure. In practice, this usually involves sealing off the work zone with polyethylene sheeting and creating negative air pressure so that airflow moves into the contained area rather than out of it. Without proper containment, remediation activity can inadvertently spread spores to spaces that were previously unaffected, which can expand the scope of the problem rather than resolve it. The containment noted in these project notes reflects standard practice for protecting the rest of the home during active work.

## RMR Treatment, Scrubbing, and Air Scrubbing: What Each Step Does

Once containment is in place, the remediation process on a job like this typically follows a defined sequence. An antimicrobial treatment (RMR-style products are a common category) is applied to affected surfaces, including masonry such as block walls, to address mold at the surface level and help inhibit regrowth. Scrubbing follows to physically remove mold material and ensure the treatment has penetrated and cleaned the surface thoroughly. Neither step alone is considered sufficient under IICRC guidelines; the combination of chemical treatment and mechanical cleaning is what produces a verifiable result.

Air scrubbing addresses a separate concern: airborne spore counts during and after remediation. An air scrubber pulls room air through a series of filters, including HEPA filtration, capturing spores before they can settle on cleaned surfaces or circulate through the structure. Running an air scrubber for an extended period, as was done on this project, is standard practice for maintaining acceptable air quality throughout the remediation process and for a period after active work concludes.

## Remediation Decisions in a Pre-Sale Context

When a property is being prepared for sale, remediation decisions sometimes carry additional considerations beyond simply eliminating the mold. In this case, the project notes describe a deliberate choice to avoid shellac application because of the visible finish it leaves behind. On a general level, encapsulants and sealers are sometimes used after remediation to coat surfaces that cannot be fully cleaned or removed. However, when a property needs to present well to buyers and inspectors, a visible coating on block walls in a mechanical room can raise questions rather than provide reassurance. Choosing methods that leave surfaces clean without adding conspicuous coatings is a reasonable approach when resale appearance is a priority. This kind of scope decision, made in advance and documented in the estimate, helps ensure the outcome matches the homeowner's actual goals.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does mold remediation in a mechanical room require the HVAC system to be shut down?**

In many cases, yes. On a typical remediation job near HVAC equipment, the system is shut down during active work to prevent spores from being drawn into ductwork and distributed through the home. The specific approach depends on the layout and the proximity of the mold to air handling components.

**What is the difference between mold remediation and mold removal?**

Mold remediation is the industry-standard term for the full process of containing, cleaning, and treating mold to bring a space back to an acceptable condition. "Mold removal" is sometimes used informally, but it can be misleading because mold spores are naturally present in most environments and cannot be entirely eliminated. The goal of remediation is to reduce mold to normal, non-harmful levels and address the conditions that allowed it to grow.

**Will mold remediation show up on a home disclosure?**

Disclosure requirements vary by state and situation. In general, sellers are advised to consult a real estate attorney about what must be disclosed. Having documented, professional remediation completed before a sale can actually support the transaction by demonstrating that a known issue was addressed according to industry standards.

**Does mold always come back after remediation?**

Not if the underlying moisture source is resolved. Mold requires moisture to grow, so remediation addresses the existing mold growth, but long-term results depend on controlling humidity and fixing any water intrusion that contributed to the original problem. A remediation contractor can often identify the likely moisture source, but addressing it may require separate work.
