Bathroom water intrusion is one of the more common calls a restoration team receives, and it often involves more hidden moisture than the visible damage suggests. When water finds its way into wall cavities, closets, and cabinetry, a methodical approach to drying and documentation is essential before any reconstruction can begin. Here is how Crew Restoration & Construction handled this type of loss for Lavoy in Sioux Falls.

> "In response to water intrusion, the affected bathroom areas were stabilized and restored. The bathroom closet door, bathroom door, and vanity were removed to access saturated materials. Moisture readings were taken throughout the space, with most drywall testing dry; however, localized wet spots were identified after rainfall. A dehumidifier was placed for targeted drying in those areas. Additional monitoring visits confirmed drying progress, and all materials were re-metered until acceptable levels were achieved. Damaged building components were measured and documented for replacement, including baseboard, casing, and doors. ## Accessing Saturated Materials: Why Removal Comes First

One of the first steps in any bathroom water loss is gaining access to the materials that have absorbed moisture. Doors, vanities, and trim components are not simply cosmetic; they can trap water against framing and drywall, slowing the drying process and creating conditions where secondary damage can develop over time.

On a typical loss like this, a restoration crew will remove fixtures and millwork carefully so that the underlying structure can be assessed and dried properly. This also allows moisture meters to reach surfaces that would otherwise be inaccessible. The goal at this stage is not demolition for its own sake but controlled access so that drying equipment can do its job effectively and so that the scope of replacement materials can be accurately documented.

In Lavoy's project, the notes indicate that the bathroom closet door, bathroom door, and vanity were removed for exactly this reason, allowing the team to take readings throughout the space and identify where moisture was still present.

## Targeted Drying and Monitoring: How Moisture Stabilization Works

Once access is established, the drying phase begins. In a standard water damage restoration project, technicians use moisture meters and thermal imaging to map wet areas, then place drying equipment, such as dehumidifiers and air movers, in positions that will draw moisture out of building materials efficiently.

What makes this phase more complex than simply running a dehumidifier is the monitoring component. Drying is not a one-visit process. On a typical loss, a restoration team will return on multiple days to re-meter affected materials, adjust equipment placement if needed, and confirm that readings are trending toward acceptable dry standards. This ongoing documentation matters for two reasons: it protects the homeowner by ensuring the space is genuinely dry before reconstruction begins, and it creates a record that supports any insurance claim.

In this project, the notes describe localized wet spots identified after rainfall, a dehumidifier placed for targeted drying, and additional monitoring visits with re-metering until acceptable levels were achieved. This is a textbook example of how the monitoring phase should work: equipment placed where the data points, and drying confirmed through repeated measurement rather than assumption.

## Scope Documentation and Preparing for Reconstruction

Before a restoration project hands off to reconstruction, every damaged component needs to be measured and documented. On a typical loss, this means creating a detailed scope that lists materials requiring replacement, including trim items like baseboard and casing, doors, and any drywall sections that could not be dried in place.

This documentation step is important for homeowners coordinating with insurance adjusters, as it provides a clear, itemized record of what was affected. It also ensures that the reconstruction phase can proceed without surprises, since the contractor taking over knows exactly what needs to be replaced and to what dimensions.

The project notes for Lavoy reflect this process: damaged components were measured and documented, the space was cleaned and stabilized, and the customer coordinated with an exterior siding contractor to address the contributing water entry point. Addressing the source of intrusion is a critical final step, because even the most thorough drying work will not prevent future damage if the exterior vulnerability remains open.
