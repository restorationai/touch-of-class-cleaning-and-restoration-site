The honest answer to choosing a restoration company: vet them *before* disaster strikes, because when water is pouring through your ceiling at 11 p.m. or smoke residue is soaking into your drywall, you will not have time to comparison-shop. If you are already in the middle of a loss, skip to the checklist below. If you are researching ahead of time — smart move. The difference between a contractor who makes your insurance claim smooth and one who makes it a nightmare often comes down to five things that most homeowners never think to ask.

## Why the Restoration Industry Attracts Bad Actors

Restoration work sits at an uncomfortable intersection: desperate customers, large insurance payouts, and almost no licensing barrier in many states. South Dakota does not require a specialty contractor's license specifically for water or fire restoration, which means anyone with a van and a moisture meter can hang a shingle. That is not a knock on the industry — there are excellent restoration crews operating in Sioux Falls and across the region — but it does mean the burden of vetting falls on you.

Storm chasers are the most visible version of this problem. After a hail event or a major flood, out-of-state crews flood the market, sign assignment-of-benefits contracts before you have read the fine print, and are gone before the final invoice clears. Local contractors are not immune to bad practices either. Knowing what to look for protects you regardless of who shows up at your door.

## The Five Questions That Actually Separate Good Contractors from Bad Ones

Ask these before you sign anything — or even before you let someone start pulling up flooring.

**1. Are you IICRC-certified, and which standards do you follow?**
The Institute of Inspection, Cleaning and Restoration Certification sets the technical standards for this industry. The S500 standard governs water damage drying; the S520 governs mold remediation. A certified technician knows the difference between Category 1 water (clean supply line) and Category 3 (sewage backup), and they will treat your home accordingly. Ask for the certification number. Legitimate firms will give it to you without hesitation.

**2. Do you carry general liability and workers' compensation insurance?**
If an uninsured worker is injured in your home, you may be liable. If an uninsured contractor causes additional damage, you may be left holding the bill. Ask for a certificate of insurance, not just a verbal confirmation. A reputable company will email it to you before the crew arrives.

**3. Will you bill my insurance carrier directly, and can I see the scope before you submit it?**
Direct insurance billing is standard for established restoration companies — it protects you from fronting tens of thousands of dollars out of pocket. More importantly, you have the right to review the scope of work before it goes to your adjuster. If a contractor resists showing you the line-item estimate, that is a red flag.

**4. How long have you been operating in this market, and can you provide local references?**
A company that has been working in Sioux Falls for years has a track record you can verify. Ask for two or three references from jobs similar to yours — a finished basement flood, a kitchen fire, a crawl space mold remediation. Call them. Ask specifically whether the final bill matched the estimate and whether the drying or remediation passed the post-work inspection.

**5. What does your post-work verification look like?**
For water damage, this means final moisture readings documented in a drying log — not just a technician's word that the structure is dry. For mold remediation, it means a clearance test performed by a third party, not the same company that did the work. For fire and smoke damage, it means an odor test after the HEPA vacuuming and sealing is complete. If a contractor cannot describe their verification process, they probably do not have one.

## What to Watch Out for When You Are Already in Crisis Mode

If a pipe has burst or a fire just happened, your judgment is compromised by stress. These are the moments contractors with bad practices count on.

- **Do not sign an assignment-of-benefits agreement without reading it.** This document transfers your insurance claim rights to the contractor. Some are legitimate; some are written to lock you into inflated pricing with no exit.
- **Do not let anyone start demolition before your adjuster has documented the loss** — unless water is actively spreading and you need emergency extraction. Photograph everything yourself before any material is removed.
- **Do not pay a large deposit upfront.** A reasonable mobilization payment is normal; paying 50% before a single piece of equipment is on-site is not.
- **Do not assume the cheapest bid is the safest choice.** Restoration is not like repainting a bedroom. Underdrying a water loss leads to mold colonization — which can begin within 24 to 48 hours in the right conditions — and that secondary damage will cost far more than the original repair.

## How to Read an Estimate (Even If You Are Not a Contractor)

Most restoration estimates are generated in Xactimate, the same software your insurance adjuster uses. That is actually useful — it means the line items are standardized and you can look them up. A few things to check:

- **Is the affected area measured correctly?** Pull up your home's floor plan or measure the rooms yourself. Inflated square footage is one of the most common padding tactics.
- **Are the line items specific to your loss?** A water damage estimate for a finished basement should list equipment (air movers, dehumidifiers), labor hours, and specific materials removed. Vague entries like "remediation services" without itemization are a problem.
- **Does the estimate include a drying protocol?** For water damage, the number of air movers and dehumidifiers should be calculated based on the affected square footage and the material types involved — not just a flat fee.

For fire and smoke damage, the estimate should distinguish between surfaces that need cleaning versus surfaces that need replacement. Smoke residue penetrates porous materials differently depending on the type of fire (protein fires from cooking smell different and require different treatment than structural fires), and a good estimate reflects that specificity.

## When the Job Is Done: How to Know the Work Actually Worked

The end of a restoration job is not when the crew packs up — it is when the structure passes verification.

For **water damage restoration**, ask for the final drying log showing moisture readings at or below the baseline readings taken in unaffected areas of the home. If readings are not documented, the drying is not verified.

For **mold remediation**, a post-remediation verification (PRV) air sample should be collected by an independent industrial hygienist — not the remediation company. Spore counts in the treated area should be at or below outdoor ambient levels.

For **fire damage restoration**, walk the space yourself after the cleaning is complete. Run your hand along surfaces in areas that were smoke-affected. If you can still smell smoke residue or see soot in corners and on window frames, the cleaning is not finished.

Keep copies of all documentation — drying logs, air quality reports, before-and-after photos — because your insurance company may request them, and they are valuable if you ever sell the property.
