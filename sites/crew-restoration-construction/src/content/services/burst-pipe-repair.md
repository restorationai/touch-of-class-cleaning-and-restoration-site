When a pipe lets go inside a wall, under a slab, or in a crawlspace, the damage rarely announces itself the way a visible leak does. Homeowners in Sioux Falls often notice it first as a hissing or dripping sound behind drywall, a spike on their water bill, or a soft spot in the flooring days after a hard freeze. By the time water is visible, it has usually already traveled along joists, wicked up baseboards, or pooled under subfloor where it can sit unnoticed for hours.

## What Burst Pipe Cleanup and Repair actually involves

A burst pipe response starts with stopping the water at the source, which usually means shutting off the main or an isolation valve before any cleanup begins. From there the work splits into two tracks that have to happen together: locating and addressing the actual pipe failure (often in coordination with a plumber), and dealing with the water that already escaped into the structure. That second part covers extraction from flooring and wall cavities, removal of saturated drywall or insulation where drying won't bring it back, and setting drying equipment calibrated to the materials involved. Depending on how long the break went undetected and what it's touching, a straightforward job can wrap in a few days, while a slab leak or a break that ran unnoticed overnight can take longer and involve more demolition.

## Our process

1. **Locate and isolate the source.** Before any water is removed, the break point gets identified, whether that's a supply line behind a vanity, a copper joint in a crawlspace, or a water line break under the slab, and the water supply is shut down to stop active flow.
2. **Extract standing and trapped water.** Pumps and extraction tools pull water from visible pooling first, then target water trapped under flooring, inside wall cavities, and along baseboards where it collects and lingers.
3. **Map the moisture before touching anything.** Moisture meters and, where useful, thermal imaging identify exactly how far the water traveled, since pipe breaks frequently push water further into a structure than the visible stain suggests.
4. **Set structural drying and monitor daily.** Air movers and dehumidifiers are placed based on the moisture map, drying is tracked against IICRC S500 benchmarks, and equipment stays until materials read dry, not just until they look dry.
5. **Repair or rebuild affected materials.** Drywall, trim, flooring, and cabinetry that couldn't be saved are documented, removed, and rebuilt once the structure is confirmed dry.

## What separates a good burst pipe response from a bad one

The most common miss is drying the surface and stopping there. Water from a pipe break travels along the path of least resistance, which usually means down inside a wall cavity or across a subfloor, well past where the drywall looks wet. A crew that only dries what's visible leaves saturated material behind the baseboard or under the flooring, and that's exactly where mold can start colonizing within 24 to 48 hours in a warm, humid cavity.

Insurance adjusters are typically looking for two things: clear documentation that the loss was sudden and accidental (a pipe burst) rather than gradual (a slow seep from corrosion, which many policies handle differently), and moisture readings that show the drying actually reached target levels before the space was closed back up. A written scope, photos of the failure point, and daily moisture logs matter more to a claim than how fast the fans got turned on.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Sioux Falls winters bring stretches of hard freeze that put real stress on plumbing in crawlspaces, garages, and exterior walls that aren't fully conditioned. Homes built before the 1980s often still run galvanized or cast iron supply lines, both of which corrode from the inside and are more prone to sudden failure than modern copper or PEX. A cold snap after a mild stretch is a common trigger, since pipes that were fine one week can freeze and split the next once temperatures drop fast.
