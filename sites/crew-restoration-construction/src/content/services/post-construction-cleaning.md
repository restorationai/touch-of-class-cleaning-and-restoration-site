The contractors are gone, the permits are closed, and the space looks almost finished — except for the fine white drywall dust coating every horizontal surface, the adhesive smears on the new hardwood, the grout haze fogging the tile, and the construction debris still tucked into corners. That gap between "done building" and "ready to occupy" is exactly where post-construction cleaning lives. It's not a standard house cleaning, and it's not something a crew with mops and paper towels can handle well. The residues left behind by construction are chemically and physically different from everyday dirt, and treating them wrong damages the very finishes you just paid to install.

## What post-construction and specialty cleaning actually involves

Post-construction cleaning is typically broken into phases that match the construction schedule. A **rough clean** happens mid-project — pulling out large debris, sweeping up aggregate, clearing the site so trades can keep working. The **final clean** is what most homeowners and property managers think of: a systematic pass through every finished surface before move-in or handoff. A **touch-up clean** may follow once furniture is staged and punch-list work is complete.

The work involves more than vacuuming. Drywall compound dust is extremely fine — particles small enough to pass through standard vacuum filters and resettle on surfaces you just wiped. Proper post-construction cleanup uses HEPA-filtered vacuums rated for fine particulate, followed by damp-wiping in a deliberate sequence (high to low, back to front) so dust doesn't migrate to already-cleaned areas. Window glass gets scraped of paint overspray and construction adhesive with razor tools before cleaning solution is applied. Tile and stone surfaces require grout haze remover matched to the grout type — acidic cleaners on the wrong stone etch the surface permanently. Appliances, HVAC registers, and light fixtures are cleaned and inspected for debris before power is restored.

Specialty cleaning under this umbrella also includes post-renovation deep cleaning after partial remodels, move-out cleans on heavily soiled properties, and detailed cleaning scopes tied to insurance restoration projects where documentation of pre-occupancy condition matters.

A thorough final clean on a new single-family home typically takes a crew of two to four people one to two full days, depending on square footage and finish complexity.

## Our process

1. **Site walk and scope confirmation.** Before any cleaning begins, the team walks the completed space with the general contractor or property owner to identify finish types (natural stone, engineered hardwood, painted millwork, specialty tile), flag areas with active punch-list work still pending, and note any surfaces that require product-specific treatment. This prevents using the wrong chemistry on a delicate finish.

2. **Debris removal and HEPA vacuuming.** All construction debris — cut material, packaging, fasteners — is removed first. Then every surface, including inside cabinets, closets, window sills, and HVAC registers, is vacuumed with HEPA-filtered equipment. This step happens before any wet cleaning so loosened dust isn't spread by moisture.

3. **Surface-specific cleaning and stain removal.** Windows, mirrors, and glass are scraped and cleaned. Grout haze is treated with the appropriate remover for the tile and grout combination. Paint overspray, adhesive residue, and caulk smears are removed from hardware, fixtures, and flooring using tools and solvents matched to the substrate. Countertops and stone surfaces are cleaned and, where specified, sealed.

4. **Detail and fixture cleaning.** Light fixtures, switch plates, door hardware, plumbing fixtures, and appliances are wiped down and polished. Interior surfaces of cabinets and drawers are cleaned. HVAC vents and returns are cleared of construction dust before the system is run.

5. **Final inspection and photo documentation.** The space is walked again surface by surface. On insurance restoration projects or commercial handoffs, the team photographs finished surfaces to document pre-occupancy condition — a record that protects both the contractor and the property owner if disputes arise later.

## What separates a good post-construction clean from a bad one

The most common mistake is sequencing. Crews that mop floors before HEPA-vacuuming ceilings and walls simply redistribute fine drywall dust onto wet surfaces, where it bonds and becomes harder to remove. The second most common mistake is using the wrong products on sensitive finishes — acidic tile cleaners on marble, solvent-based degreasers on engineered hardwood, or abrasive pads on brushed hardware. These errors show up immediately as etching, cloudiness, or scratching, and they're often not covered under the contractor's warranty because the damage occurred post-completion.

On insurance restoration projects, adjusters and project managers look for documented surface conditions at handoff. A post-construction clean without photo documentation leaves the restoration contractor exposed if a homeowner later claims a finish was damaged during the project. Experienced crews treat the final walk as a liability document, not just a quality check.

Less obvious but equally important: construction dust inside HVAC systems. If registers aren't cleaned and the system is run before the final clean, fine particulate circulates through the ductwork and resettles on every surface in the structure — undoing hours of work and potentially requiring duct cleaning as a separate scope.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Sioux Falls winters affect post-construction cleaning in a few practical ways. New construction or renovation completed between November and March often sits with windows sealed and minimal ventilation, which concentrates fine drywall dust and off-gassing from new materials. When the structure is first opened and heated, that dust becomes airborne again — which is why a thorough HEPA vacuum pass before any HVAC operation matters more in winter handoffs than in summer ones.

South Dakota's low winter humidity also means wood floors and millwork installed during dry months may show gaps or movement once the home is occupied and humidity stabilizes. Noting these conditions during the final clean walk protects everyone from post-occupancy disputes about finish quality.
