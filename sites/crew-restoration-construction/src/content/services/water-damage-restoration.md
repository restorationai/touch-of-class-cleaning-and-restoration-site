Standing water doesn't wait. Within the first hour after a pipe bursts or a washing machine hose lets go, water is already wicking into drywall, soaking into subfloor panels, and beginning to swell door frames. Within 24 to 48 hours, the conditions for mold colonization are in place. Water damage restoration is a race against that clock — and the margin between a clean, fully dried structure and a home that needs gut-and-rebuild is often measured in how quickly the right equipment gets on-site.

## What Water Damage Restoration actually involves

Water damage restoration is not mopping up and running a fan. At its core, it's a controlled drying science. Technicians use truck-mounted or portable extractors to remove standing water, then deploy industrial air movers and low-grain refrigerant (LGR) or desiccant dehumidifiers to pull moisture out of materials that look dry but aren't. Thermal imaging cameras and calibrated moisture meters map the full extent of the saturation — because water travels, and it almost always travels further than the visible damage suggests.

Timeline varies by category and material. A Category 1 loss (clean water from a supply line) in a single room with hardwood floors might dry in three to five days. A Category 2 loss (gray water from an overflowing dishwasher or toilet overflow) touching carpet, pad, and OSB subfloor can run seven to ten days of active drying. Category 3 losses — sewage backups, floodwater — require contaminated material removal before drying even begins, and the timeline extends accordingly.

Throughout the process, moisture readings are logged daily. Drying is not complete when the surface feels dry — it's complete when the materials reach their documented dry standard.

## Our process

1. **Emergency water removal.** The first priority is stopping active water intrusion (if the source hasn't been shut off) and extracting standing water. Truck-mounted extractors can remove hundreds of gallons per hour. Portable units reach areas a truck mount can't — crawl spaces, finished basements, upper floors.

2. **Moisture mapping and documentation.** Before a single dehumidifier is placed, technicians read every affected wall cavity, floor assembly, and ceiling with moisture meters and thermal imaging. This creates a baseline map that drives the drying plan and documents the scope for your insurance carrier.

3. **Structural drying with monitored equipment placement.** Air movers are positioned to create a vortex effect across wet surfaces; dehumidifiers are sized to the cubic footage of the drying chamber. Equipment isn't set and forgotten — readings are taken every 24 hours and equipment is repositioned or scaled back as materials dry toward their target.

4. **Category-appropriate containment and material removal.** For Category 2 and Category 3 losses, saturated porous materials — carpet, pad, drywall below the flood cut line — are removed and disposed of before drying begins. Containment barriers may be erected to protect unaffected areas from cross-contamination.

5. **Final moisture verification and clearance.** When all materials reach their dry standard, a final moisture map is documented. This report goes to you and your adjuster as proof of completion — and it's the foundation for any reconstruction scope that follows.

## What separates a good water damage response from a bad one

The most common failure point in water damage restoration is incomplete drying — and it almost always comes from inadequate moisture mapping at the start. Technicians who skip thermal imaging or rely on visual inspection alone routinely miss water that has migrated behind baseboards, into wall cavities, or under hardwood flooring. That hidden moisture doesn't disappear; it becomes a mold problem that surfaces weeks later, often after the homeowner thought everything was resolved.

A second common failure is under-sizing equipment. One residential dehumidifier from a hardware store is not a substitute for a properly calculated drying system. LGR dehumidifiers pull moisture at conditions that consumer units can't match, and the grain depression they achieve is the difference between a five-day dry and a twelve-day dry — or no dry at all.

Insurance adjusters look for daily moisture logs, equipment placement records, and a documented dry standard. Without that paper trail, supplements and reinspection requests get complicated. A thorough documentation package protects the homeowner as much as it protects the contractor.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Sioux Falls and the surrounding eastern South Dakota region see conditions that drive water damage claims in predictable patterns. Winter freeze-thaw cycles — particularly the hard freezes that hit uninsulated exterior walls and garage-adjacent plumbing — are a leading cause of burst pipe losses from November through March. Spring snowmelt combined with saturated soil pushes groundwater against foundation walls and through window wells, making sump pump failures a high-frequency event in March and April. Summer severe weather brings hail-damaged roofs that leak for weeks before the damage is discovered, leading to attic and ceiling losses that are already well along by the time a homeowner calls.

Homes in older Sioux Falls neighborhoods — particularly those built before 1980 in the core city — often have original cast iron drain lines that are prone to root intrusion and backup, and plaster-over-lath wall assemblies that hold moisture differently than modern drywall. Both factors affect drying timelines and should be accounted for in any restoration plan.
