A kitchen fire that stayed out in twenty minutes can still leave a house smelling like it burned for hours. Smoke doesn't stop at the room of origin: it rides HVAC ductwork into bedrooms, settles into drywall pores, and bonds to anything with a slight electrostatic charge, cabinets, carpet fibers, even the inside of light fixtures. If the odor is still there after the fire department leaves and the flames are out, that's residue and off-gassing, not a smell that airs out on its own.

## What Smoke Damage Restoration actually involves

Smoke damage restoration is a separate discipline from fire damage repair, even though the two often happen on the same job. Where fire damage deals with charred structure, smoke damage restoration deals with what the smoke did to everything the fire never touched: the film of residue on walls and ceilings, the odor trapped in porous materials, and the corrosive byproducts that settle on metal surfaces and start pitting them within days. The work involves identifying the soot type (dry protein soot from a kitchen fire behaves very differently than the thick, oily synthetic soot from a burning couch or electronics), cleaning affected surfaces with the matched chemistry, and sealing or replacing materials that held onto the odor past the point of cleaning. Equipment ranges from HEPA vacuums and dry chemical sponges to thermal foggers and hydroxyl generators, depending on how deep the residue penetrated.

## Our process

1. **Soot and residue assessment.** We identify soot type, walk every affected room including attic and duct runs, and document pre-existing conditions for the insurance file.
2. **Source and contents triage.** Salvageable contents are separated from total losses, and porous items with heavy odor penetration are flagged for specialized cleaning or disposal.
3. **Surface cleaning matched to residue type.** Dry sponging for light protein soot, wet cleaning and degreasers for synthetic residue, and HVAC cleaning where smoke traveled through the duct system.
4. **Odor neutralization.** Thermal fogging or hydroxyl treatment breaks down odor at the molecular level rather than masking it, applied after surfaces are cleaned, not before.
5. **Sealing and clearance.** Any surface that still carries odor after cleaning gets sealed with an odor-blocking primer before repainting, and we walk the property again before calling it closed.

## What separates a good Smoke Damage Restoration response from a bad one

The most common mistake is treating smoke damage like a paint job: repaint over residue, spray an air freshener, call it done. Odor comes back within weeks because the source, the residue itself, was never removed, just covered. A second mistake is skipping the HVAC system. If the furnace or AC ran during or after the fire, soot gets pulled through the return ducts and redistributed every time the system cycles, which is why odor sometimes seems to reappear in rooms nowhere near the fire.

Adjusters look closely at documentation here: photos of residue before cleaning, a room-by-room scope that separates cleanable surfaces from replace-and-seal items, and a clear record of what equipment ran and for how long. Cleaning done per IICRC S700 fire and smoke restoration guidelines, with soot type identified before a cleaning method is chosen, holds up better under adjuster review than a generic wipe-down invoice.

## What does Smoke Damage Restoration cost?

Cost depends heavily on soot type, how far the smoke traveled, and whether the HVAC system needs cleaning or duct sealing in addition to surface work. Light residue in a single room costs far less to resolve than whole-house odor penetration from a synthetic fire, and every loss gets a written scope from Crew Restoration & Construction before work begins so there are no surprises once the job is underway. Homeowners insurance typically covers smoke damage restoration when it results from a covered fire loss, though policy language and deductibles vary.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Sioux Falls winters mean furnaces and forced-air systems run for months at a stretch, so any smoke event between October and April has more opportunity to spread through ductwork before anyone notices the odor in a distant room. Closed windows in cold weather also mean less passive airing-out, which is part of why odor from a small fire can seem to linger longer here than it would in a milder climate. Wildfire smoke drifting in from regional fire activity is a different, lower-intensity version of the same problem: it settles into HVAC filters and soft materials and can leave a smoky background odor in a home even without a fire on the property.
