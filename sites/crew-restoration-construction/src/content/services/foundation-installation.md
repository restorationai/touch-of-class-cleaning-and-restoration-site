A foundation that's cracking, settling, or was never engineered for a full basement isn't a patch job. It's a structural problem that gets worse every freeze-thaw cycle until the footing itself is replaced or extended. Homeowners planning an addition, converting a crawl space, or dealing with a foundation that's failed outright need a poured or engineered system installed correctly the first time, not a stopgap that shifts again next spring.

## What Foundation Installation actually involves

Foundation installation covers new footings and walls for additions, detached structures, egress conversions, and full replacement of a failed foundation under an existing home. The work starts below grade: excavation to the required frost depth, formed footings, reinforced concrete walls or slab, and a drainage system that keeps groundwater away from the structure. Timelines vary with scope. A small addition footing might be poured and cured within a week or two. A full basement replacement under an occupied house, where the structure has to be temporarily supported while the old foundation comes out, runs longer and is sequenced in stages so the home stays structurally sound throughout.

## Our process

1. **Site evaluation and soil review.** We look at the existing foundation condition, drainage patterns, and soil type before recommending a system, since Sioux Falls area clay soils behave very differently wet versus dry.
2. **Engineering and permitting.** Foundation work requires stamped structural plans and a permit through the local building department. We handle the paperwork and coordinate inspections so the job closes out clean.
3. **Excavation and forming.** Footings are dug to the required frost depth and formed with rebar placed per the engineered spec, not eyeballed. This is the step that determines whether the foundation heaves in year three.
4. **Pour, cure, and waterproof.** Concrete is poured, cured under controlled conditions, and sealed with a waterproofing membrane and drain tile before backfill.
5. **Backfill, compaction, and final inspection.** Backfill is compacted in lifts, not dumped in all at once, and the job is signed off by the inspector before we consider it finished.

## What separates a good foundation installation from a bad one

The most common failure point is depth: a footing poured a few inches short of the frost line will heave once the ground freezes and thaws around it, and the cracking that follows can take years to show up as a serious problem. Under-reinforced walls, missing or improperly lapped rebar, and skipped compaction testing on backfill are the next most common shortcuts, all of which are invisible once the job is buried. A stamped engineering plan and a documented compaction test are what a competent inspector, and a sharp insurance adjuster reviewing a related claim, will ask for first. When a new foundation ties into an existing structure, like an addition footing meeting an original foundation wall, the connection detail matters as much as the concrete itself; a poorly tied joint is where future cracking almost always starts.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Sioux Falls sits on clay-heavy soil that expands when saturated and shrinks when it dries, which is part of why footing depth and drainage design matter more here than in sandier soil. Frost depth in this region runs deep enough that shallow pours are a common cause of the heaving and cracking we see in older or hastily built additions. Spring, when the ground has thawed but before heavy rains saturate the soil, and fall are generally the most workable windows for excavation and pour. Winter installation is possible with insulated forms and heated enclosures, but it adds cost and time to the schedule.
