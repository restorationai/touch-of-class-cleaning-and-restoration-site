When a burst pipe floods a warehouse floor at 3 a.m., or a grease fire tears through a manufacturing plant's production wing, the scale of the problem is nothing like a residential loss. Standing water in a 40,000-square-foot facility can wick into concrete slabs, corrode steel racking, compromise electrical conduit, and halt production lines within hours. Industrial restoration in Macomb requires equipment, sequencing, and coordination that residential crews simply are not built for.

## What industrial restoration actually involves

Industrial water damage, fire damage, and structural losses play out differently than residential ones in almost every dimension. Concrete floors absorb moisture slowly but hold it for weeks. Steel structural members corrode from the inside of wall cavities before surface rust appears. Smoke from synthetic materials used in manufacturing and warehousing, such as plastics, rubber, and coatings, leaves a different residue profile than wood smoke and requires different chemical neutralization. Mold colonization in a facility with poor air circulation can spread across a large footprint before it is visible.

Industrial restoration work typically involves commercial-grade extraction units capable of moving hundreds of gallons per hour, desiccant dehumidification systems suited to large open spans, air movers positioned in calculated patterns across concrete and epoxy-coated floors, and thermal imaging to locate moisture behind insulated metal panels. For fire losses, industrial restoration includes soot characterization by material type, odor neutralization through thermal fogging or hydroxyl generation, and coordinated contents pack-out for salvageable equipment and inventory.

Timeline varies by loss type and facility size, but industrial structural drying typically runs longer than residential drying because of slab thickness, insulation density, and the sheer volume of affected cubic footage.

## Our process

1. **Site assessment and safety staging.** Before any equipment moves in, the facility is evaluated for structural integrity, electrical hazards, and air quality. In a fire loss, this includes identifying areas where ceilings or mezzanine decking may be compromised. In a water loss, it includes locating the source and confirming it is controlled.

2. **Containment and source isolation.** For Category 2 or Category 3 water intrusions, affected zones are contained to prevent cross-contamination of clean inventory, equipment, or office areas. For fire losses, containment limits soot migration into unaffected production areas.

3. **Extraction, debris removal, and initial drying setup.** Industrial extraction equipment removes standing water from floor drains, low points, and pits. Debris, damaged insulation, and unsalvageable materials are removed to allow drying equipment to work against the actual structure rather than saturated contents. High-capacity dehumidifiers and air movers are positioned based on the facility's layout and airflow characteristics.

4. **Moisture monitoring and documentation.** Concrete slabs, block walls, and steel-framed insulated panels are monitored at regular intervals using calibrated moisture meters and thermal imaging. Readings are logged and provided to the property owner and insurance carrier as part of the claim documentation package.

5. **Restoration and return to operations.** Once the structure meets drying targets, restoration work begins: repairing damaged wall panels, replacing insulation, restoring flooring surfaces, and coordinating with mechanical and electrical trades where needed. The goal is to return the facility to operational condition with as little disruption to the production schedule as possible.

## What separates a good industrial restoration response from a bad one

The most common failure in industrial water damage response is treating a large facility like a scaled-up residential job. Residential desiccants and air movers are undersized for open warehouse spans. Residential moisture readings taken at drywall do not translate to concrete slab readings. A crew that does not understand how moisture moves through an insulated metal building panel will pull equipment too early and leave saturated insulation behind a dry-reading surface.

For industrial fire damage, the critical distinction is soot characterization. Synthetic materials common in manufacturing facilities produce wet, oily soot that bonds aggressively to metal surfaces and requires alkaline cleaning agents, not the dry-sponge methods used on protein soot. Skipping this step and applying the wrong chemistry can permanently etch or stain equipment and structural steel.

Insurance adjusters on commercial losses look for moisture logs with GPS-stamped readings, photo documentation of affected materials before removal, a written drying protocol, and a clear scope of work that separates emergency mitigation from restoration. Gaps in this documentation are the most common reason commercial claims are delayed or disputed.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Macomb County's freeze-thaw cycle is one of the most consistent sources of industrial water losses in the region. Rooftop HVAC units, fire suppression lines in unheated storage bays, and overhead sprinkler systems in loading dock areas are all vulnerable when temperatures drop sharply in January and February. Facilities with older construction, common in Macomb's industrial corridors, often have inadequate pipe insulation in exterior walls and roof penetrations. Spring snowmelt combined with clay-heavy soils in the area can also overwhelm floor drains and below-grade utility spaces in older manufacturing buildings.
