## What storm damage actually involves

A severe storm doesn't just break windows and scatter shingles. It opens your home's envelope to water intrusion, structural stress, and secondary damage that compounds by the hour. A tree limb punching through a roof at 2 a.m. exposes attic insulation and ceiling drywall to rain before any crew can respond. Wind-driven water migrates behind siding, soaks wall cavities, and sits in places that look perfectly dry from the inside. The visible damage is rarely the whole story.

Storm damage restoration covers the full scope of what severe weather leaves behind: emergency tarping and board-up to stop active intrusion, debris removal, structural assessment, water extraction from wind-driven rain, and coordinated reconstruction of everything the storm tore open. It is not a single trade. A competent response pulls together roofing, carpentry, water mitigation, and sometimes mold prevention into one managed sequence.

## Our process

**1. Emergency stabilization**
Before any assessment form is filled out, the priority is stopping the bleeding. Open roof sections get tarped. Broken windows and doors get boarded. If a tree is still in contact with the structure, we document its position and work around it safely until removal is complete. This step determines how much secondary damage you avoid.

**2. Damage documentation and scope development**
Every affected surface is photographed, measured, and logged before a single piece of debris moves. Wind uplift, impact points, water migration paths, and structural deflection are all noted. This documentation is what your insurance adjuster will use to authorize the claim, and gaps here cost homeowners money later.

**3. Debris removal and structural assessment**
Tree limbs, displaced roofing materials, and shattered components are removed in a sequence that protects the structure underneath. Once the debris is clear, we assess framing, sheathing, and load-bearing elements for damage that isn't visible from the exterior. Compromised rafters and headers don't always announce themselves.

**4. Water intrusion mitigation**
Any storm that opened the building envelope likely introduced water. We use moisture meters and thermal imaging to trace where wind-driven rain traveled inside wall assemblies and above ceilings. Wet insulation is removed. Affected cavities are dried with commercial air movers and dehumidifiers before reconstruction begins, because sealing wet framing is what creates mold problems weeks later.

**5. Reconstruction and closeout**
Repairs follow the documented scope: roofing, siding, windows, framing, insulation, drywall, and finish work. The goal is returning the home to pre-loss condition, not a patchwork of mismatched materials. Final inspection confirms that the building envelope is fully closed and watertight.

## What separates a good storm damage response from a bad one

The most common failure in storm damage work is treating it as a roofing job. A roofer who patches the penetration but never checks the ceiling cavity below it leaves saturated insulation in place. That insulation holds moisture against the sheathing for weeks. By the time the homeowner notices a stain or a smell, mold has already colonized the framing.

A second common gap is incomplete documentation before debris removal. Once a tree is cut and hauled away, the impact geometry is gone. Insurance adjusters rely on that evidence to authorize structural repairs beyond the obvious. Experienced storm restoration crews photograph impact craters, stress fractures in rafters, and displaced flashing before the chainsaw starts.

Insurance adjusters also look closely at the line between storm-caused damage and pre-existing deterioration. Worn shingles that failed because of age rather than wind load are handled differently than shingles that were torn off by a documented storm event. A thorough scope distinguishes the two clearly, which protects the claim.

Finally, wind-driven rain behaves differently than plumbing water. It enters at angles, travels along framing members, and pools in places that defy gravity logic. Crews trained only on plumbing leaks sometimes miss it entirely.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Macomb County sits in a corridor that sees lake-effect weather systems from Lake Huron and Lake St. Clair, which means fast-moving storms with high straight-line winds are a regular feature of spring and early summer. The region's housing stock includes a significant number of homes built in the 1950s through 1980s with older roofing underlayment and aging siding profiles that are more vulnerable to uplift than newer construction.

Fall storms in southeast Michigan arrive with less warning than summer thunderstorms and often coincide with leaves clogging gutters, which forces roof runoff to back up under flashing. Ice storm events in late fall and early spring can add weight loading that compromises already-stressed roof sections. Timing a repair before the next weather system moves through matters here.
