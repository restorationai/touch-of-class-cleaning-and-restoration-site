When a pipe bursts behind a kitchen cabinet or a house fire fills every room with soot, the structure isn't the only casualty. Furniture, documents, electronics, clothing, and family heirlooms absorb water, smoke odor, and contamination just as fast as drywall does, often faster. Content recovery is the work of triaging, cleaning, and restoring those personal belongings instead of writing them all off as a total loss, and the window to save them is frequently measured in hours, not days.

## What Content Recovery actually involves

Content recovery covers everything inside the structure that isn't nailed down: upholstered furniture, rugs, electronics, books and paper documents, clothing, kitchenware, and sentimental items like photographs or heirlooms. Depending on the type of loss, items are either cleaned on-site or packed out to an off-site facility for specialized treatment. Water-damaged wood furniture needs controlled drying to prevent warping and veneer separation. Smoke-affected fabrics need ozone or hydroxyl treatment to break down odor molecules rather than just mask them. Electronics exposed to water or soot often need corrosion control within a narrow timeframe before internal components start to oxidize. The goal throughout is documentation: every item is inventoried, photographed, and tracked so the homeowner and the insurance carrier both know exactly what happened to it.

## Our process

1. **Inventory and triage.** Every affected item is photographed and logged, then sorted into categories: restorable on-site, restorable off-site, and non-salvageable. This inventory becomes part of the claim documentation.
2. **Pack-out and transport.** Items that need off-site cleaning are boxed, labeled, and moved to a controlled facility so structural drying and repairs can proceed in the home without contents in the way.
3. **Category-specific cleaning.** Textiles go through wet or dry cleaning depending on contamination level. Hard contents are wiped down and sanitized. Electronics are assessed separately, often by a specialist, before any power is reapplied.
4. **Odor and contamination treatment.** Smoke odor and Category 2/3 water contamination require more than surface cleaning. Items may need thermal fogging, ozone chambers, or enzymatic treatment depending on what they were exposed to.
5. **Return and reconciliation.** Restored items are returned and checked against the original inventory. Anything determined non-salvageable is documented with photos and reasoning, which supports the insurance claim rather than leaving it as a dispute.

## What separates a good content recovery response from a bad one

The most common mistake is treating contents like an afterthought to structural drying. A crew that packs boxes without a real inventory leaves the homeowner with no way to prove what was lost, and leaves the adjuster with no basis to approve a claim. Another frequent failure is cleaning items too late: upholstery and wood furniture that sit wet for more than 24 to 48 hours often develop mold colonies inside the cushion or joinery that surface cleaning won't reach. Electronics are another area where corners get cut. Turning on a water-exposed device to see if it "still works" can cause a short that destroys it permanently; it needs to be assessed powered-off first. Adjusters generally want to see itemized documentation, not a lump-sum estimate, and they want photo evidence showing condition before cleaning was attempted. A response that skips this step tends to get contested, which slows down the homeowner's payout.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Macomb's older housing stock, much of it built mid-century with basements that see seasonal groundwater intrusion, means contents stored in lower levels are frequently the first casualty of a spring thaw or a summer storm surge. Humidity swings through a Michigan summer also shorten the window before wet upholstery or stored documents start to mildew, so items pulled from a flooded basement need faster triage than the same items might need in a drier climate.
