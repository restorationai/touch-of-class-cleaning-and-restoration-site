The honest answer: most water damage restoration takes **3 to 5 days for drying alone**, and the full process, drying, repairs, and any necessary mold prevention, commonly runs **1 to 3 weeks** from start to finish. That range is wide because it depends almost entirely on how much water got in, where it went, and how quickly the response started. A slow drip behind a cabinet wall behaves very differently than a burst supply line that ran for six hours while you were at work.

## Why the Timeline Varies So Much

Water doesn't stay where it lands. Within minutes of a significant leak, water is moving laterally through flooring, wicking upward into drywall, and pooling in low spots you can't see. The longer it sits, the deeper it migrates, and the deeper it migrates, the longer it takes to pull out.

A few factors that stretch or compress the timeline:

- **Category of water.** Clean water from a supply line is the fastest to remediate. Greywater (dishwasher overflow, washing machine backup) adds complexity. Sewage or floodwater, what the industry calls Category 3, requires protective equipment, antimicrobial treatment, and often the removal of porous materials that can't be safely dried in place.
- **How long it sat.** Water that's been sitting for 24 to 48 hours has already begun promoting microbial growth in organic materials like wood framing, drywall paper, and subflooring. That changes the scope from a drying job to a drying-plus-remediation job.
- **What it touched.** Concrete and tile dry faster than hardwood. Hardwood dries faster than engineered flooring with a closed backing. Plaster walls, common in older Macomb County homes built before the 1970s, hold moisture differently than paper-faced drywall and can read dry at the surface while the lath behind them stays saturated for days.
- **How much airflow and heat are available.** Michigan winters complicate drying. Cold air holds less moisture, which can actually help dehumidification, but if the structure is cold, materials dry more slowly. Restoration crews adjust equipment accordingly, but the physics still matter.

## The Drying Phase: What's Actually Happening

This is the part most homeowners don't fully understand, and it's the reason restoration takes longer than it feels like it should.

After standing water is extracted, the materials themselves are still saturated. Industrial air movers accelerate evaporation at the surface; commercial dehumidifiers pull that moisture out of the air before it can re-deposit elsewhere. Technicians take moisture readings, in the wall cavity, at the subfloor, in the framing, and track them against a drying goal specific to the material type.

A typical residential drying cycle runs **3 to 5 days**, with readings taken each day. If the numbers aren't moving fast enough, equipment gets repositioned or added. If a wall cavity isn't responding, it may need to be opened to allow direct airflow inside.

Drying is considered complete when moisture readings return to normal range for the material and the region's ambient conditions, not just when things feel dry to the touch. This distinction matters: flooring that feels firm underfoot can still hold enough moisture to buckle or grow mold over the following weeks if it's closed up too soon.

## What Happens After Drying

Once the structure is verified dry, the scope of repairs becomes clear. This is when the timeline branches significantly:

**Minor losses** (a small section of drywall, no flooring damage, no structural involvement) can move to repairs within a week of the initial event. A patch, prime, and paint job might be done in a day or two.

**Moderate losses** (partial flooring removal, multiple rooms affected, cabinetry or trim involved) typically take 1 to 2 weeks for repairs after drying is complete. Flooring materials often need to be ordered, which adds lead time.

**Major losses** (structural framing affected, extensive flooring, multiple trades involved, plumber, electrician, drywaller, flooring installer) can extend the repair phase to 4 to 8 weeks or longer, particularly if permits are required. In Macomb County, work that involves structural repairs or electrical modifications typically requires a permit through the local building department, and inspection scheduling adds time you can't control.

If mold is confirmed, either visually or through testing, remediation has to happen before any repairs begin. Mold remediation adds its own timeline depending on the extent of growth, the materials affected, and whether post-remediation clearance testing is required.

## What Slows Everything Down (and What You Can Control)

Some delays are outside your hands. Others aren't.

**Delays you can avoid:**

1. **Waiting to call.** Every hour of delay is additional saturation depth. If you discover water damage on a Friday evening and decide to wait until Monday, you've added two days of migration and microbial risk.
2. **Running fans from a hardware store instead of professional equipment.** Box fans move air but don't dehumidify. They can spread moisture to adjacent dry areas and create conditions where mold colonizes faster. They also don't generate the data, the daily moisture logs, that your insurance carrier will want.
3. **Closing off the affected area.** Sealing a wet room to contain the smell traps humidity and slows evaporation. Keep interior doors open unless a technician has set up containment for a specific reason.
4. **Replacing flooring or drywall before drying is confirmed complete.** This is the most common and most costly mistake. Trapping residual moisture under new materials leads to mold growth that won't be discovered until it's a much larger problem.

**Delays that are normal and expected:**

- Insurance adjuster scheduling (typically 2 to 5 business days for an in-person visit, though many carriers now accept photo documentation to start the claim faster)
- Material lead times for matching flooring or specialty trim
- Permit processing for structural or electrical work
- Subcontractor scheduling during busy seasons

## When to Call a Professional

If the affected area is larger than roughly 10 square feet, involves any category of water other than clean supply water, or has been sitting for more than a few hours, professional drying equipment and documentation are worth it, not just for the outcome, but for the insurance process.

Restoration professionals use calibrated moisture meters and thermal imaging to find water that isn't visible. They generate drying logs that document the progression to dry standard, which protects you if a mold claim surfaces later. They also know when a situation is beyond drying, when materials need to come out rather than be dried in place.
