Basement flooding in Macomb County isn't a matter of if, it's a matter of when. Southeast Michigan's clay-heavy soil drains poorly, spring snowmelt pushes the water table up fast, and a single heavy storm can overwhelm a sump pump that was working fine last week. The good news: most basement floods are preventable with a handful of targeted upgrades and seasonal habits. Below are eight concrete steps you can take before the next big rain rolls in off Lake St. Clair.

---

## 1. Test Your Sump Pump Before the Season Starts

Pour a five-gallon bucket of water into the sump pit. The pump should kick on within seconds, discharge the water, and shut off cleanly. If it hesitates, runs continuously, or makes a grinding noise, the impeller or float switch is likely failing. A sump pump that worked last October may have sat idle long enough for the float to stick or the motor capacitor to weaken.

While you're down there, check the discharge line too. In Michigan winters, the exterior end of that pipe can freeze solid, and a frozen discharge means water has nowhere to go even when the pump is running perfectly.

## 2. Install a Battery Backup (or Water-Powered) Sump System

The most common reason basements flood during a storm is a power outage, which is also exactly when your primary pump needs to be running. A battery backup unit sits in the same pit and activates automatically when the primary pump fails or when the water level rises faster than one pump can handle.

If your home runs on a municipal water supply, a water-powered backup is another option: it uses the pressure from your incoming water line to eject pit water without any electricity. Both systems add a meaningful layer of redundancy for under a few hundred dollars installed.

## 3. Regrade the Soil Around Your Foundation

Walk around your house after a rain and watch where puddles form. If water is pooling against the foundation wall, the grade is working against you. Soil should slope away from the house, roughly six inches of drop over the first ten feet. Over time, soil settles and that slope reverses, especially in the sandy-loam mix common in newer Macomb Township subdivisions.

Adding topsoil and tamping it into a gentle slope is a weekend project. It won't fix a failed waterproofing membrane, but it can meaningfully reduce the hydrostatic pressure pushing against your basement walls during a sustained rain event.

## 4. Clean and Extend Your Downspouts

A single downspout can discharge 12 gallons of water per minute during a heavy rain. If that water drops two feet from the foundation and stops, it's going straight down the wall and into the soil at the worst possible location. Extensions, the accordion-style plastic tubes, are inexpensive and push the discharge point four to six feet away from the house.

Also clean your gutters before spring and again in late fall. Clogged gutters overflow at the roofline and pour water directly against the foundation rather than routing it to the downspout. In neighborhoods with mature trees, much of Sterling Heights and older Macomb, this is a twice-a-year maintenance item, not optional.

## 5. Inspect Window Wells and Basement Windows

Below-grade window wells collect rainwater and debris. If the well doesn't have a gravel bed at least a foot deep, or if the drain at the bottom is clogged, water will pool until it finds the path of least resistance, which is usually the window frame. Check the caulk around basement window frames annually and replace it when it starts to crack or pull away from the brick.

Plastic window well covers are a cheap fix that keeps leaves and direct rainfall out of the well entirely. They're not a substitute for a functional drain, but they reduce the volume of water the well has to manage.

## 6. Locate and Maintain Your Floor Drain

Most Michigan basements built before the 1990s have a floor drain connected to either the sanitary sewer or a dry well. During a heavy rain, municipal sewer systems in older parts of Macomb can surcharge, meaning the sewer backs up under pressure and pushes water up through the lowest drain in the house, which is your basement floor drain.

A backwater valve (also called a sewer backflow preventer) installs in the drain line and closes automatically when flow reverses. If your basement has flooded with water that smells like sewage rather than rain, a backwater valve is likely the single most effective upgrade you can make. A licensed plumber can confirm whether your drain connects to the sanitary sewer and whether a valve is feasible for your system.

## 7. Check Your Basement Walls for Cracks and Efflorescence

White, chalky deposits on a concrete or block wall, called efflorescence, are mineral salts left behind when water moves through the masonry and evaporates. They're not structural damage on their own, but they're a reliable sign that water is migrating through the wall. Horizontal cracks in block walls are more serious: they can indicate lateral soil pressure and should be evaluated by a structural engineer or waterproofing contractor before the next wet season.

Hairline cracks in poured concrete walls can often be injected with polyurethane or epoxy from the interior. Larger gaps, or any crack that has shifted vertically, warrant a professional assessment.

## 8. Know What Your Homeowner's Policy Actually Covers

This step won't stop water from entering, but it can prevent the financial shock that follows. Standard homeowner's insurance policies in Michigan typically exclude flood damage caused by surface water or groundwater. That coverage usually requires a separate flood policy through the National Flood Insurance Program or a private carrier.

Sewer backup coverage is a different endorsement still, and one that's often surprisingly affordable to add. Review your declarations page before the season starts. If you're not sure what's covered, call your agent and ask specifically about groundwater intrusion, sewer backup, and sump pump failure. The answer may change what upgrades you prioritize.

## When Prevention Isn't Enough

Even a well-maintained basement can take on water after an extreme rain event, the kind that overwhelms storm infrastructure across the county, not just your property. If water has already entered your basement, the clock starts immediately. Porous concrete and wood framing absorb moisture fast, and in Michigan's humid summers, mold can begin colonizing wet materials within 24 to 48 hours.

If you're dealing with standing water, saturated drywall, or a musty smell that appeared after a rain event, that's the point where drying equipment and moisture meters matter more than a mop.
