The short answer: it depends on where the water came from. Homeowners insurance typically covers sudden, accidental water damage, a pipe that bursts overnight, a washing machine hose that fails, a dishwasher that leaks through the subfloor. It generally does not cover flooding from outside your home, slow leaks you could have caught, or water that seeped in because of deferred maintenance. Knowing which category your damage falls into before you call your adjuster can save you hours of frustration and help you avoid a claim denial that's difficult to reverse.

## The Core Distinction: Sudden vs. Gradual, and Internal vs. External

Insurance companies draw two hard lines when they evaluate a water damage claim.

The first is **sudden versus gradual**. A supply line to your refrigerator that fails without warning on a Tuesday morning is sudden. A slow drip under the bathroom sink that's been staining the cabinet for six months is gradual, and almost universally excluded. Adjusters are trained to look for water staining, mineral deposits, and soft or discolored wood that suggest the damage built up over time. If those signs are present, the claim is at risk even if you genuinely didn't notice the leak.

The second line is **internal versus external**. Standard homeowners policies (HO-3 is the most common form) cover water that originates inside the structure, burst pipes, appliance failures, an HVAC condensate line that backs up. They exclude water that enters from outside: storm surge, overflowing rivers, heavy rain that backs up through a floor drain, or groundwater that pushes through a foundation wall. That last category requires a separate flood insurance policy, typically purchased through the National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) or a private carrier.

If your water came in through a window well, a crack in the poured concrete foundation, or a floor drain during a storm, your standard policy almost certainly won't cover it, and that distinction matters before you pick up the phone.

## What Standard Homeowners Insurance Usually Covers

Within the sudden-and-accidental, internal-source category, coverage is generally broad:

- **Burst or frozen pipes**, including the cost to open walls to access and repair the pipe, not just the water cleanup
- **Appliance failures**, washing machines, dishwashers, water heaters, and refrigerators with ice makers
- **Accidental overflow**, a bathtub or sink left running that overflows onto the floor below
- **Sudden HVAC failures**, a condensate pan that cracks or an air handler that leaks
- **Roof leaks from a covered peril**, if a windstorm damages your roof and rain enters through the opening, the resulting interior water damage is typically covered under the wind claim

Coverage usually extends to both the structure (walls, flooring, ceilings, built-in cabinetry) and personal property, though personal property limits and sub-limits vary by policy. Your policy's declarations page will show your dwelling coverage limit (Coverage A) and your personal property limit (Coverage C), those are the numbers that matter most when you're estimating a loss.

## What Homeowners Insurance Typically Excludes

This list is where most claim surprises happen:

- **Flood**, any water that originates outside and enters the home, regardless of cause
- **Sewer or drain backup**, water that reverses through a floor drain or toilet during a heavy rain event. Some carriers offer this as an endorsement (add-on) for a modest premium; check your policy
- **Gradual leaks and seepage**, slow drips, weeping foundation walls, condensation damage that built up over months
- **Maintenance failures**, a water heater that rusted through because it was 18 years old, a roof that leaked because shingles were visibly deteriorating
- **Mold remediation**, if mold is present because the underlying water damage was gradual or unaddressed, most policies exclude the mold work even if they would have covered the original leak

One important note on mold: if a covered sudden loss (say, a burst pipe) is not dried properly and mold develops within days, some policies will cover the remediation as part of the original claim. The key is documentation and speed. Water behind plaster or inside wall cavities can read dry at the surface while the lath or sheathing behind it stays saturated, a condition that creates ideal mold conditions within 24 to 72 hours. Getting professional drying equipment in place quickly is both a practical and a financial decision.

## Steps to Take Immediately After a Water Loss

If you've just discovered water damage, the order of your next few hours matters for both safety and your claim.

1. **Stop the source.** For a burst pipe, that means the main shutoff valve, usually at the meter near the street or where the line enters the house. For an appliance, pull it away from the wall and close the supply valve behind it.
2. **Document before you touch anything.** Walk every affected room with your phone and shoot video. Open cabinet doors, photograph the underside of counters, capture any visible staining or standing water. Adjusters work from documentation, and photos taken in the first hour are the most valuable.
3. **Call your insurance carrier to open a claim.** You don't need to know your exact coverage before you call, that's the adjuster's job. Opening the claim starts the clock on response time and creates a record.
4. **Begin mitigation, or call someone who can.** Most policies include a duty-to-mitigate clause, meaning you're expected to take reasonable steps to prevent additional damage. That can mean pulling up soaked rugs, moving furniture off wet flooring, or running fans. For anything involving structural materials (subfloor, drywall, insulation), professional drying equipment is almost always more effective and better documented than consumer fans.
5. **Keep every receipt.** Emergency mitigation costs, hotel stays if the home is uninhabitable, equipment rental, professional services, are often reimbursable under your Additional Living Expenses (ALE) coverage. Save everything.

## How the Claims Process Actually Works

After you open a claim, your carrier will assign an adjuster, either a staff adjuster or an independent adjuster contracted by the carrier. They will schedule an inspection, review your documentation, and produce a scope of loss that estimates the cost to repair or replace damaged materials.

A few things worth knowing before that inspection:

- **You can hire a public adjuster** to represent your interests in the claim, separate from the carrier's adjuster. This is especially useful on large or complex losses.
- **The adjuster's estimate is not always final.** If you believe the scope is incomplete, for example, if they didn't account for concealed damage inside a wall cavity, you can dispute it through a supplemental claim or, in some cases, invoke the appraisal clause in your policy.
- **Depreciation affects your check.** Most policies pay Actual Cash Value (ACV) first, which is replacement cost minus depreciation. If you have Replacement Cost Value (RCV) coverage, you receive the depreciation holdback after repairs are completed and you submit receipts.
- **Your deductible comes off the top.** A $2,500 deductible on a $15,000 claim means your net payout is $12,500 before any depreciation adjustments.

## When to Call a Water Damage Professional

Some water damage situations are genuinely manageable with a mop and a rental dehumidifier. A small appliance leak caught within an hour, on a tile floor with no cabinetry affected, may dry without professional help.

Call a water damage restoration company when:

- Water has been standing for more than a few hours
- The affected area includes carpet, hardwood, engineered flooring, or any wood subfloor
- Water has reached drywall, insulation, or wall cavities
- The source was a drain, toilet, or anything that might carry contamination
- You can smell anything musty within 48 hours of the event
- Your insurance carrier is involved and you need documented drying logs for the claim

Professional drying equipment, commercial dehumidifiers, air movers, and thermal imaging cameras that show moisture behind surfaces, produces the kind of moisture readings and drying logs that adjusters expect to see. It also catches the saturation that a surface-dry room hides.
