Water inside a commercial building moves fast and doesn't respect business hours. Whether it's a burst supply line in a server room, a roof drain that backed up overnight, or a slow leak behind a restroom wall that finally soaked through the drywall, the first 24 to 48 hours after a water intrusion event largely determine how long your business is offline, and how much the recovery costs. The decisions you make in those first hours, before a restoration crew arrives, can either contain the damage or compound it.

## Understanding What You're Actually Dealing With

Commercial water damage is not a scaled-up version of a residential leak. The stakes are different because the systems are different. A single water event in a commercial building can simultaneously affect:

- **Structural systems**: steel decking, concrete slabs, and load-bearing framing can all absorb water in ways that aren't visible from the surface
- **Mechanical systems**: HVAC ductwork, electrical conduit, and elevator shafts create pathways that carry water far from the original source
- **Tenant and inventory assets**: stock, equipment, documents, and data infrastructure are often the most expensive losses and the hardest to document after the fact
- **Occupancy and code compliance**: depending on the extent of damage, your local authority having jurisdiction (AHJ) may require an inspection before the building can be reoccupied

In Southeast Michigan, commercial buildings also face a specific regional challenge: Macomb County sits in a low-lying area with a high water table and aging combined sewer infrastructure. During heavy rain events, sewer backups into basement-level mechanical rooms and loading docks are common, and that category of water (Category 3, or "black water" under professional water damage categories) carries contamination risks that require a different response than a clean supply-line break.

Knowing the source and category of the water before you start moving things around matters. It changes what protective equipment your staff should wear, what materials can be salvaged versus removed, and how your insurance carrier will classify the claim.

## Immediate Steps in the First Two Hours

These are sequential, order matters.

1. **Shut off the water source** if it's a supply-side failure and you can safely reach the shutoff. For a burst pipe, that means the isolation valve for that line, or the building main if you can't locate the branch valve. For a roof or sewer event, there's nothing to shut off, move to step two.
2. **Cut power to affected areas** at the breaker panel. Water and live electrical systems are the most dangerous combination in a commercial water loss. Do not enter a flooded space until power is confirmed off.
3. **Photograph and video everything before you move anything.** Walk the perimeter of the affected area with your phone. Capture the water line on walls, affected materials, any visible equipment, and the source of the intrusion if you can safely access it. This documentation is the foundation of your insurance claim.
4. **Call your insurance carrier to open a claim.** Most commercial policies have a notice requirement, meaning you need to report the loss promptly, not after cleanup is already underway. Get a claim number before work begins.
5. **Begin moving salvageable inventory and equipment out of the wet zone** only after power is off and you've documented the scene. Prioritize electronics, paper records, and anything with high replacement cost or long lead times.
6. **Contact a commercial restoration contractor.** The longer standing water sits on a concrete slab or inside a wall cavity, the more secondary damage accumulates. Mold colonization can begin within 24 to 72 hours in warm, humid conditions, and Southeast Michigan summers create exactly those conditions.

## What Not to Do (Common Mistakes That Extend Downtime)

Under pressure to reopen, property managers and business owners often make moves that feel productive but create larger problems downstream.

**Don't use shop vacs and box fans as your primary drying strategy.** Consumer-grade equipment doesn't generate the airflow or dehumidification capacity to dry structural assemblies, concrete, framing, insulation, and subfloor, to the moisture content levels required to prevent mold growth. Surface readings can look dry while materials two inches in remain saturated.

**Don't discard damaged materials before your adjuster or a restoration contractor has documented them.** Disposing of wet carpet, drywall, or ceiling tile before they're documented and measured removes the evidence your claim depends on. Take photos, but leave materials in place until you have written authorization to remove them.

**Don't assume the damage is limited to what's visible.** Water migrates through the path of least resistance. A leak at the third-floor mechanical room can travel down through wall cavities and appear as a stain on the first-floor ceiling with no obvious wet area in between. Thermal imaging and moisture meters are the only reliable way to map the actual extent of intrusion.

**Don't delay because you're waiting to see if it dries on its own.** It won't, not in a sealed commercial building with limited airflow. Every day of delay is a day closer to a secondary mold loss, which is a separate and often more contentious insurance claim.

## When the Situation Requires a Professional Restoration Contractor

Some water events are genuinely manageable with in-house facilities staff: a small toilet overflow on a tile floor, caught immediately, cleaned and dried within an hour. Most commercial water losses are not that situation.

Call a professional restoration contractor when:

- Standing water covers more than a few square feet, or has been present for more than a few hours
- The water source is a sewer backup, roof drain, or any source that may carry biological contamination
- The affected area includes carpet, drywall, insulation, or wood framing
- HVAC systems, electrical panels, or data infrastructure are in the affected zone
- You cannot confirm the full extent of the moisture intrusion with the equipment you have on hand
- Your insurance carrier requires a licensed contractor for the loss to be covered

A qualified commercial restoration team will bring industrial desiccant dehumidifiers, air movers, thermal imaging cameras, and calibrated moisture meters. They'll establish a drying goal based on industry drying standard for water damage, monitor readings daily, and provide the documentation your adjuster needs to close the claim efficiently.

## The Recovery Timeline: What to Expect After the Crew Arrives

Commercial water damage restoration is not a single visit. Understanding the typical phases helps you plan for business continuity realistically.

**Days 1–2 (Mitigation):** Water extraction, removal of unsalvageable materials, placement of drying equipment. This phase stops the loss from growing. Your space will be loud, staged with equipment, and not operational in the affected areas.

**Days 3–7 (Structural drying):** Equipment runs continuously. A technician checks moisture readings daily and adjusts equipment placement. The goal is to bring all structural materials to a dry standard before any reconstruction begins. Rushing this phase is the single most common cause of mold problems appearing months after a "completed" restoration.

**Week 2 and beyond (Reconstruction):** Once materials are confirmed dry, reconstruction begins, new drywall, flooring, ceiling tile, paint. Timeline varies significantly based on scope. A single-room loss might be complete in two weeks. A multi-floor event can run months.

Throughout the process, a good restoration contractor communicates directly with your insurance adjuster, provides scope-of-work documentation, and flags any supplemental damage discovered during reconstruction, so you're not managing that coordination yourself while also trying to run a business.
