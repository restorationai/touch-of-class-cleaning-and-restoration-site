## Why Troy Properties See Water Heater Damage Differently

Troy's housing stock skews toward homes built during Oakland County's postwar and suburban-boom decades, which means a significant portion of water heaters are tucked into utility closets on the main floor or in finished lower levels rather than in a dedicated mechanical room with a floor drain. When a tank-style unit fails, whether the bottom rusts through, a supply line fitting corrodes, or a pressure-relief valve discharges, the water lands directly on finished flooring instead of draining harmlessly away.

Michigan's temperature swings also accelerate tank wear. The repeated thermal cycling from cold winters to humid summers stresses the glass lining inside steel tanks, and Troy's water, supplied through the city's connection to the Detroit Water and Sewerage Department system, carries mineral content that contributes to sediment buildup at the tank bottom. That sediment insulates the burner, forces the unit to run hotter, and shortens tank life. A unit that should last 10 to 12 years sometimes fails closer to 8 in this region.

## What the Cleanup Process Looks Like in Troy

The first thing that matters after a water heater flood is stopping the source, shutting the cold-water supply to the tank and, if the unit is gas, turning off the gas valve at the appliance. Once the water is no longer flowing, the real assessment begins.

Extraction comes first. Truck-mounted or portable high-capacity extractors pull standing water from hard floors and begin pulling moisture from the subfloor below. In Troy homes with engineered hardwood or glue-down luxury vinyl plank, both common in renovations from the 2000s onward, the adhesive layer can trap moisture underneath and give a false dry reading at the surface. Thermal imaging and pin-type moisture meters together give a more complete picture of where water has actually traveled.

Drying follows extraction. Desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers, combined with directed airflow from axial air movers, bring structural materials down to acceptable moisture content over several days. The number of drying days depends on how far the water traveled and what materials absorbed it, not on a fixed schedule. Documentation, moisture readings logged daily, supports the insurance claim and confirms when drying is genuinely complete rather than just surface-dry.

If drywall or insulation absorbed water, those materials typically need to come out. Wet fiberglass batt insulation does not dry effectively in place, and drywall that stays wet for more than 24 to 48 hours becomes a mold risk regardless of how well the rest of the space is dried.

Troy homes built in the 1970s and early 1980s frequently used polybutylene supply lines in addition to copper, and some of those lines were never replaced during later renovations. If a water heater flood prompts a closer look at the supply plumbing feeding the unit, it is worth asking a licensed plumber to confirm what material is behind the wall.
