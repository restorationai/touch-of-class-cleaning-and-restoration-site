## Why Rochester Hills Industrial Facilities Face Distinct Restoration Challenges

The climate here does real work on commercial structures. Oakland County winters regularly cycle through freeze and thaw multiple times between December and March, which stresses roof membranes, expansion joints, and overhead fire suppression lines in ways that warmer-climate facilities never experience. A pinhole leak in a suppression system can go unnoticed until it has saturated insulation batts above a drop ceiling, and by the time standing water is visible on a production floor, the structural steel decking above may already be holding moisture.

Rochester Hills also carries a significant share of facilities built during the 1970s and 1980s industrial expansion in Oakland County. Those buildings often used construction methods, insulation types, and HVAC configurations that are no longer standard. Older vapor barriers, fiberglass batt insulation packed against metal wall panels, and concrete tilt-up construction all behave differently under saturation than modern assemblies. Restoration work in these buildings requires moisture mapping that accounts for those materials rather than assuming uniform drying rates.

Fire losses in manufacturing environments add another layer. Smoke and soot from a production fire carry residue from plastics, lubricants, and coatings that are common in automotive-adjacent manufacturing. That residue is chemically different from a residential house fire and requires different cleaning chemistry and surface protocols to clear adequately before operations can resume.

## Our Industrial Restoration Process in Rochester Hills

The first priority on any industrial loss is a thorough scope assessment before any equipment is staged. In a warehouse or manufacturing facility, that means walking the full affected area with moisture meters and thermal imaging to identify where water has migrated behind wall panels, under concrete slabs, or into mezzanine decking. Water follows the path of least resistance, and in a large open-span building, it can travel a significant distance from the source before pooling.

Once the scope is documented, extraction and drying equipment is sized to the actual cubic footage and material types involved, not to a generic formula. Large-capacity desiccant dehumidifiers are often the right tool in Rochester Hills facilities during winter months, because refrigerant-based units lose efficiency when ambient temperatures drop, and many industrial buildings are not fully climate-controlled throughout. Drying logs are maintained throughout the process to document conditions for both the facility owner and the insurance carrier.

Content and equipment protection runs parallel to structural drying. Production machinery, inventory, and tooling that cannot be relocated get protected in place. Where relocation is necessary, we coordinate with facility management on sequencing so that operations can continue in unaffected areas wherever possible.

Final clearance documentation is provided before the job is closed, giving the facility a written record of pre- and post-restoration moisture readings. That record matters for insurance purposes and for any future due-diligence review of the building.

## Local Note

One pattern that comes up specifically in Rochester Hills industrial buildings from the 1970s and 1980s construction era: metal wall panel systems in those facilities were often installed with fiberglass batt insulation pressed directly against the interior face of the panel with no true vapor barrier separation. When those panels take on moisture from a roof leak or a suppression discharge, the insulation holds water against the metal for an extended period, accelerating corrosion on the panel fasteners and framing. Standard moisture readings at the panel surface can read lower than the actual moisture content behind the batt. Thermal imaging is the reliable way to find those pockets, and it changes the drying plan significantly when they are present.
