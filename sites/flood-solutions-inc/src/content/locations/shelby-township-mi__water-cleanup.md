Shelby Township sits on the western edge of Macomb County, where clay-heavy glacial soils slow drainage and back-to-back Great Lakes storm systems can dump several inches of rain in a matter of hours. When that water finds its way into a basement, crawl space, or finished lower level, through a sump pump that couldn't keep pace, a window well that overflowed, or a supply line that let go, it moves fast and hides in places that look dry on the surface. Flood Solutions inc has been responding to water losses across Macomb County since 1996, and our crews know exactly what standing water does to the kinds of homes and foundations common throughout this part of Michigan.

## Why Shelby Township Properties See Water Intrusion Issues

The dominant soil type across much of Shelby Township is dense glacial clay, the same geology that underlies most of Macomb County. Clay soil does not absorb rainfall readily; it sheds water laterally and directs it toward foundations, window wells, and low-lying garage slabs. During spring thaw or after a prolonged summer storm, that lateral pressure can overwhelm even a properly maintained sump system.

Shelby Township also experienced significant residential development from the 1970s through the 1990s, producing a large stock of ranch-style and split-level homes with finished basements. Those finished lower levels, drywall, carpet, drop ceilings, are particularly vulnerable because water can travel several feet behind a finished wall before it becomes visible. By the time a homeowner notices a wet baseboard or a soft spot in the carpet, the framing and insulation behind the wall may already be saturated.

On older parcels closer to the township's agricultural roots, drainage tile systems installed decades ago can fail silently, redirecting groundwater toward the foundation rather than away from it.

## Our Water Cleanup Process in Shelby Township

The first priority when we arrive is containment and assessment. We use moisture meters and thermal imaging to map exactly where water has traveled, including behind walls and under subfloors, before a single piece of equipment is placed. In Shelby Township's finished-basement homes, this step matters more than almost anywhere else, because the finished surfaces routinely conceal saturated wall cavities that a visual inspection alone would miss.

Once we have a full moisture map, we extract standing water with truck-mounted and portable extraction units. Carpet and pad are assessed for salvageability; in most cases where standing water has been present for more than a few hours, pad is removed because it retains moisture and becomes a mold substrate quickly. Shelby Township's humid summers, with dew points that regularly climb into the uncomfortable range from June through August, mean ambient humidity works against the drying process, so we deploy commercial dehumidifiers sized to the actual cubic footage of the affected space, not residential units that are undersized for the job.

Drying typically involves a combination of air movers positioned to create directed airflow across wet surfaces, desiccant or refrigerant dehumidification depending on conditions, and daily moisture readings to track progress. We do not close out a job until readings confirm the structure has returned to a stable, pre-loss moisture range.

## Shelby Township Insurance Coordination

Most standard homeowner policies in Michigan cover sudden and accidental water losses, a burst pipe, an appliance failure, an ice dam backup, but exclude gradual seepage or sump pump overflow unless a specific rider is in place. We document every affected area with photographs, moisture readings, and written scope before any materials are removed, which gives your adjuster a complete picture of the loss. We work with most major carriers and can communicate directly with your claims representative to keep the process moving. If your policy does include a sump pump or water backup endorsement, that documentation becomes especially important for substantiating the claim.

## Local Note

Shelby Township's split-level homes from the 1970s and 1980s present a specific drying challenge that's easy to underestimate: the half-level between the garage and the main living area often contains a short wall cavity and subfloor assembly that sits directly above the garage slab. When water enters through the garage, from a storm drain backup or a vehicle tracking in significant snowmelt, it can wick upward into that intermediate floor assembly and go undetected for days. We check that zone specifically on any loss involving garage-adjacent spaces, because missing it means moisture lingers in a concealed cavity long after the visible surfaces read dry.
