## Why Troy Properties See Water Damage Differently

Troy's residential build-out spans several decades, and that mix matters when water gets into a structure. Homes constructed in the 1960s and 1970s, common throughout the city's older subdivisions, frequently have block foundation walls rather than poured concrete. Block walls are porous by nature, and hydrostatic pressure from saturated clay soil pushes moisture through the mortar joints long after the rain stops. Newer construction in Troy's eastern corridors tends to have finished basements with drywall, insulation, and luxury vinyl plank over concrete slabs, all of which trap moisture against the slab and create ideal conditions for secondary damage if water isn't extracted quickly.

Troy also sits in a region that receives significant lake-effect moisture from Lake St. Clair and Lake Huron weather systems, meaning heavy rain events can arrive fast and overwhelm residential sump systems. When two or three inches fall in an afternoon, the window between a sump alarm and a flooded basement can be under an hour.

## Our Water Removal Process in Troy

When we arrive at a Troy property, the first step is always containment assessment, not equipment deployment. We identify the water source, confirm it's controlled, and then map the affected area with moisture meters and thermal imaging before a single extraction hose goes down. This matters because water in Troy's older block-wall basements often wicks laterally into wall cavities and under floor systems before it's visible at the surface.

Extraction comes next, using truck-mounted and portable units calibrated to the volume and type of water present. After standing water is removed, we place industrial air movers and desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers in a configuration matched to the room geometry and building materials. Concrete slabs, block walls, and wood subfloors all release moisture at different rates, and drying plans account for each material separately rather than treating the space as a single unit.

Moisture readings are logged at each visit so the drying curve is documented, not estimated. This record also becomes part of the claim file if you're working with a homeowner's insurance carrier.

## Troy Insurance Coordination

Most standard homeowner policies in Michigan cover sudden and accidental water discharge, which includes burst pipes, appliance failures, and overflow events. Slow seepage or gradual leaks are typically excluded, which is why documenting the cause of loss at the time of extraction matters. We photograph source conditions, affected materials, and moisture readings in a format that aligns with what adjusters need to process a claim.

If your policy includes a deductible that applies before coverage kicks in, we can walk through the scope of work and what the documentation will show before you decide how to proceed. We bill carriers directly on covered losses.

## Local Note

Troy's subdivisions built during the 1970s and early 1980s often have floor drains in the utility room that tie into the municipal sanitary system rather than a sump pit. During heavy rain events, when the city's system is under pressure, those drains can backflow rather than drain. If you're seeing water come up through a floor drain during a storm rather than in from a wall or window, that's a backflow condition and it changes both the extraction approach and the insurance category. Sewer backup coverage is typically a separate rider on a Michigan homeowner policy, not part of the base water damage clause, and knowing the difference at the time of loss affects how the claim is filed.
