## Why Clinton Township Basements Flood the Way They Do

The glacial lake clay that underlies much of Macomb County is nearly impermeable. When the ground is saturated, water finds the path of least resistance, and that path is usually through a foundation wall crack, a failed sump pump, or a floor drain that backs up under hydrostatic pressure. This is not a fluke event; it is the predictable result of the region's geology meeting a heavy rain event or a rapid snowmelt in late winter or early spring.

Clinton Township also has a significant inventory of ranch-style and split-level homes built between the late 1950s and the mid-1980s. Many of those basements were finished later, often with fiberglass batt insulation tucked against the block wall and drywall installed directly over it. That construction detail is efficient to build and efficient to flood: water wicks up the drywall paper, the insulation holds moisture against the block, and what looks like a contained wet spot on the floor is often a saturated wall cavity running several feet up.

Sump pump failures are the single most common call in this township. A power outage during the same storm that is flooding the yard is a worst-case scenario, and it happens here regularly enough that a backup pump or battery unit is worth the investment before the next storm season.

## How Basement Flooding Cleanup Works Here

Every flooded basement cleanup starts with an honest read of what is actually wet, not just what looks wet. Standing water is removed first using truck-mounted extraction equipment that pulls far more volume per minute than a portable unit. Once the visible water is gone, the real assessment begins.

Moisture mapping with calibrated meters tells us what the surface readings miss. In Clinton Township's older finished basements, that often means finding that the concrete block wall is holding water inside its cores, or that the subfloor under a laminate floor is reading elevated three feet from the visible wet zone. Those readings drive the drying plan, not assumptions.

Affected materials are evaluated for salvageability. Wet drywall that has been saturated for more than 24 to 48 hours typically cannot be dried in place without creating conditions that favor mold colonization, so controlled demolition, removing the lower portion of the wall to allow airflow to the framing and block, is often the right call. Structural lumber and concrete block can usually be dried and treated. The goal is to remove only what cannot be saved and document everything that comes out for the insurance claim.

Industrial air movers and refrigerant or desiccant dehumidifiers run in a calculated configuration until daily moisture readings confirm the structure has returned to normal range. That process typically takes several days, not hours, and the equipment stays until the numbers say it is done.

Sudden and accidental water losses, a burst pipe, an appliance failure, a sump pump overflow, are typically covered under a standard homeowner's policy, though coverage for groundwater or surface flooding usually requires a separate flood policy. The distinction matters when filing, and documentation matters even more.
