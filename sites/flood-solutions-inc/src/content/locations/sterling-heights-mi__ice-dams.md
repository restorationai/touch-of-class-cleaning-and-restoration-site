Michigan winters put Sterling Heights roofs through a freeze-thaw cycle that most homeowners never think about until water starts staining a bedroom ceiling in January. Ice dams form when heat escaping from the attic melts snow on the upper roof, and that meltwater refreezes at the colder eave line, backing up under shingles and finding every gap in the roof deck. For a lot of Sterling Heights homes built during the city's big residential boom of the 1960s through 1980s, that gap is closer to the surface than owners realize.

## Why Sterling Heights Homes See Ice Dam Problems

Sterling Heights grew fast during Macomb County's postwar suburban expansion, and a large share of the housing stock is ranch and split-level construction with lower-pitched rooflines and attic insulation that was standard for the era but thin by today's code. Lower-slope roofs shed snow more slowly than steep colonial rooflines, which gives ice dams more time to build along the eaves. Add in Michigan's habit of alternating a hard freeze with a 35-degree thaw within the same week, and the shingles along the eave never really get a break from that cycle. Attics with bathroom or kitchen exhaust fans venting straight into the attic space (a shortcut used in a fair number of older Macomb County builds) make the problem worse by pumping extra warm, moist air right where you don't want it.

## Our Ice Dam Process in Sterling Heights

We start outside, checking the eaves, gutters, and soffit for active ice buildup and any signs water has already tracked back under the roofline into the attic or exterior wall cavity. Inside, we look at the attic insulation depth and check for the telltale frost or wet insulation pattern that tells us where warm air is escaping. If water has already reached drywall, plaster, or framing, we get air movers and dehumidification running per the IICRC S500 drying standard while we sort out the source. Steam-based ice removal is our preference over chipping or hacking at ice, since a pry bar or hammer on a frozen shingle in a Michigan January is a fast way to turn a water problem into a torn-shingle problem. Once the dam is cleared and the space is dry, we document everything for whoever ends up handling the insurance side.

## Sterling Heights Insurance Coordination

Most homeowner policies in Michigan treat ice dam water intrusion as a covered peril, but adjusters look closely at whether the damage stems from a sudden dam versus a long-term insulation or ventilation deficiency that the homeowner should have addressed. We photograph the ice formation, the point of water entry, and the interior damage separately so the claim tells an accurate story instead of getting lumped together and questioned. For condos and HOA-governed developments around Sterling Heights, roof and exterior maintenance responsibility sometimes falls to the association rather than the unit owner, so we ask early who holds the roof line item before starting interior repairs, which saves everyone a dispute later.

## Local note

One pattern we watch for in Sterling Heights: homes with additions built onto the original structure, common where a family finished a garage or added a sunroom decades after the original build, often have a seam where the old roofline meets the new one at a different pitch or insulation depth. That transition point collects snow and ice differently than the rest of the roof and is frequently where we find the first sign of a dam even when the rest of the roofline looks fine.

Ice dams rarely announce themselves until water is already inside, and by the time you see a stain spreading across a ceiling in Sterling Heights, the ice has usually been building for days.
