## Why Sterling Heights Basements Flood the Way They Do

The soil profile under most of Sterling Heights is dense glacial clay with limited natural drainage. During a heavy storm, the ground saturates quickly and surface water has nowhere to go except toward the lowest point on the property, which is almost always the basement wall or window well. The city's storm sewer system handles ordinary rain well, but a fast-moving line of thunderstorms, the kind that drops two inches in under an hour, can overwhelm catch basins and push water back up through floor drains.

Housing stock matters here too. A large portion of Sterling Heights homes were built in the 1960s and 1970s, when basement waterproofing standards were minimal and sump pump installation was not yet routine. Many of those homes have poured concrete or concrete block foundations that have been settling and developing hairline cracks for fifty-plus years. A crack that held fine through last summer may open enough during a freeze-thaw cycle to let in a steady seep the next spring. Add an aging sump pump that trips during a power outage and the basement can take on several inches before anyone notices.

## What Basement Flooding Cleanup Looks Like Here

The first priority on any flooded basement job in Sterling Heights is stopping the water source if it is still active, whether that means shutting off a supply line, helping the homeowner locate the main shutoff, or coordinating with the city if a municipal line is involved. Once the source is controlled, water removal starts with truck-mounted extraction equipment capable of pulling hundreds of gallons quickly, because the longer water sits against concrete block or sits under vinyl flooring, the deeper it travels.

After bulk water is out, the real work begins. Moisture readings are taken at the wall cavities, along the base of any finished walls, under any subfloor material, and at the rim joist area, which is one of the most commonly overlooked saturation points in a Michigan basement. Drying equipment, including industrial air movers and dehumidifiers, is positioned based on those readings, not by formula. Daily monitoring checks whether the numbers are moving in the right direction. Finished drywall that reads dry at the surface can still be holding moisture in the paper facing and the framing behind it, so readings are taken at multiple depths before equipment is pulled.

Materials that cannot be dried in place, saturated carpet padding, swollen laminate, insulation that has absorbed water, are removed and documented before disposal. That documentation matters for insurance claims.

## Working with Insurance on Basement Water Damage Claims

Whether a basement flooding claim is covered depends on the cause. Sudden and accidental discharge from a supply line or appliance is typically covered under a standard homeowner policy. Groundwater intrusion from outside, sometimes called seepage, is usually excluded unless the homeowner carries a separate water backup rider. Flood Solutions inc documents the loss thoroughly, photographs affected materials before anything is moved, and provides the carrier with the moisture data and scope of work they need to process the claim. That documentation also protects the homeowner if there is a dispute about pre-existing conditions versus storm damage.

## Local Note

Sterling Heights homes built in the 1960s and early 1970s frequently have concrete block basement walls rather than poured concrete, and block walls behave differently when they get wet. Each hollow core in the block can hold water, meaning the wall itself becomes a reservoir. A block wall can feel and read dry on the interior surface while the cores are still holding significant moisture. Drying a block wall properly requires extended equipment run times and sometimes interior surface treatment to allow that trapped moisture to migrate out. Crews that pull equipment too early on a block-wall basement in this city often get a callback when efflorescence or mold shows up weeks later.
