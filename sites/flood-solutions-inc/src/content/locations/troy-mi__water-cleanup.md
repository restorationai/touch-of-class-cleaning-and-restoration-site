Troy sits on some of Oakland County's flattest terrain, and when a water main bursts or a basement sump pump fails during one of Michigan's late-winter freeze-thaw cycles, there is nowhere for the water to drain quickly. That combination, clay-heavy soil, shallow water tables in lower-lying subdivisions, and homes built across several decades with varying foundation styles, means water cleanup in Troy rarely stops at pulling out a shop vac and running a fan. Getting it right the first time matters, because secondary mold growth can begin colonizing wet framing within 24 to 48 hours of a loss.

## Why Troy Properties See Water Damage Differently

Oakland County's clay-dominant soil does not absorb groundwater the way sandy soils do further north. After a heavy rain or a rapid snowmelt event, that moisture has limited places to go, and it often finds the path of least resistance into crawl spaces and basement walls. Troy's housing stock reflects several distinct construction eras: ranch homes and split-levels built in the 1960s and 1970s with block-wall foundations, larger colonials from the 1980s and 1990s with poured concrete, and newer construction in the northern reaches of the city. Block foundations are particularly vulnerable because the hollow cores can hold standing water invisibly, reading dry at the surface while remaining saturated inside the wall cavity.

The city also sits within a broader regional drainage network that can back up during significant storm events, sending water through floor drains before a homeowner realizes what is happening. That kind of intrusion carries a different contamination classification than a clean supply-line break, and it changes the scope of cleanup considerably.

## Our Water Cleanup Process in Troy

Every job starts with a thorough moisture assessment before any equipment is staged. Thermal imaging cameras and calibrated moisture meters map where water has traveled, under hardwood floors, behind baseboards, into wall cavities, so nothing gets missed because it looked dry to the eye.

Once the scope is clear, the process moves in sequence:

- **Standing water extraction** using truck-mounted and portable extraction units, including tools sized for tight crawl spaces common in Troy's older ranch-style homes
- **Structural drying** with commercial-grade desiccant and refrigerant dehumidifiers, calibrated to the ambient conditions inside the structure
- **Air movement** positioned to pull moisture out of wall cavities and subfloor assemblies, not just surface materials
- **Moisture monitoring** on a documented schedule so drying progress is measurable and defensible for insurance purposes
- **Antimicrobial treatment** on affected surfaces where contamination risk warrants it, particularly in losses involving category 2 or category 3 water

Documentation runs parallel to every step, photos, moisture readings, equipment logs, because Troy homeowners filing claims with their carriers need a clear paper trail.

## Local Note

Troy's split-level homes from the late 1960s and early 1970s present a specific drying challenge that is easy to underestimate. The half-level between the garage and the main living floor often contains the home's original ductwork running through an uninsulated chase. When water enters from a basement or crawl space, it wicks into that chase and travels upward, sometimes appearing as a moisture reading on the first floor with no obvious source. Checking duct chases with a probe meter is a step that gets skipped on a visual-only inspection, and it is one of the more common reasons a Troy home tests wet again after what looked like a completed dry-out.
