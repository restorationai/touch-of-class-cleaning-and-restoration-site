## Why Troy Properties Are Particularly Vulnerable After Severe Weather

Troy's residential neighborhoods were largely built out between the 1960s and the 1990s, which means the dominant roof systems are aging three-tab asphalt shingles rather than the heavier architectural shingles common in newer construction. Three-tab shingles lose granule coverage over time, and once that protective layer thins, a single storm with 60-mph gusts can lift tab edges, crack seams, and create water pathways that look minor from the ground but allow steady infiltration during any subsequent rain. The city also has a significant canopy of mature hardwoods planted during those same decades, and large-diameter limbs over rooflines are a consistent source of structural punctures during tornado-warned cells and severe thunderstorm events.

Troy's relatively flat topography means surface water has nowhere fast to go. When storm drains back up during heavy rainfall, water sheets across driveways and toward foundations rather than draining to lower ground. Homes with window wells and finished lower levels are especially exposed to this kind of lateral water entry, which is a different problem from a roof breach but often happens in the same storm event.

## What Storm Damage Restoration Looks Like in Troy

The first priority after a storm is stopping additional water from entering the structure. That means emergency tarping of roof breaches and boarding of broken windows or door frames before any assessment of interior damage begins. Once the envelope is secured, the process shifts to a systematic interior inspection: moisture mapping with thermal imaging and calibrated meters to find water that has already migrated behind walls or under flooring, even if the surface feels dry to the touch.

Water behind plaster or older drywall in Troy's mid-century ranch and colonial homes can read dry at the surface while the cavity stays saturated for days. Drying equipment, including industrial air movers and desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers, is positioned based on that moisture mapping rather than on visible water lines alone. Structural debris from fallen limbs is documented, removed, and catalogued for the insurance claim before any reconstruction work begins. The sequence matters because insurers want to see the damage in its original state, not after materials have been moved.

Troy's mature tree canopy is one of the city's most valued features, but it creates a specific post-storm hazard that is easy to underestimate: a limb that punches through a roof and is then removed by a tree service before a restoration contractor arrives leaves an open hole with no documentation of the original impact zone.
