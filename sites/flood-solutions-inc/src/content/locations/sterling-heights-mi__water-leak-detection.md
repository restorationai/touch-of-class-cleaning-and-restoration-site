A slow leak inside a wall or beneath a slab can go unnoticed for weeks while water migrates through concrete, insulation, and framing. ## Why Sterling Heights Homes Are Prone to Hidden Leaks

Much of Sterling Heights was developed rapidly during the postwar suburban boom and again through the 1970s and 1980s, which means a large share of the housing stock is now carrying plumbing that is 40 to 50 years old. Copper supply lines from that era are susceptible to pinhole corrosion, especially where the water chemistry shifts seasonally. The clay-dominant soil beneath the city expands when saturated and contracts during dry stretches, and that repeated movement puts lateral stress on underground service lines and slab penetrations. Homes built on slab foundations, which are common throughout the city, are particularly vulnerable because a leak beneath the concrete has nowhere obvious to surface. By the time a homeowner notices a warm spot on the floor or a spike on their Detroit Water and Sewerage Department bill, the water has often been migrating for weeks.

## How We Detect Leaks in Sterling Heights Properties

Accurate leak detection is a process of elimination before it is ever a process of excavation. We start with a pressure isolation test to confirm a leak exists and narrow it to the supply side, drain side, or a specific zone of the system. From there, electronic amplification equipment listens for the acoustic signature of water escaping under pressure, even through several inches of concrete or behind finished drywall. Thermal imaging cameras reveal temperature differentials that indicate moisture movement inside wall cavities and beneath flooring without requiring any demolition. When a slab leak is suspected, we combine acoustic listening with line tracing to pinpoint the breach within inches before any concrete is touched. The goal is to hand the plumber a precise location, not a search zone.

## What Makes Leak Detection Different Here

Sterling Heights properties present a specific challenge that does not apply uniformly across Metro Detroit. The city's older subdivisions, many built before modern vapor barriers were standard, often have crawl spaces and slab edges where groundwater intrusion and plumbing leaks produce nearly identical moisture signatures at the surface. Distinguishing a plumbing leak from seasonal groundwater infiltration requires pressure testing in combination with moisture mapping, not acoustic equipment alone. We adjust the diagnostic sequence based on the home's construction era and foundation type, because running the same protocol on a 1965 ranch as on a 2002 two-story produces incomplete results. That calibration matters when the next step is opening a wall or cutting concrete.

## Local Note

Homes in Sterling Heights that were built during the 1970s subdivision expansions frequently used a mix of copper and galvanized steel in the same system, with transition fittings that are now at or past their service life. Those transition points are a common leak origin that does not always show up clearly on acoustic equipment because the fitting itself dampens the sound. When our technicians are working in homes of that vintage, we pay particular attention to where the two pipe materials meet, typically near the water heater, under kitchen islands, and at outdoor hose bib connections, because that is where pinhole failures concentrate.
