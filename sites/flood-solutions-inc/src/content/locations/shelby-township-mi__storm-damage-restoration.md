Shelby Township sits in the middle of Macomb County's storm corridor, where fast-moving spring squall lines and late-summer derechos can drop several inches of rain in under an hour, snap mature oaks onto rooftops, and push debris into window wells before most residents have time to move cars into the garage. When that kind of weather hits, the damage it leaves behind is rarely limited to what you can see from the curb. Storm damage restoration here means working through a layered problem: wind-lifted shingles that let water into the attic, saturated insulation that doesn't show up on a moisture meter at the surface, and structural framing that took an impact load it wasn't designed for.

## Why Shelby Township Properties See Severe Storm Damage

Macomb County's flat glacial-lake plain gives storms almost nothing to slow them down as they push in from the west and southwest. Shelby Township's mix of established subdivisions and newer construction means the housing stock ranges from brick ranches built in the 1970s with original wood-framed roof trusses to 2000s-era colonials with engineered lumber and dimensional shingles. Both can sustain serious damage, but they fail differently. Older trusses can rack under wind shear; newer homes sometimes have soffit and fascia systems that peel away cleanly in high winds, leaving wide-open channels for rain infiltration along the roofline.

The township's clay-heavy soils compound the problem. When the ground is already saturated from a wet spring, even moderate rainfall has nowhere to go. Water backs up against foundation walls, finds gaps around window wells, and enters basements through floor-wall joints. A storm that drops two inches of rain on a dry July afternoon is manageable; the same storm after a wet May is a different situation entirely.

## Our Storm Damage Restoration Process in Shelby Township

The first priority after any storm is stopping active water intrusion. That means tarping breached rooflines and boarding compromised windows before any interior assessment begins. Once the structure is stabilized, the real diagnostic work starts: thermal imaging along ceilings and exterior walls, moisture mapping of every affected room, and a structural walk to identify racking, shifted door frames, or compromised load-bearing members.

Water that entered through a roof breach or a blown window travels farther than most homeowners expect. It follows framing cavities, pools above drop ceilings, and wicks into wall insulation. We trace the moisture path rather than treating only the visible wet area, because stopping at the surface is how mold problems develop three weeks after a storm when the homeowner thinks everything is fine. Drying equipment is positioned based on the moisture map, not by room count, and readings are logged daily until materials reach target levels.

Tree impact damage adds a structural assessment layer. If a limb or trunk has struck a roof, we document load-path integrity before crews work in that section. Shelby Township's mature tree canopy is one of the things that makes the area attractive to buyers, but those same trees become projectiles in a severe line storm.
