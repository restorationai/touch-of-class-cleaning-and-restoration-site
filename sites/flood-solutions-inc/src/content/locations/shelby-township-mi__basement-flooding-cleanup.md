## Why Shelby Township Properties See Basement Flooding

Macomb County's glacial clay soils are the single biggest driver of basement water problems in this part of metro Detroit. Clay compacts tightly, which limits how quickly ground water can move away from a foundation after heavy rainfall. During the wet seasons, hydrostatic pressure builds against basement walls and floor slabs, forcing water through hairline cracks and around pipe penetrations that a homeowner might not notice until there are several inches of standing water on the floor.

Shelby Township also experienced significant residential construction through the 1970s, 1980s, and 1990s, meaning a large share of the housing stock now has aging sump pump systems, older drain tile, and cove joints that have been flexing through decades of freeze-thaw cycles. Michigan winters are hard on concrete. A joint that sealed fine last April may open just enough over the winter to let in a serious flow when snowmelt combines with spring rain. Finished basements, which are common in this area, hide that vulnerability until the carpet is already soaked.

## Our Basement Water Removal Process in Shelby Township

The first step on any flooded basement job is a thorough inspection before extraction begins. Standing water can mask structural concerns, electrical hazards, and the presence of sewage if a floor drain has backed up. Once the space is confirmed safe, truck-mounted extraction equipment pulls bulk water out far faster than portable units, which matters because every hour water sits against wood framing and drywall is another hour of absorption.

After extraction, the work shifts to drying the materials that absorbed water rather than just the water you can see. In Shelby Township homes with finished lower levels, that typically means removing baseboards, drilling small evaporation holes in drywall above the floor line, and placing commercial-grade desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers alongside high-velocity air movers in a calculated pattern. Moisture readings are taken at multiple points in the wall cavity, not just at the surface, because drywall can feel dry to the touch while the wood stud behind it is still well above safe moisture content. The drying phase runs until readings are consistent with the dry standard for that material type, not on a fixed calendar schedule.

Whether a basement flooding claim is covered depends heavily on the cause of loss.
