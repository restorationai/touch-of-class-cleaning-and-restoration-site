When a water loss, fire, or storm tears into framing, subflooring, or drywall, drying and cleanup only solve half the problem. Once the structure is stable and dry, someone has to put the walls, cabinetry, and finishes back the way they were, correctly, to code, and in a way an insurance adjuster will sign off on. That's the gap reconstruction services fill: the rebuild phase that starts where demolition and remediation end.

## What reconstruction services actually involve

Post-damage reconstruction covers everything from a single drywall patch after a slow leak to full structural rebuilds after a house fire. The scope depends entirely on what was removed during mitigation: if a crew cut out two feet of baseboard and wet drywall, reconstruction might mean new drywall, texture match, and paint. If framing members were charred or load-bearing walls were compromised, it means structural carpentry, inspections, and multiple rough-in trades before anything gets closed up again.

Good reconstruction work treats the rebuild as its own project with its own timeline, not an afterthought tacked onto mitigation. That means a written scope of work, a materials list that matches existing finishes where possible, and sequencing that respects permit and inspection requirements rather than rushing to close walls before an inspector sees the framing.

## Our process

1. **Scope of work and estimate.** We document what was removed during mitigation, what remains structurally sound, and what needs to be rebuilt, then put that into a written scope with line-item pricing before any work begins.
2. **Permits and code review.** Structural work, electrical, and plumbing rough-in typically require permits through the local building department. We pull what's required and schedule inspections at the right points in the build, not after the fact.
3. **Framing and structural repair.** Damaged studs, joists, or subfloor get replaced to match existing structure. This is where fire damage reconstruction differs most from water-related rebuilds: charred framing often needs full replacement rather than drying and reuse.
4. **Rough-in coordination.** Electrical, plumbing, and HVAC trades go back in before insulation and drywall close the wall cavity. We coordinate these trades so inspections pass on the first pass.
5. **Finishing and final walkthrough.** Drywall, texture matching, paint, trim, flooring, and cabinetry get installed to match the home's existing finish as closely as possible, followed by a walkthrough against the original scope.

## What separates a good reconstruction response from a bad one

The most common mistake is closing walls before an inspector or adjuster has seen the framing, which can stall a claim or force a re-opening of finished work. A second is texture and paint mismatch: drywall texture varies by era and region, and a rebuild that doesn't match the surrounding wall looks obviously patched even after paint.

Adjusters typically look for photo documentation at each stage: pre-demolition condition, exposed framing, rough-in before closing, and finished work. Reconstruction crews that skip this documentation leave homeowners without the paper trail needed to close out a claim cleanly. Another gap shows up with cabinetry and flooring: matching an eight-year-old cabinet line or a discontinued flooring run sometimes isn't possible, and a straightforward answer to the homeowner up front avoids disputes later.

Structural reconstruction after fire also requires sign-off that framing members meet current code, not just a visual match to what was there before. A rebuild that skips this step can pass inspection on the surface and still leave a structural question unresolved.

## What does reconstruction cost?

Reconstruction costs vary more than almost any other restoration service because the scope ranges from a single-room patch to a full interior rebuild. Every loss is different, which is why we provide a written scope of work with line-item pricing before work starts rather than a flat estimate over the phone. Homeowners insurance typically covers reconstruction tied to a covered peril (a burst pipe, a kitchen fire) under the dwelling portion of the policy, though code-upgrade costs and some finish-level choices may fall outside coverage.

| Scenario | Typical range |
|---|---|
| Single room, drywall and paint only | $1,500 - $4,500 |
| Bathroom rebuild after water damage | $4,000 - $12,000 |
| Kitchen reconstruction after fire | $15,000 - $45,000 |
| Structural framing repair (single room) | $8,000 - $25,000 |
| Whole-floor reconstruction after major fire | $40,000 - $120,000+ |

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Vista's dry season and surrounding brush-filled canyons mean fire-related reconstruction work tends to pick up in late summer and fall, which can extend permit and inspection timelines when the building department sees a seasonal surge in fire-related rebuilds countywide. Winter's rainy stretch, typically December through March, is the window to watch for exterior work: roof, siding, and window reconstruction scheduled during a wet spell risks new moisture intrusion before the envelope is closed back up. Coastal and inland Vista properties also skew toward older stucco and slab-on-grade construction, which affects how framing repairs and moisture barriers get detailed during rebuild.
