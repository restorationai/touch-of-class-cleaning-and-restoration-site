Smoke doesn't stop moving once the fire is out. Acidic soot particles keep etching into metal, grout, and painted surfaces for days after a fire, and the odor works its way into drywall, subfloor, and ductwork long after the visible damage looks manageable. A kitchen fire that scorched one cabinet can leave a smoke film across an entire floor plan within hours, carried by the home's own air handling system. ## What smoke damage restoration actually involves

Smoke damage restoration is different from fire damage repair in that the structure itself may be largely intact, but every surface the smoke touched needs to be evaluated and treated. That includes walls, ceilings, cabinetry, flooring, ductwork, and contents like upholstery and electronics. The type of smoke matters: a grease fire in the kitchen leaves a thick, sticky protein residue that's nearly invisible but smells intensely, while a structure fire involving synthetic materials (carpet, foam, plastics) leaves a dry, smeary soot that spreads further and stains more aggressively. Equipment typically includes HEPA air scrubbers, thermal foggers to neutralize odor at a molecular level, and in some cases ozone generators for unoccupied spaces. Timelines range from a few days for a contained, single-room event to several weeks when HVAC systems, insulation, and framing cavities are involved.

## Our process

1. **Soot and residue characterization.** We identify whether the residue is protein-based, synthetic, or a wet smoke from a smoldering fire, because each type requires a different cleaning agent and approach. Getting this wrong means re-cleaning surfaces that look fine but still off-gas odor.
2. **Containment and HVAC isolation.** Affected areas get sealed off and the HVAC system is shut down or isolated so soot particles already in the ductwork don't recirculate into clean rooms during the cleanup.
3. **Surface cleaning and residue removal.** Walls, ceilings, trim, and hard surfaces are cleaned with agents matched to the soot type, from dry sponging on delicate surfaces to wet cleaning on washable paint and tile.
4. **Thermal fogging and odor neutralization.** Once surfaces are clean, we treat remaining odor at the source using thermal fogging or HEPA filtration rather than masking it with fragrance, which only hides the problem temporarily.
5. **Final inspection and contents assessment.** We check for residual odor in cavities, behind outlets, and inside ductwork, and flag any contents (textiles, electronics, documents) that need off-site specialty cleaning.

## What separates a good smoke damage response from a bad one

The most common mistake is treating smoke damage like a surface cleaning job and skipping the HVAC system entirely. If ductwork isn't cleaned or sealed, soot recirculates for months and the smell returns every time the system kicks on. Another frequent miss is using an all-purpose cleaner on protein smoke residue, which can actually set the stain and odor into porous materials instead of lifting it. Insurance adjusters pay close attention to documentation of pre-existing versus fire-related damage, so a thorough moisture and soot inspection with photos before any cleaning starts matters for the claim, not just the repair. A rushed job that paints over smoke-stained drywall without neutralizing the odor underneath is one of the most common callbacks in this trade.
