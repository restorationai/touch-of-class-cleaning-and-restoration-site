## What Sewage Cleanup and Sanitization actually involves

Sewage intrusion is classified as Category 3 water under the IICRC S500 standard, meaning it's treated as grossly contaminated regardless of its original source. That classification changes the whole approach: porous materials that might be salvaged after a clean-water leak, carpet pad, drywall below the flood line, insulation, upholstery, usually have to come out. Crews work in full PPE (suits, respirators, nitrile gloves, rubber boots) and extract standing wastewater with trash pumps or truck-mounted vacuum units before any cleaning begins. Hard surfaces get cleaned, disinfected, and in many cases re-cleaned after a dwell time to let the antimicrobial agent work, then the space is dried and monitored like any other water loss. A contained single-room backup might wrap in a few days; a septic overflow that's spread through a crawlspace or slab-on-grade foundation can take longer because of how much material has to be removed and how carefully the subfloor has to be verified dry before rebuild starts.

## Our process

1. **PPE staging and safety perimeter.** Before anyone enters the affected area, the crew suits up and isolates the space to keep contamination from tracking into unaffected rooms.
2. **Raw sewage extraction.** Standing wastewater and solids are pumped and vacuumed out first, separate from any structural drying equipment that will be used later.
3. **Removal of contaminated porous materials.** Carpet, pad, baseboards, and affected drywall are cut and removed per Category 3 protocol rather than cleaned in place.
4. **Cleaning and antimicrobial application.** Remaining hard surfaces and framing are washed, disinfected with an EPA-registered antimicrobial, and given proper dwell time before the space is sealed back up.
5. **Drying, monitoring, and clearance.** Moisture meters and thermal imaging confirm the subfloor and framing are back to a dry standard before any reconstruction begins.

## What separates a good sewage cleanup response from a bad one

The most common shortcut is treating a sewage backup like a regular water loss, cleaning the surface and drying it out without removing porous materials that absorbed contaminated water. That gets a room looking fine while leaving bacteria and odor-causing residue inside wall cavities or under flooring, which usually resurfaces as a smell or a health complaint weeks later. A second mistake is skipping the dwell time on antimicrobial treatments, applying disinfectant and wiping it off before it's had time to actually work. Adjusters reviewing a sewage claim typically want to see documentation that porous materials were removed (not just cleaned), photos of the contamination source, and moisture readings confirming the structure dried to an acceptable standard before rebuild. Missing that paper trail is one of the more common reasons a sewage-related claim gets questioned.
