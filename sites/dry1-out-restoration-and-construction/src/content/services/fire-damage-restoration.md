## What Fire Damage Restoration actually involves

Fire damage restoration is really three jobs running at once: removing the soot and residue, neutralizing the odor at a molecular level, and drying out anything soaked by hose lines or sprinklers before secondary water damage sets in. The type of soot matters. A kitchen grease fire leaves a different residue than a fire that burned synthetic furniture or electrical wiring, and each requires a different cleaning agent and approach. Crews typically work room by room: content inventory and pack-out for anything salvageable, dry and wet sponge cleaning on structural surfaces, HEPA vacuuming, and thermal fogging or ozone treatment to break down odor molecules that have bonded to porous materials. Depending on the extent of the fire, this process runs anywhere from a few days for a contained, single-room fire to several weeks when structural framing, insulation, or HVAC ductwork needs to be opened up and replaced.

## Our process

1. **Soot and residue characterization.** Before any cleaning starts, we identify whether the residue is protein-based, synthetic/plastic-based, or a mix, because using the wrong cleaning agent can set a stain permanently into drywall, wood trim, or fabric.
2. **Structural stabilization and water mitigation.** Suppression water, fire hose runoff, or sprinkler discharge gets extracted and dried per the IICRC S500 standard before odor and soot work begins, otherwise you end up fighting mold on top of smoke damage.
3. **Contents pack-out and cleaning.** Salvageable belongings are inventoried, photographed, and moved off-site for ultrasonic or specialty cleaning while structural work happens, which also protects them from dust and cross-contamination during repairs.
4. **Thermal fogging and ozone or hydroxyl treatment.** Heat-based fogging mimics how smoke originally penetrated materials, forcing deodorizing agents into the same pores the smoke reached. Ozone or hydroxyl generators then run in a sealed space to break down remaining odor compounds in air and soft materials.
5. **Final cleaning, seal, and reconstruction handoff.** Surfaces are cleaned or sealed with an odor-blocking primer where needed, and any framing, drywall, or flooring that didn't survive gets scoped for rebuild.

## What separates a good fire damage response from a bad one

The most common mistake is treating fire damage like a cleaning job instead of a chemistry problem. Soot is acidic, and the longer it sits, the more it etches into metal fixtures, glass, and even porcelain. A rushed job wipes visible soot off walls but skips the HVAC system, which keeps recirculating smoke odor for months after the "cleanup" is done. Another miss is odor masking instead of odor removal, spraying perfume over the problem instead of using ozone or hydroxyl treatment to actually break down the smoke molecules bonded into drywall and insulation. Insurance adjusters look closely at documentation: photos of soot patterns before cleaning, moisture readings from suppression water, and a clear scope distinguishing what was cleaned versus what had to be removed and replaced. Structural fire damage that isn't fully opened up and inspected, especially around electrical runs and roof framing, can hide damage that shows up as a warped beam or a charred stud years later.
