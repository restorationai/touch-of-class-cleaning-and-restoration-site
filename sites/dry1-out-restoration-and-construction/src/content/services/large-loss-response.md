## What a large loss response actually involves

Large loss and catastrophic restoration is defined less by the type of damage and more by its scale and complexity. A loss typically crosses into "large loss" territory when it spans multiple units or structures, involves a commercial or multi-family property, or carries an estimated claim value that insurers route to a dedicated large-loss or catastrophe adjuster rather than a standard field adjuster. That changes how the job runs. Instead of a two- or three-person crew with a handful of air movers, a catastrophic response calls for coordinated teams working multiple structures simultaneously, industrial-capacity extraction and desiccant dehumidification equipment, and documentation built around Xactimate line-item estimating from day one because the carrier's review process is more rigorous at this claim size.

Timeline matters more here, not less. A delayed response on a single-unit water loss means one family out of their home longer. A delayed response on a 60-unit complex means 60 units accumulating secondary damage, tenants relocated, and a property owner losing rental income on every day the structure sits wet or unsecured.

## Our process

1. **Scene assessment and multi-structure scoping.** Before equipment moves, we map the full extent of the loss across every affected unit or structure, not just the units with the most visible damage, because moisture and smoke migration rarely respect unit lines.
2. **Resource mobilization.** Crew size, drying equipment count, and any subcontracted trades (electrical, structural, demo) are scaled to the footprint of the loss, not deployed incrementally as problems surface.
3. **Carrier and adjuster coordination.** On large losses, we work directly with the assigned large-loss adjuster or catastrophe team, aligning our Xactimate scope with their review process and providing daily or weekly progress documentation rather than a single final report.
4. **Phased mitigation and drying.** Affected units or zones are prioritized by severity and by what's needed to get occupants or tenants back in place fastest, with moisture monitoring tracked per zone so drying decisions are made on data, not guesswork.
5. **Reconciliation and closeout.** Photo documentation, moisture logs, and equipment run-time records are compiled into a file built to withstand carrier audit, since large claims get scrutinized more closely than standard ones.

## What separates a good large loss response from a bad one

The most common failure on catastrophic jobs isn't technical, it's logistical: a contractor underestimates how much equipment and labor the scope actually requires and tries to run a multi-structure loss like three separate single-unit jobs. That shows up as inconsistent drying times across units, missed secondary moisture in shared walls or chases, and a documentation trail too thin to support the claim value.

Adjusters reviewing large-loss files look for a few specific things: moisture readings logged per room or unit (not just a single summary number), equipment placement that matches the documented wet area, and a clear chain of custody on anything removed for drying or disposal. Jobs that get flagged for supplemental review usually have gaps in exactly these areas. A property owner and their adjuster are both better served by a scope document that holds up to scrutiny the first time than by a faster-looking estimate that gets kicked back for revisions.

## What does large loss and catastrophic response cost?

Cost on large-loss work scales with structure count, square footage, and the category of water or degree of fire and smoke involved, so industry-typical ranges are wide. Every loss gets a written scope before work begins, built from direct measurement and photo documentation rather than a flat estimate. In most cases, the property's commercial or multi-family policy covers mitigation and reconstruction for a covered peril, though coverage specifics (business interruption, code-upgrade coverage, ordinance and law) vary by policy and are worth reviewing with the adjuster early.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Vista's dry season brings Santa Ana wind events that elevate wildfire risk for commercial and multi-family properties near wildland-urban interface zones, while the winter months bring atmospheric river storms capable of overwhelming aging roof systems and site drainage on larger commercial footprints. Properties built before modern stormwater codes are more prone to large-scale water intrusion during these events, which is often how a single storm turns into a multi-structure, large-loss claim rather than an isolated repair.
