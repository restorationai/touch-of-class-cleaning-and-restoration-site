**Water pooling under a retail stockroom at 2 a.m., or smoke rolling through an office HVAC system after an electrical fire, doesn't wait for business hours.** A burst supply line behind a break room sink, a kitchen fire in a strip-mall restaurant, or a roof leak that's been dripping into a drop ceiling for a week all threaten the same thing for a business owner: lost revenue for every day the doors stay closed. Commercial restoration is built around that clock, mitigating the damage while keeping as much of the operation running as the loss allows.

## What commercial restoration actually involves

Commercial losses behave differently than residential ones. A flooded office suite might have raised flooring, server rooms, and shared HVAC returns that can spread moisture or smoke odor to tenants who never had a leak. A restaurant fire brings grease-laden protein soot that clings to surfaces and migrates through duct work fast. Retail spaces have merchandise, point-of-sale systems, and foot traffic to account for during drying or demolition. Equipment for commercial work scales up accordingly: industrial air movers and LGR dehumidifiers for larger square footage, negative air machines to keep contamination out of occupied areas, and thermal foggers or HEPA scrubbers for smoke and odor. Timelines depend heavily on occupancy type. A single office suite might dry out in three to five days; a multi-tenant building with shared mechanical systems can take longer simply because every affected zone needs to be verified dry or clean before reoccupancy.

## Our process

1. **Site safety and emergency mitigation.** Power and water are shut down where needed, structural hazards are flagged, and standing water or active smoke is addressed first so the building is safe to work in.
2. **Scope and documentation for the claim.** Affected square footage, equipment, inventory, and finishes are photographed and logged, because commercial carriers and adjusters expect a defensible paper trail tied to business interruption coverage.
3. **Extraction, drying, or soot and odor removal.** Water losses move into extraction and structural drying per IICRC S500 guidelines; fire losses move into soot characterization, surface cleaning, and thermal fogging or ozone treatment for odor, depending on what the soot type requires.
4. **Phased work to protect occupancy.** Where possible, the space is sectioned so unaffected areas can reopen or stay in use while the damaged zone is contained and worked.
5. **Final verification and sign-off.** Moisture readings, air quality, or odor checks are documented and a walkthrough is done with the property manager or owner before the space is handed back.

## What separates a good commercial restoration response from a bad one

The most common mistake on commercial jobs is treating them like oversized residential ones. A crew that doesn't account for shared HVAC returns can push smoke odor or moisture into neighboring units, turning a one-tenant loss into a multi-tenant claim. Skipping containment between work areas and active retail or office space is another frequent miss, it invites cross-contamination and complaints from staff or customers. Adjusters reviewing commercial claims look closely at pre-loss documentation, equipment logs, and whether drying or cleaning followed a recognized standard, not just whether the space looks dry or clean. A thorough response also documents business interruption factors early, since those numbers are harder to reconstruct after the fact than they are to log in real time.

## What does commercial restoration cost?

Commercial restoration costs vary more than residential work because they depend on square footage, occupancy type, and whether the business needs to stay partially open during the work. Every loss gets a written scope before work begins, since an office suite with clean water behaves nothing like a restaurant kitchen fire. Most commercial property policies cover mitigation and reconstruction costs, and many include business interruption coverage, though specifics depend on the policy and carrier.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Vista's dry season brings a higher risk of electrical and kitchen-equipment fires in commercial spaces, particularly in older strip-mall buildings with aging wiring. The winter rainy months bring the opposite problem: flat commercial roofs and parapet drains that haven't been maintained are a common source of slow leaks into ceiling tiles and office drywall, often going unnoticed until a tenant reports a stain or odor. Buildings with rooftop HVAC units are especially prone to leaks around flashing and ductwork penetrations during heavy rain.
