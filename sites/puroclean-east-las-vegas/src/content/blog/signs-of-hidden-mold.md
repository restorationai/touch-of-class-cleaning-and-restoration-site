Mold doesn't always announce itself with a black stain on the ceiling. More often it's growing inside a wall cavity, under a bathroom vanity, or beneath flooring that got wet once and never fully dried — and the only clue is a smell you can't place or a cough that won't quit. If something feels off in your home but you can't find the source, these seven signs are worth taking seriously.

## 1. A Persistent Musty Odor You Can't Trace

Mold produces microbial volatile organic compounds (mVOCs) as it digests organic material — drywall paper, wood framing, carpet backing. That earthy, damp-basement smell is the byproduct. If you notice it in a specific room but can't find visible growth, run your hand along the baseboard near exterior walls and under windows. In Las Vegas, homes built before 2000 often have single-pane windows that sweat in winter, soaking the sill framing repeatedly over years. A smell that's stronger when the HVAC kicks on suggests mold may be inside the ductwork or near an air handler.

## 2. Unexplained Allergy Symptoms That Improve When You Leave Home

Mold spores are small enough to stay airborne for hours. If someone in the household has a runny nose, itchy eyes, or a dry cough that clears up on weekends away or during vacation — and returns within a day of coming home — that pattern is worth noting. It doesn't confirm mold, and nothing in this post is medical advice, but it's a meaningful data point. Keep a simple log: symptoms, time of day, which rooms. That information will be useful whether you end up calling an allergist or a remediation company.

## 3. Visible Discoloration That Isn't Quite Dirt

Not all mold looks like the dramatic black patches you see in photos. Early-stage growth can appear as a faint gray or greenish smear, a fuzzy white patch on grout, or a slightly darker section of drywall that looks like a water stain but hasn't changed in weeks. Check behind furniture pushed against exterior walls, inside closets on the north-facing wall of a room, and along the bottom edge of bathroom drywall near the tub. If you wipe a spot with a dry cloth and it comes back within a few days, it's likely biological, not just dust.

## 4. Warping, Buckling, or Soft Spots in Walls and Floors

Mold needs moisture, and moisture changes the structure of building materials before mold is visible. Press gently on drywall near a sink, tub, or exterior wall. Soft or spongy drywall means the paper facing and gypsum core have absorbed water. On hardwood or laminate floors, look for boards that have cupped (edges curving upward) or that feel springy underfoot — signs of moisture trapped beneath. In Las Vegas, monsoon-season water intrusion through improperly sealed stucco is a common culprit. A single heavy August storm can push water into a wall cavity that stays damp for weeks in the sealed, air-conditioned environment.

## 5. A Past Leak or Flood That Was Never Professionally Dried

Mold can colonize wet drywall within 24 to 48 hours under the right temperature and humidity conditions. If your home had a slow plumbing leak, a dishwasher overflow, or any water intrusion that was cleaned up with towels and a fan — but never assessed with moisture meters — there's a real chance the structural materials behind the surface stayed wet long enough for mold to take hold. This is especially common after supply-line failures under sinks, which can drip slowly for weeks before the cabinet floor gives it away.

## 6. Rust Stains or Efflorescence on Concrete and Masonry

White chalky deposits (efflorescence) on a block wall, garage floor, or stucco exterior mean water has been moving through the material and depositing minerals on the surface. Rust streaks around fasteners in a garage or utility room tell the same story. These aren't mold themselves, but they're reliable indicators that moisture is present at levels that support mold growth on adjacent framing, insulation, or drywall.

## 7. Your HVAC System Has Never Been Inspected After a Water Event

Air handlers and ductwork are dark, often humid environments. If your system pulled in air during or after a flood, leak, or even a long period of high indoor humidity, the interior surfaces of the air handler — the evaporator coil housing, the drain pan, the blower compartment — can harbor mold that then circulates through every room every time the system runs. If you've had any water event in the utility closet or attic where your air handler lives, have the system inspected before assuming the rest of the house is clean.

## What To Do If You Recognize These Signs

Start with documentation, not demolition. Take photos of any visible discoloration, warping, or staining. Note where and when you first noticed each sign. If you have a hygrometer (a basic one costs under $15 at any hardware store), check the relative humidity in the affected room — anything consistently above 60% in a Las Vegas home is elevated enough to sustain mold growth.

**Don't** spray bleach on suspected mold and consider the problem solved. Bleach kills surface mold on non-porous materials like tile, but it doesn't penetrate drywall or wood, and it doesn't address the moisture source. Disturbing mold without containment can release spores into areas of the home that were previously unaffected.

**Don't** run a box fan across a moldy surface. Airflow disperses spores.

If the affected area is larger than roughly 10 square feet — about a 3×3 patch — the EPA recommends professional remediation rather than DIY cleanup. That threshold drops further if anyone in the home has asthma, a compromised immune system, or is very young or elderly.

## When To Call a Professional

Call a remediation company when: you can smell mold but can't find it, the suspected area is inside walls or under flooring, the growth covers more than a small surface area, or the affected space includes an HVAC component. A qualified remediator will use moisture meters and sometimes thermal imaging to find hidden moisture, establish containment to keep spores from spreading during work, remove affected materials, and dry the structure to documented moisture levels before any reconstruction begins.

## The Recovery Process: What Comes After Remediation

Professional mold remediation typically involves containment of the work area with plastic sheeting and negative air pressure, physical removal of affected materials (drywall, insulation, sometimes framing), HEPA vacuuming and antimicrobial treatment of remaining surfaces, and drying verification with moisture meters before the area is closed up again. Depending on the scope, the remediation itself may take one to several days. Reconstruction — new drywall, flooring, paint — follows once clearance moisture readings are confirmed. Most homeowners' insurance policies cover mold remediation when it results from a sudden, covered water loss (like a burst pipe), but not from long-term neglect or maintenance issues. Document everything before any work begins.
