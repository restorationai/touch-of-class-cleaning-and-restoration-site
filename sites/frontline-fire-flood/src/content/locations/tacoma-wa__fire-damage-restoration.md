Tacoma's mix of century-old Craftsman bungalows, mid-century wood-frame construction, and newer infill development means a house fire here rarely behaves the way it does in a city built mostly after 1980. Older balloon-frame homes, common throughout the city's historic neighborhoods, allow smoke and heat to travel vertically inside wall cavities with almost no resistance, pushing soot and toxic residue into attic spaces and crawlspaces long before the fire department clears the scene. When that happens, the visible char is only part of the story. Frontline Fire & Flood's IICRC FSRT-certified technicians respond 24/7 and understand what Tacoma's housing stock actually hides after a fire.

## Why Tacoma's Building Stock Shapes Fire Damage Restoration

Tacoma's residential neighborhoods developed in distinct waves, Victorian-era homes near the waterfront, post-war ramblers spreading south and east, and a wave of infill construction that accelerated after the 2010s. Each era brought different framing methods, insulation materials, and interior finishes, and each responds to fire and smoke differently.

Balloon framing, used widely in homes built before roughly 1940, lacks the fire stops that platform-frame construction introduced later. In a balloon-framed home, a kitchen fire can push smoke through the wall cavity all the way to the attic in minutes. Plaster-and-lath walls, still intact in many of Tacoma's older properties, absorb smoke odor compounds differently than modern drywall. The plaster itself is dense and slow to off-gas, which means odor neutralization takes longer and requires thermal fogging or hydroxyl treatment rather than surface wiping alone.

Tacoma also sits in a marine climate where post-fire humidity management is a real concern. After a fire is extinguished, firefighting water combines with Puget Sound's naturally damp air to accelerate secondary damage, mold colonization can begin within 24 to 48 hours in an unventilated, wet structure. Drying and smoke restoration have to happen in parallel, not sequentially.

## Our Fire Damage Restoration Process in Tacoma

Every fire loss is different, but the sequence of decisions matters. When we arrive, the first priority is a safety and structural assessment, determining which areas are safe to enter, whether utilities need to remain isolated, and where the smoke migration boundary actually is (which is almost always larger than it looks).

From there, the process moves through these stages:

- **Board-up and tarping** to secure the structure against Tacoma's frequent rain while work is underway
- **Contents inventory and pack-out** for salvageable items, documented for your insurance carrier
- **Controlled demolition** of unsalvageable materials, charred framing, smoke-saturated insulation, compromised drywall or plaster
- **HEPA vacuuming and dry sponge cleaning** of smoke residue on structural surfaces
- **Odor neutralization** using thermal fogging and hydroxyl generation calibrated to the structure's size and material composition
- **Structural drying** to address firefighting water before reconstruction begins
- **Reconstruction coordination**, handled in-house so you are not managing separate contractors

Because we hold both IICRC FSRT (Fire & Smoke Restoration) and IICRC ASD (Applied Structural Drying) certifications, smoke and moisture work are managed as a single scope, not handed off between crews.

## Local Note

In Tacoma's older neighborhoods, particularly those with homes built before World War II, original fir subfloor and old-growth framing lumber is still common beneath later renovations. After a fire, that dense old-growth wood can hold smoke odor compounds deep in the grain even after the surface appears clean. Standard deodorization protocols designed for modern lumber sometimes underperform on these materials. We extend dwell times on odor treatments and re-test with air sampling before closing out the scope, because a home that smells fine on day three can off-gas again when summer temperatures rise and the structure warms.
