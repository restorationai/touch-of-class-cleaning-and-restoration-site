University Place sits in a pocket of Pierce County where marine air off Puget Sound meets the shade cast by mature Douglas firs and Western red cedars, a combination that keeps outdoor surfaces damp well into summer and gives mold spores exactly the low-light, high-humidity conditions they prefer. When that moisture finds its way into a crawl space, behind vinyl siding, or beneath a bathroom subfloor, the clock starts immediately: mold can begin colonizing porous materials within 24 to 48 hours of a moisture event. If you're noticing a musty odor that doesn't clear with ventilation, discoloration on walls or ceilings, or a recent water intrusion you thought dried on its own, those are signals worth taking seriously.

## Why University Place Properties See Mold Issues

The housing stock across University Place skews toward mid-century construction, ranch-style homes, split-levels, and early tract developments built through the 1950s, '60s, and '70s. Many of those homes were framed with old-growth lumber that is dense and rot-resistant on its own, but the insulation and vapor barrier standards of that era were minimal by today's codes. Crawl spaces in particular were often left with bare soil, inadequate cross-ventilation, and no continuous poly sheeting, conditions that draw ground moisture upward year-round.

The area's clay-heavy soils compound the problem. Clay drains slowly, so after a hard Pacific Northwest rain, water pools against foundation walls and perimeter footings longer than it would in sandier ground. That sustained contact is enough to push moisture through block or poured-concrete foundations and into the structural framing above. Homes closer to Chambers Bay, where the terrain drops toward the water, can see this effect amplified by a higher seasonal water table.

Modern additions and remodels sometimes introduce a different vulnerability: improperly flashed window and door penetrations, or bathroom tile work installed over greenboard rather than cement backer. Both are common sources of slow, hidden leaks that go undetected until mold is already established behind the finished surface.

## Our Mold Remediation Process in University Place

Remediation starts with containment, not cleaning. Before any affected material is disturbed, the work area is sealed with polyethylene sheeting and placed under negative air pressure using HEPA-filtered air scrubbers. This keeps spores from migrating to unaffected rooms while the crew works.

Once containment is established, the source of moisture is identified and addressed. Removing mold without correcting the underlying moisture condition is a temporary fix at best. Moisture meters, thermal imaging, and borescope inspection help locate water pathways that aren't visible at the surface, particularly useful in the plaster-over-wood-lath walls found in older University Place homes, where surface readings can appear dry while the lath behind holds significant moisture.

Affected porous materials, drywall, insulation, carpet padding, are removed, bagged, and disposed of per Washington State Department of Ecology guidelines. Structural framing that can be cleaned and dried in place is treated with an EPA-registered antimicrobial and dried to verified moisture content before containment is removed. The team holds IICRC AMRT (Applied Microbial Remediation Technician) and IICRC WRT (Water Damage Restoration Technician) certifications, and the firm is EPA Lead-Safe Certified, relevant in University Place's older housing stock where lead-based paint can be disturbed during remediation work.

In University Place's older ranch-style homes, bathroom exhaust fans were frequently ducted into the attic cavity rather than through the roof, a code violation by current standards but common in construction from the 1960s and '70s.
