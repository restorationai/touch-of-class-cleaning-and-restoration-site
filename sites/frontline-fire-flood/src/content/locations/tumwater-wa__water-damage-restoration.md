Tumwater sits at the southern tip of Puget Sound, where marine air funnels up the Deschutes River corridor and annual rainfall regularly soaks the region from October through April. That persistent moisture, combined with a housing stock that ranges from mid-century ramblers to newer subdivisions built on the area's characteristic clay-heavy soils, creates conditions where a single plumbing failure or roof leak can escalate faster than it would in drier climates. When water gets into your walls or under your floors here, the damp ambient air slows evaporation and gives mold a shorter window to take hold than most homeowners expect.

## Why Tumwater Properties Face Elevated Water Damage Risk

Thurston County's clay-dominant soils drain slowly. After a heavy rain event, that soil stays saturated for days, and hydrostatic pressure against foundation walls and crawl space vapor barriers is a real and recurring problem for homes throughout Tumwater. Crawl spaces are especially common in this area's housing stock, and a compromised vapor barrier or a blocked perimeter drain can push moisture up through subfloor assemblies long after the rain stops.

Older homes, many built in the 1950s through 1970s, often have galvanized steel supply lines that have spent decades corroding from the inside out. A pinhole leak behind a kitchen cabinet or bathroom vanity can saturate wall cavities and subfloor sheathing for weeks before anyone notices the soft spot underfoot. Newer construction is not immune either: Tumwater's growth over the past two decades brought tract homes with complex rooflines and more penetration points where flashing can fail during wind-driven rain.

The city's position near the Capitol Lake basin also means that storm events occasionally overwhelm older stormwater infrastructure, and surface water intrusion into garages and lower-level living spaces is a pattern that comes up repeatedly in this part of Thurston County.

## Our Water Damage Restoration Process in Tumwater

Every job starts with a thorough moisture assessment, not just a visual walk-through. Thermal imaging cameras and calibrated moisture meters let us read what is happening behind drywall and under flooring before a single panel comes off. In Tumwater's older homes, that step matters more than usual: plaster-and-lath construction, still present in many pre-1960 properties, holds water differently than modern drywall. The surface can read nearly dry while the lath and framing behind it stay saturated.

Once we have a clear moisture map, water extraction begins using truck-mounted and portable extraction units sized to the loss. Structural drying follows using commercial-grade desiccant and refrigerant dehumidifiers paired with high-velocity air movers, positioned according to a drying plan calibrated to the ambient conditions on that specific day, because a drying setup that works in July performs differently in a Tumwater November when outdoor humidity is already near saturation.

We monitor drying progress with daily readings and adjust equipment placement as materials dry unevenly. Nothing comes off-rent until moisture readings confirm the structure has reached its pre-loss equilibrium. Our team holds IICRC Water Restoration Technician (WRT) and Applied Structural Drying (ASD) certifications, and drying protocols follow the IICRC S500 standard.
