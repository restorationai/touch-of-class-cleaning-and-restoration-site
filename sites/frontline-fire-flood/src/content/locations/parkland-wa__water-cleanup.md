## Why Parkland Properties See Water Damage Issues

Parkland's housing stock spans several decades, and the mix matters when water gets in. Homes built in the 1960s and 1970s, a common vintage throughout the unincorporated Pierce County neighborhoods that make up much of the area, were often constructed on crawl space foundations with minimal vapor barriers. When gutters overflow or ground saturation rises, that crawl space becomes the first casualty: insulation soaks through, floor joists absorb moisture, and the musty smell works its way up into living areas before a homeowner realizes anything is wrong.

The area's clay-heavy soils compound the problem. Unlike sandy or gravelly ground that drains quickly, clay holds water near the surface and directs it toward foundations rather than away from them. A heavy rain event that would be unremarkable in a well-drained neighborhood can mean several inches of standing water in a crawl space here. Pacific Lutheran University sits near the heart of Parkland, and the surrounding residential streets, many of them with mature tree canopies and aging stormwater infrastructure, see recurring drainage issues when those trees shed debris and clog downspouts during fall storms.

## Our Water Cleanup Process in Parkland

Every water cleanup job starts with moisture mapping, not assumptions. Technicians use thermal imaging cameras and calibrated moisture meters to find water that has migrated behind walls, under flooring, and into insulation, places that look fine to the eye but are still actively wet. That step matters especially in older Parkland homes where plaster walls and original hardwood floors can mask significant moisture retention.

Once the full scope is documented, truck-mounted extraction units pull standing water from the structure. Industrial air movers and low-grain refrigerant dehumidifiers are then positioned according to the IICRC S500 drying standard, which accounts for the specific volume of wet material, ambient humidity, and airflow patterns in the space. In Parkland's climate, where outdoor relative humidity rarely drops low enough to help, dehumidification does most of the work, and equipment typically runs continuously until moisture readings return to pre-loss baselines across all affected materials.

For jobs involving sewage backup or flood water from outside the structure, all affected porous materials are handled as contaminated waste per industry Category 3 water protocols, and the area is treated and documented accordingly before any drying equipment is placed.
