## Why Auburn Properties Are Especially Vulnerable to Storm Damage

Auburn's position in the Green River valley means stormwater has nowhere fast to go. The valley floor's relatively flat gradient slows drainage, and during sustained rainfall events the ground saturates quickly, raising the effective water table close to slab level in some older neighborhoods. Homes built on the valley floor in the 1960s and 1970s, a large share of Auburn's residential stock, often have crawl spaces rather than full basements, and those crawl spaces become the first casualty when wind-driven rain compromises a roof or a tree limb punches through a soffit.

The city also sits in a recognized wind corridor. Storms tracking northeast from the Olympic Peninsula or south from the Snoqualmie Pass area accelerate through the valley, and mature Douglas firs and big-leaf maples, common on larger lots throughout Auburn, become projectiles when root systems are loosened by weeks of wet soil. Tree-impact damage to roofs, gutters, and exterior walls is among the most common storm calls in this part of King County.

Auburn's older housing stock adds another layer of complexity. Homes from the mid-century era frequently have original cedar shake or composition shingle roofs that have been patched rather than replaced, and those aging surfaces fail faster under impact or sustained wind load than modern architectural shingles. Once the roof envelope is breached, water tracks along rafters and top plates before it ever appears on a ceiling, meaning the visible stain you see inside is rarely the full extent of the intrusion.

## Our Storm Damage Restoration Process in Auburn

Every storm response starts with a systematic exterior and interior assessment before any work begins. We document the breach point, whether that's a displaced ridge cap, a tree-impact hole, or failed flashing around a chimney, and photograph it for your insurance carrier before we touch anything. Temporary protective measures go up first: heavy-duty poly tarping over roof openings, board-up of broken windows or doors, and sandbag or berm placement if active water intrusion is still occurring.

Once the structure is stabilized, we move inside. Moisture mapping with thermal imaging and calibrated meters tells us where water has traveled beyond the obvious wet zone. In Auburn's crawl-space homes, that often means pulling up sections of subfloor or accessing the crawl to check for saturation in the joist bays, areas that look fine from above but are holding water below. Industrial air movers and desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers run in a calculated configuration based on the IICRC S500 standard for structural drying, with daily moisture readings to track progress and adjust equipment placement.

Debris removal, downed limbs, displaced roofing materials, damaged siding, is handled concurrently so the property is safe for the restoration crew and for your family if you're still in the home.

## Local Note

One pattern that shows up repeatedly in Auburn's valley-floor neighborhoods is what crews call a "hidden soffit failure." Wind lifts a section of aluminum soffit, water enters the eave cavity, and the homeowner sees nothing inside for days, sometimes weeks, because the water is wicking laterally along the top of the ceiling drywall rather than dripping through. By the time a stain appears, the insulation above the ceiling is fully saturated and mold has had a meaningful head start. If your soffit took a hit in a storm, it's worth having the eave cavity checked with a moisture meter even if your ceilings look dry.
