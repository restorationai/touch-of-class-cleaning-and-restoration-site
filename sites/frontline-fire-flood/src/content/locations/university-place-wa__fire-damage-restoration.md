## Why University Place Properties See Distinctive Fire Damage Patterns

Much of University Place's residential stock was built between the late 1950s and early 1980s, a construction era that used materials and methods that complicate post-fire restoration in specific ways. Older fiberglass batt insulation absorbs smoke odor compounds deeply and rarely cleans, it almost always requires full removal. Plywood subfloors from that period can delaminate from heat exposure even when the surface looks intact, a failure that doesn't always show up until restoration crews pull back flooring. Aluminum wiring, present in a meaningful share of homes built during the 1960s and early 1970s in Pierce County, requires licensed electrical inspection before any reconstruction work begins, a step that affects the project timeline and should be factored into your insurance claim from the start.

The climate adds another layer. Western Washington's persistent humidity means that water used in fire suppression doesn't dry on its own, it migrates into framing and insulation within hours, setting up secondary mold colonization if drying equipment isn't deployed quickly. On a fire job, that means the restoration crew is managing two damage types simultaneously: smoke and char on one hand, and moisture intrusion on the other.

## The Fire Damage Restoration Process Here

Every fire loss is different, but the sequence of decisions follows a consistent logic calibrated to what crews actually encounter in University Place homes. The first priority after the fire marshal releases the structure is a room-by-room scope: thermal imaging to locate hidden heat pockets, air quality readings to understand soot particulate levels, and moisture mapping to document suppression water before it migrates further.

From there, the work moves in phases. Unsalvageable materials, heavily charred framing, smoke-saturated insulation, delaminated subfloor panels, are documented, photographed, and removed before any cleaning begins. Cleaning smoke residue from surfaces that remain requires matching the cleaning chemistry to the soot type: dry soot from fast-burning fires responds to dry-chemical sponges; wet, oily soot from smoldering synthetic materials needs alkaline cleaners and more aggressive agitation. Odor control follows, using thermal fogging and hydroxyl generation to reach the same surfaces smoke reached, inside ductwork, inside wall cavities, inside cabinetry. The IICRC FSRT standard guides the process throughout.

Reconstruction, framing, drywall, insulation, finish work, begins only after clearance readings confirm the structure is clean and dry. Frontline Fire & Flood holds EPA Lead-Safe certification, which matters in University Place's older housing stock where lead paint in pre-1978 homes must be handled under specific containment protocols during demolition.
