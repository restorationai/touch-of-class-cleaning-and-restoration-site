## Why Kent Properties See Distinctive Storm Damage

The Green River Valley's flat floor drains slowly. When a storm drops two or more inches of rain in a short window, common in western Washington's fall and winter seasons, surface water backs up before the valley's stormwater system can move it. That standing water finds its way into crawl spaces, garages, and slab-on-grade foundations faster than most homeowners expect.

Kent's hillside neighborhoods, where the terrain rises sharply toward the east and west benches, face a different problem: slope drainage and soil saturation that can undermine root systems and send mature trees into rooftops and fences. The valley floor and the hillside zones are both storm-vulnerable, just in different ways.

Housing stock matters here too. Kent has a substantial inventory of homes built in the 1960s through the 1980s, when construction standards for roof sheathing and window flashing were less stringent than current code. Older cedar shake roofs, still common in some of the quieter residential pockets, are especially susceptible to wind uplift and debris impact. Once a shake or shingle field is breached, water tracks along rafters and into wall cavities quickly, and the damage visible from the attic rarely tells the full story of what's happening inside the wall assembly.

## Our Storm Damage Restoration Process in Kent

The first priority after a storm is stopping ongoing water entry. That means emergency tarping of compromised roofs, boarding of broken windows or doors, and temporary weatherproofing before any restoration work begins. We document everything photographically before we move or remove a single piece of material, that documentation is the foundation of your insurance claim.

Once the structure is stabilized, we assess moisture penetration using thermal imaging and calibrated moisture meters. Water driven by wind behaves differently than a slow pipe leak: it travels farther horizontally, saturates insulation behind siding, and can reach interior wall cavities that look completely dry from the surface. We map the full extent of intrusion before scoping the dryout.

Structural drying in Kent's climate requires equipment sized for the ambient humidity. Western Washington's baseline relative humidity is high enough that passive drying is rarely adequate, we use industrial dehumidifiers and directed airflow to bring structural materials within acceptable moisture content ranges, following IICRC S500 and S520 drying protocols. Tree-impact damage that has fractured roof decking or wall framing gets a structural assessment before any cosmetic repair begins, so nothing gets closed up until the framing is confirmed sound.
