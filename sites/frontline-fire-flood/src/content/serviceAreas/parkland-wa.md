## Restoration emergencies common in Parkland

Parkland sits in the Puget Sound lowlands, where the Pacific Northwest's wet season runs long and the ground stays saturated for months at a stretch. Annual rainfall consistently exceeds 38 inches, and the stretch from October through March brings extended periods of heavy rain that test gutters, downspouts, and aging foundation drainage systems. Crawlspace flooding is among the most common calls here, water pools against foundation walls, vapor barriers fail, and wood framing begins absorbing moisture before a homeowner notices anything from inside the house. The smell of mildew in a bedroom or a soft spot in a subfloor is often the first sign that something has been wet for weeks.

Parkland's housing stock also includes a significant share of mid-century construction, homes built in the postwar decades when the area grew rapidly alongside Joint Base Lewis-McChord. Those structures often have original plumbing, minimal crawlspace ventilation, and wall assemblies that hold moisture rather than releasing it. When a supply line fails or a roof leak goes undetected through a rainy season, water can travel far inside a wall cavity before it surfaces.

Fire risk is real year-round, but late-summer dry spells bring elevated danger. Smoke and soot from structure fires penetrate HVAC systems and settle into porous materials, carpet, insulation, wood framing, in ways that require more than surface cleaning to resolve.

## Services we provide in Parkland

**Water damage restoration**, Crawlspace flooding, burst pipes, appliance failures, and storm-driven intrusion are the most frequent water losses in Parkland. The work involves more than extraction: moisture meters and thermal imaging locate water that has migrated behind plaster or under flooring, and drying equipment runs until readings confirm the structure has returned to safe levels.

**Fire and smoke damage restoration**, After a structure fire, smoke residue continues to corrode surfaces and off-gas odors for days. Restoration includes debris removal, structural drying if suppression water is involved, soot cleaning on all affected surfaces, odor neutralization, and coordination with your insurance adjuster throughout.

**Mold remediation**, Parkland's damp climate means mold can begin colonizing within 24 to 48 hours of a moisture event. Remediation follows established industry protocols: containment, HEPA filtration, removal of affected materials, and treatment of the underlying moisture source so the problem does not return.

**Reconstruction**, Once mitigation is complete, the same team handles structural repairs, framing, drywall, flooring, and finish work, so you are not managing two separate contractors through an already stressful process.

**Biohazard and specialty cleanup**, Sewage backups, trauma scenes, and hazardous material incidents require handling that goes beyond standard restoration. These jobs are managed with appropriate containment and disposal protocols.

## Building stock, site conditions, and permits in Parkland

Much of Parkland developed between the 1940s and 1970s, with another wave of construction through the 1980s and 1990s. Older homes in the area commonly sit over unconditioned crawlspaces, a foundation type that concentrates moisture risk at the floor system. Plumbing in mid-century homes may include galvanized steel supply lines, which corrode from the inside out and can fail with little warning; homes from the 1980s may have polybutylene supply piping, a material known for brittleness over time. Both eras carry the potential for asbestos-containing materials in floor tile, pipe insulation, and textured drywall compound, which affects how a restoration scope is written and what abatement steps precede any demolition.

Parkland is an unincorporated community in Pierce County, so building permits for structural repair and reconstruction are issued through Pierce County rather than a city building department. Pierce County enforces the Washington State Building Code, and the region falls within a moderate seismic zone, a factor that can affect how structural repairs are designed and permitted. The soils across much of the South Sound lowlands include glacial till and clay-heavy layers that drain slowly, contributing to the crawlspace and foundation water problems common in the area.
