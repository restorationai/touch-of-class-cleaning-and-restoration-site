If water is actively spreading through your home right now, stop the source first, find your main water shutoff valve and turn it off. Then move people and pets out of the affected area. Everything after that matters, but nothing matters more than those two steps. The next 24 hours will determine how much of your home can be saved, how much mold you'll be fighting in two weeks, and how smoothly your insurance claim goes. Here's what to do, in order.

## Stop the Water and Make the Space Safe

If the source is a burst pipe, a failed supply line under a sink, or a washing machine hose, your main shutoff valve is usually located where the water line enters the house, in a utility room, crawlspace, or near the water meter at the street. Turn it clockwise until it stops. If you're on a well, switch off the pump at the breaker.

Once the water is off, assess the space before you walk into it:

- **Check for electrical hazards.** If water is standing near outlets, appliances, or your electrical panel, do not enter the room. Turn off the breaker for that area at the main panel, from a dry location, before going in.
- **Look at the ceiling.** A bulging or sagging ceiling means water has pooled above you. Puncture it carefully with a screwdriver in one spot to let it drain in a controlled way rather than collapse.
- **Identify the water category.** Clean water from a supply line is different from water that backed up through a drain or toilet. Gray or black water (sewage, floodwater) carries pathogens and requires protective gear, gloves, eye protection, and ideally an N95 mask, before you touch anything.

Don't assume the damage stops where you can see water. Water moves through wall cavities, under flooring, and into subfloor assemblies fast. A wet spot on the carpet may mean the padding and subfloor beneath it have been saturated for hours.

## Document Everything Before You Touch It

Before you move furniture, pull up carpet, or start mopping, spend ten minutes with your phone camera. This documentation is the foundation of your insurance claim, and it's nearly impossible to recreate after the fact.

1. **Photograph the source**, the broken pipe, the failed appliance, the point of entry.
2. **Walk every affected room** and photograph walls, floors, ceilings, and personal property. Get close-ups of waterlines on walls and flooring.
3. **Open cabinet doors and closets** adjacent to the wet area and photograph inside, water migrates further than it looks.
4. **Note the time and date** in your photos or in a written log. Insurers often ask when the loss was discovered.
5. **Call your insurance company** to open a claim. You don't need to know the full extent of damage yet, you just need a claim number. Ask specifically whether your policy covers additional living expenses if the home becomes uninhabitable.

Keep damaged materials even if they look like trash. Cut-out drywall, ruined flooring, and soaked insulation are evidence. Don't throw anything away until an adjuster or restoration contractor has documented it.

## What You Can Do Right Now (and What You Should Leave Alone)

There's meaningful work you can start immediately, but there's also a short list of things that look helpful and aren't.

**Do:**
- Remove standing water with a wet/dry shop vac if you have one. Every gallon you extract now is less absorption into structural materials.
- Move furniture, rugs, and personal items out of the wet area. Lift furniture legs off wet carpet with aluminum foil or wood blocks to prevent staining.
- Open windows and interior doors to increase airflow, weather permitting.
- Run your home's HVAC system on fan-only mode if the system itself wasn't affected, moving air matters.
- Place towels at doorways to slow water migration into dry rooms.

**Don't:**
- Run a standard box fan pointed at wet drywall without professional guidance. In the wrong conditions, this can push moisture deeper into wall cavities or spread mold spores that haven't yet colonized.
- Use a wet/dry vac on sewage-contaminated water without proper protective equipment and a plan for safe disposal.
- Turn the heat up high thinking it will dry things faster. High heat with trapped humidity creates exactly the conditions mold needs.
- Assume the floor is dry because the surface feels dry. Hardwood and tile can feel dry at the surface while the subfloor beneath holds significant moisture. A moisture meter tells the real story, surface feel does not.
- Pull up hardwood flooring prematurely. Depending on the species and construction, hardwood can sometimes be dried in place. Pulling it up without assessment may destroy salvageable material.

## When to Call a Water Damage Professional

Some water losses are genuinely manageable with a shop vac and fans. A small, contained spill on a concrete floor with no wall contact is one of them. Most residential water losses are not.

Call a water damage restoration professional when:

- The affected area is larger than roughly 10 square feet of flooring, or involves any wall cavity
- The water source was a drain, toilet, or anything that came from outside the building
- The ceiling, insulation, or subfloor is involved
- You can smell a musty or earthy odor, that smell means biological activity has already started
- You have a finished basement, crawlspace, or any area with limited airflow
- Your home was built before 1980 and drywall or flooring may contain asbestos or lead-based paint (disturbing these materials during demo requires specific handling)

Professional restoration crews use thermal imaging cameras and calibrated moisture meters to find water that isn't visible. They set drying equipment, industrial dehumidifiers and air movers, sized to the actual moisture load in the structure, not the room size. The goal is to dry the structure to pre-loss moisture levels, documented with daily readings, so there's no guesswork about whether mold conditions exist.

## The 24-to-72 Hour Window and What Comes After

Mold can begin colonizing wet organic materials, drywall paper, wood framing, carpet backing, within 24 to 48 hours under the right conditions. That window is why the first day matters so much. But "dry" doesn't mean safe until moisture readings confirm it.

After the immediate response, a proper water damage recovery typically involves:

1. **Moisture mapping**, establishing a baseline of where moisture is and how deep it goes
2. **Controlled demolition**, removing materials that cannot be dried in place (saturated drywall, wet insulation, ruined flooring)
3. **Structural drying**, running drying equipment for several days, with daily monitoring
4. **Clearance testing**, confirming moisture levels have returned to acceptable ranges before reconstruction begins
5. **Reconstruction**, replacing what was removed

The timeline varies by the size of the loss and the materials involved. A single bathroom with a failed supply line might dry in three to four days. A loss that reached the subfloor or wall framing takes longer.

If you're in the early hours of a water loss right now, focus on the steps above. Document, stop the source, protect yourself, and get professional eyes on the moisture picture as soon as you can. The decisions made in the first day have a long reach.
