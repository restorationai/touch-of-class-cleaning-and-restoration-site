Hidden mold doesn't announce itself. By the time you see a fuzzy patch on the wall, colonies may have been growing behind it for weeks, spreading through drywall, insulation, and framing while the surface looked fine. The seven signs below don't require a mold test to notice. If two or more apply to your home, treat it as a signal worth investigating, not a coincidence worth ignoring.

## 1. A Musty Smell That Moves Around

Mold produces microbial volatile organic compounds (mVOCs) as it digests organic material. That earthy, basement-like odor, sometimes described as wet cardboard or old soil, is metabolic exhaust. What makes it tricky is that the smell often strengthens in one room and fades in another depending on airflow, humidity, and whether the HVAC system is running.

If the smell is strongest near a wall, under a sink cabinet, or when you first turn on the heat, follow it. The source is rarely where the smell is worst, it's usually where air is escaping from a confined space.

## 2. Discoloration That Isn't Quite a Stain

Paint that's yellowing in a corner, drywall with a gray or greenish tint, or grout lines that keep darkening even after you clean them, these are worth a closer look. Mold colonies range from white to black, and early-stage growth often looks more like a smudge or shadow than the dramatic black spots most people picture.

Run your hand along the discolored area. If the surface feels slightly soft, spongy, or bowed, moisture has been sitting behind it long enough to compromise the material, and long enough for mold to establish itself.

## 3. Warping, Bubbling, or Peeling Surfaces

Wood swells when it absorbs water. Drywall paper delaminates. Paint bubbles when moisture vapor pushes through from behind. These physical changes happen before mold becomes visible, but by the time you're seeing them, the conditions for mold growth, sustained moisture and an organic food source, have already been in place.

In western Washington, where Lakewood homes deal with persistent rain from October through April, exterior walls and below-grade spaces are especially prone to this pattern. A wall that looks fine in summer may show these signs by February.

## 4. Allergy Symptoms That Only Happen at Home

Mold spores are small enough to stay airborne for hours. When someone in the household starts experiencing a runny nose, itchy eyes, or a cough that clears up when they leave for work, and returns when they come home, the building environment is worth examining. This pattern is particularly common in bedrooms, where people spend eight hours breathing recirculated air.

This is not medical advice, and symptoms alone don't confirm mold. But the pattern is a useful data point when you're trying to decide whether to investigate further.

## 5. A Past Leak That Was "Dried Out" Quickly

This is one of the most underappreciated risk factors. A burst pipe, an overflowing washer, or a roof leak that was mopped up and forgotten can still result in hidden mold if the structural materials behind the surface weren't dried to the right moisture content. Drywall can feel dry to the touch while the wood framing behind it holds enough moisture to sustain mold growth for months.

The IICRC S520 standard, the industry benchmark for mold remediation, distinguishes between surface dryness and structural dryness for exactly this reason. If your past water event wasn't verified with moisture meters at multiple depths, the cleanup may have stopped too soon.

## 6. Rust or Mineral Staining on Pipes and Fixtures

Corroded pipe connections, rust rings around supply lines under sinks, or white mineral deposits on a water heater are signs of slow, chronic moisture, the kind that doesn't cause a dramatic flood but quietly feeds mold in the cabinet below or the wall behind. These fixtures often sit in enclosed spaces with limited airflow, which makes them ideal mold environments.

Check under every sink in the house. Pull out the cabinet liner and look at the base of the cabinet box. A soft floor, a dark ring, or any soft spot in the wood is a red flag.

## 7. HVAC Registers That Smell Musty When the System Runs

If the musty smell appears specifically when the air handler kicks on, mold may be growing on or near the evaporator coil, in the drain pan, or inside the ductwork itself. HVAC systems are efficient at distributing spores throughout a home, a small colony near the air handler can affect air quality in every room.

This is one situation where a visual check isn't enough. The evaporator coil and drain pan are typically behind an access panel and require someone who knows what they're looking at.

## What To Do If You Recognize These Signs

**Don't start pulling up carpet or cutting into walls yourself.** Disturbing mold without containment releases spores into the air and can spread contamination to areas that were previously clean. This is the most common mistake homeowners make, the impulse to investigate makes the problem harder to remediate.

Instead, take these steps:

1. **Reduce humidity in the affected area.** Run a dehumidifier if you have one. Keep windows closed if outdoor humidity is high, in the South Sound, that's most of the year.
2. **Document what you're seeing.** Photograph the discoloration, the smell location, any warping or staining. This matters for insurance conversations later.
3. **Stop any active water source.** If you can identify a dripping pipe, a slow roof leak, or a condensation problem, address it before mold testing or remediation. Mold will return if the moisture source stays.
4. **Call a professional for assessment before remediation.** A certified mold inspector or remediation contractor can use moisture meters, thermal imaging, and air sampling to locate the source without opening up walls unnecessarily.

## When to Call a Professional

If you're seeing two or more of the signs above, especially if there's a history of water intrusion in the home, a professional assessment is the right next step. Mold remediation isn't a DIY project once it's inside wall cavities, under flooring, or in an HVAC system. The scope of work, containment requirements, and post-remediation verification are all specific to the contamination level and material type.
