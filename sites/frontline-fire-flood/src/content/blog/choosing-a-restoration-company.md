Choosing a restoration company after a loss is one of the most consequential decisions you'll make as a property owner, and it usually happens when you're already stressed, wet, or watching smoke damage darken your ceiling. The short answer: vet the company *before* you sign anything. Check that they carry the right certifications and insurance, confirm they'll document the damage in a format your adjuster will accept, and make sure the scope of work is in writing before a single piece of drywall comes down. Everything below expands on exactly how to do that.

## Why the Restoration Industry Attracts Bad Actors, and How to Spot Them

Restoration work is largely invisible. Drying equipment runs inside walls, antimicrobial treatments go on surfaces that get covered up, and most homeowners have no frame of reference for what a legitimate job should cost. That information gap is exactly what predatory contractors exploit.

After a significant storm or a regional flooding event, storm-chasers, contractors who follow disaster declarations from state to state, move through affected neighborhoods offering fast starts and low prices. Some are legitimate. Many are not. The pattern is usually the same: a verbal estimate, a quick start, and then a final invoice that bears little resemblance to what was discussed.

Lakewood and the surrounding Pierce County area see this after heavy rain events, when the clay-heavy soils in parts of the region can't absorb runoff fast enough and water finds its way into crawl spaces and finished basements. The urgency of standing water is real, mold can begin colonizing wet organic material within 24 to 48 hours, but that urgency should not push you into signing an assignment of benefits or a work authorization before you understand what you're agreeing to.

**Red flags to watch for:**

- A contractor who asks you to sign over your insurance claim before providing a written scope of work
- No verifiable local address or business license
- Pressure to start immediately without a written estimate
- Reluctance to work with your insurance adjuster or to itemize costs
- No mention of drying standards, moisture documentation, or clearance testing

## What to Look for in a Legitimate Restoration Contractor

The restoration industry has a credentialing body, the Institute of Inspection, Cleaning and Restoration Certification (IICRC), that sets the technical standards most insurance carriers expect contractors to follow. The S500 standard governs water damage drying; the S520 governs mold remediation. When a company says they follow IICRC standards, that's a meaningful statement. When they can't tell you which standard applies to your loss, that's a problem.

Beyond certifications, look for:

**Proof of insurance.** Ask for a certificate of liability insurance and a workers' compensation certificate. A crew working in your home without workers' comp exposes you to liability if someone is injured on your property.

**A written scope of work.** Before any demolition begins, you should have a line-item document describing what will be removed, what equipment will be used, how long the drying phase is expected to take, and what clearance criteria will be used to confirm the job is complete.

**Moisture documentation.** A legitimate water damage restoration job produces a drying log, daily moisture readings taken at multiple points, showing the affected materials moving from wet to dry over the course of the drying phase. If a contractor can't show you what that documentation looks like, they may not be producing it, which creates problems when your carrier reviews the claim.

**Direct insurance billing experience.** Most major carriers have preferred protocols for how losses are documented and billed. A contractor who regularly works with adjusters knows how to submit a Xactimate estimate, how to photograph damage in a way that supports the claim, and how to handle supplements when hidden damage is discovered mid-job.

## Questions to Ask Before You Hire Anyone

Treat this like any other significant contractor decision. You're allowed to ask questions before you sign.

1. **Are you IICRC certified?** Ask which certifications the company holds and which technicians on the crew are certified, company-level and technician-level credentials are different things.
2. **Can I see a sample scope of work or drying report?** A company that produces these routinely will have no problem showing you a redacted example.
3. **How do you handle hidden damage?** Water behind plaster can read dry at the surface while the lath beneath stays saturated. Fire damage in a structure with a forced-air system can leave smoke residue in ductwork that isn't visible during the initial walkthrough. Ask how the company handles discoveries that expand the scope.
4. **Will you communicate directly with my adjuster?** You want a yes, and you want to understand what that process looks like.
5. **What does the closeout process look like?** For mold jobs especially, ask whether a post-remediation verification (clearance test) is included or whether that's a separate cost.

## What NOT to Do in the First 24 Hours

The decisions made in the first day after a loss can complicate the restoration and the insurance claim.

- **Don't throw away damaged materials before they're documented.** Your adjuster needs to see the damage. Photograph everything before moving it, and ideally leave structural materials in place until the adjuster or contractor has completed their inspection.
- **Don't run fans and assume the problem is handled.** Household box fans move air but don't dehumidify. In a Lakewood winter, when outdoor humidity is already high, running fans without dehumidification can actually slow drying and encourage mold growth. Professional drying equipment, refrigerant or desiccant dehumidifiers and air movers calibrated to the affected area, is a different tool entirely.
- **Don't sign an assignment of benefits without reading it.** An AOB transfers your right to negotiate the claim directly to the contractor. That can work in your favor with a trustworthy company, but it removes your leverage if a dispute arises.
- **Don't assume the visible damage is the full extent of it.** A water stain on a ceiling may represent a slow leak that's been saturating the subfloor above it for weeks. A smoke smell in one room after a kitchen fire can mean smoke residue has traveled through the HVAC system into rooms that look clean.

## The Recovery Timeline: What to Expect After You Hire

Understanding the typical phases of a restoration job helps you hold a contractor accountable and manage your own expectations.

**Emergency services (Day 1):** Water extraction, board-up or tarping, initial moisture mapping, and containment setup for mold jobs. This phase stops the loss from getting worse.

**Drying and demolition (Days 1–5, depending on category and class of water damage):** Affected materials that can't be dried in place, saturated drywall, soaked insulation, warped flooring, are removed. Drying equipment runs continuously. Moisture readings are logged daily.

**Clearance and documentation (End of drying phase):** Moisture readings confirm that affected materials have reached dry standard. On mold jobs, a third-party industrial hygienist typically performs air sampling to confirm the remediation was effective. This report goes to your carrier.

**Reconstruction:** Once the structure is dry and cleared, rebuild can begin. Some restoration companies handle both mitigation and reconstruction under one contract; others subcontract the rebuild. Know which model you're working with before the job starts.
