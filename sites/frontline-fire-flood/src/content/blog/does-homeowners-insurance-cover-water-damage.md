The short answer: it depends on *how* the water got in. Homeowners insurance typically covers sudden, accidental water damage, a pipe that bursts overnight, a washing machine hose that fails, a dishwasher that overflows. It almost never covers damage that built up slowly over time, water that entered from the ground, or flooding from a river or heavy rain. Understanding which side of that line your situation falls on is the difference between a covered claim and a bill you pay entirely out of pocket.

## The Core Distinction: Sudden vs. Gradual

Insurance carriers draw a hard line between two categories.

**Sudden and accidental** losses are generally covered under a standard HO-3 policy:

- A supply line behind your refrigerator or washing machine ruptures without warning
- A pipe freezes and bursts during a cold snap
- A toilet supply valve fails and floods the bathroom
- A neighbor's unit above yours leaks through your ceiling (though their policy may be primary)

**Gradual damage** is almost always excluded. If a slow drip under your kitchen sink has been rotting the cabinet floor for six months, insurers treat that as a maintenance failure, something you could have caught and fixed. The same logic applies to a roof that's been leaking around a flashing for two seasons. Adjusters are trained to look for staining patterns, mold growth, and wood deterioration that indicate a problem existed long before the claim date.

The practical takeaway: the moment you notice water where it shouldn't be, document it and act on it. A leak you knew about and didn't fix is a much harder claim than one that genuinely caught you off guard.

## What Standard Policies Don't Cover (and What Does)

Two exclusions trip up homeowners more than any others.

**Flood damage**, meaning water that enters your home from the outside, whether from a storm surge, an overflowing creek, or surface runoff after heavy rain, is excluded from every standard homeowners policy. In the South Sound, where Lakewood sits in a region that sees significant winter rainfall and has low-lying areas near American Lake and Clover Creek, this distinction matters. Flood coverage requires a separate policy through the National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) or a private flood carrier. If you're in a FEMA-designated flood zone, your mortgage lender likely required you to carry it. If you're not sure, your agent can pull your property's flood map designation.

**Sewer and drain backup** is also excluded from most base policies. If the municipal sewer line surcharges during a heavy rain event and raw sewage backs up through your floor drain, that's not covered unless you added a sewer backup endorsement, a relatively inexpensive rider that many homeowners don't know to ask for.

Other common exclusions:

- Water seeping through foundation walls or a basement floor (hydrostatic pressure)
- Damage from a sump pump that failed due to a power outage (unless you have equipment breakdown coverage)
- Mold remediation costs beyond a low sublimit, which varies by policy

## How to Read Your Policy Before You Need It

Most people open their declarations page for the first time while standing in a wet room. A few things to look for before that happens:

1. **Find your water damage exclusions section.** It's usually labeled "Perils Insured Against" or "Exclusions" in the policy form. Look for language about "seepage," "leakage," "continuous or repeated," and "flood."
2. **Check your Coverage A limit.** This is your dwelling coverage, the amount that pays to repair or rebuild structure. Make sure it reflects current construction costs, not what you paid for the house.
3. **Look for sublimits on mold.** Some policies cap mold remediation at $5,000 or $10,000 even if the underlying water damage is covered. In the Pacific Northwest's damp climate, mold can establish in as little as 24–48 hours after a water event, so that sublimit can matter quickly.
4. **Note your deductible.** For smaller losses, a supply line leak that damaged a section of flooring, the repair cost may be close to or below your deductible. Filing a claim you're unlikely to collect on can still affect your renewal rate.

If you're uncertain how your policy reads, call your agent and ask them to walk you through a hypothetical: "If a pipe bursts in my wall and damages the drywall and flooring, what's covered and what's my out-of-pocket?"

## What to Do the Moment You Discover Water Damage

How you respond in the first hour affects both the physical outcome and your claim.

1. **Stop the source.** Shut off the supply valve closest to the leak. If you can't find it or it won't close, turn off the main shutoff, in most Lakewood homes it's near the water meter at the street or at the point where the main enters the house.
2. **Document everything before you touch it.** Walk through and photograph standing water, wet materials, and the apparent source. Video is better than photos alone. Insurers want to see the loss as it was found.
3. **Extract standing water if you can do it safely.** A wet/dry vacuum or towels for small amounts. The longer water sits on wood subfloor or against drywall, the deeper it wicks.
4. **Call your insurance company.** Most carriers have 24-hour claims lines. Report the loss promptly, some policies have language about "timely notice," and delay can complicate a claim.
5. **Do not tear out materials yet.** Your adjuster or an independent inspector may need to see affected areas in place. Take photos, but hold off on demolition unless the situation is unsafe.

One thing worth knowing: insurers generally cover "reasonable and necessary" emergency mitigation costs, water extraction, drying equipment, temporary tarps, even before a formal claim decision. Keep receipts for everything.

## When the Damage Is Behind the Walls

This is where water damage gets expensive and complicated. A pipe that leaks inside a wall cavity can read completely dry at the surface while the framing, insulation, and back of the drywall stay saturated. Standard moisture meters measure surface readings; a professional uses thermal imaging and penetrating probes to find what's actually wet.

If you see a stain on drywall or ceiling material but the source isn't obvious, resist the urge to cut a small inspection hole and call it done. Water travels along framing members and pooled insulation, the wet area is often several feet from the stain. A proper moisture map tells you how far the drying equipment needs to reach and gives your adjuster documentation of the actual scope.

Mold is the clock you're racing. In the Pacific Northwest's ambient humidity, mold colonization on wet organic material can begin within 24 to 48 hours. Once it establishes, remediation becomes a separate scope of work, and as noted above, your policy's mold sublimit may be lower than you expect.

## Closing: When to Bring in a Professional

For a small, contained spill on a tile floor, you may be able to handle cleanup yourself. But if water has touched drywall, insulation, wood framing, or subfloor, or if you can't confirm the source is fully dry, the cost of getting it wrong is higher than the cost of getting it checked. A restoration professional can run moisture readings, map the affected area, and give you documentation that supports your insurance claim.
