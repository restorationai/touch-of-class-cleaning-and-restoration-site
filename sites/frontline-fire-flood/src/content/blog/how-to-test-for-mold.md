Testing for mold starts with your nose and your eyes, not a kit. If you smell something musty in a room that's been damp, or you see dark staining along a baseboard or ceiling seam, there's a reasonable chance mold is already growing. The real question isn't whether to test, it's *how* to test in a way that actually tells you something useful. DIY mold test kits are widely available and cheap, but their results are often misleading. A professional inspection costs more and takes longer to schedule, but it gives you information you can actually act on. Here's how to think through both options.

## What DIY Mold Test Kits Actually Tell You

The most common DIY kits, the kind you find at hardware stores or order online, fall into two categories: air sampling kits and surface swab kits.

**Air sampling kits** typically ask you to leave a petri dish open in a room for a set period (often 48–96 hours), then mail it to a lab. The lab reports back which mold species grew in the dish.

**Surface swab kits** let you wipe a suspicious area and mail the swab for analysis.

Both approaches have a fundamental problem: mold spores are everywhere. They float through every home, every day, through open windows, on clothing, on pets. A petri dish left in any room will almost always grow *something*. That result doesn't tell you whether you have a mold problem, it tells you that air exists in your home.

What a DIY kit cannot tell you:

- Whether the spore count in your home is elevated above normal outdoor baseline levels
- Where the mold colony is actually located (surface staining and airborne spores can be far apart)
- Whether the source moisture problem is ongoing or resolved
- Whether the species present poses a health concern at the concentrations found

The labs that process these kits are often accredited, and the species identification can be accurate. The limitation isn't the lab, it's that the sampling method doesn't give the lab enough context to interpret the result meaningfully.

**When a DIY kit might still be worth it:** If you're trying to document *something* for a landlord conversation or an insurance question and you need any paper trail at all, a kit gives you a dated lab result. It's a low bar, but it's not nothing.

## What a Professional Mold Inspection Includes

A certified mold inspector approaches the problem differently. Rather than sampling first and asking questions later, they start with a physical assessment of the building, looking for the moisture conditions that allow mold to colonize in the first place.

A thorough inspection typically includes:

1. **Moisture mapping** using a pin-type or non-invasive moisture meter to read wall cavities, subfloor assemblies, and ceiling materials without opening them up
2. **Thermal imaging** (infrared camera) to identify cool, damp zones behind finished surfaces where mold commonly hides, behind plaster, inside wall assemblies, under flooring
3. **Visual assessment** of crawl spaces, attic sheathing, HVAC components, and other areas DIY kits never reach
4. **Air and surface sampling** performed with calibrated equipment, compared against outdoor baseline samples taken the same day, which is the only way to know whether indoor spore counts are actually elevated
5. **A written report** identifying affected areas, likely moisture sources, and a recommended scope of work

In Western Washington, older housing stock, including a significant portion of Lakewood's mid-century homes, often has crawl spaces with vapor barriers that have shifted, deteriorated, or were never installed correctly. Ground moisture in the Puget Sound region is persistent enough that a crawl space problem can feed mold into a home's living areas for months before a homeowner notices anything at the surface. An inspector with a moisture meter and a thermal camera will find that; a petri dish on your kitchen counter will not.

## Signs You Should Skip the Kit and Call a Professional Directly

Some situations don't warrant the DIY-vs-kit debate. Go straight to a professional inspection if:

- You can see visible mold growth covering more than a few square feet
- The smell is strong and persistent, not just occasional
- Someone in the home has respiratory symptoms (coughing, wheezing, sinus congestion) that improve when they leave and return when they come back
- You've had a water loss, a burst pipe, a roof leak, flooding, that wasn't dried out within 24–48 hours
- You're buying or selling a home and need documentation
- Your HVAC system has been running through a space you suspect is affected (air handlers can distribute spores through an entire building)

Mold begins colonizing porous materials, drywall paper, wood framing, insulation facing, within 24 to 72 hours of sustained moisture exposure. By the time you smell it, the colony is usually well established. A DIY kit at that point delays the professional assessment you were going to need anyway.

## What NOT To Do While You're Deciding

- **Don't run fans or your HVAC system** in or near a suspected mold area before an inspection. Airflow spreads spores to unaffected parts of the building.
- **Don't bleach it and assume it's handled.** Bleach kills surface mold on non-porous materials like tile, but it doesn't penetrate drywall or wood. The colony below the surface survives, and the moisture that fed it is still there.
- **Don't open walls yourself** to investigate. Disturbing a mold colony without containment releases a concentrated burst of spores into the living space.
- **Don't ignore a musty smell in a closet or under a sink** just because you can't see anything. Mold grows in dark, still spaces first, what you can see is often the last place it appears.

## The Longer Picture: Testing Is Step One, Not the Whole Answer

A positive mold test, whether from a kit or a professional, is the beginning of a process, not the end of one. The test tells you mold is present. It doesn't fix the moisture source that allowed it to grow, and it doesn't remove the affected material.

Effective mold remediation addresses both: the colony itself (through containment, removal of affected materials, and treatment of remaining surfaces) and the underlying moisture problem (the leak, the vapor intrusion, the condensation point). Remediating without fixing the moisture source is like painting over rust, it looks resolved until it isn't.

If a professional inspection confirms active mold growth, the next step is a remediation scope of work, a documented plan for what gets removed, what gets treated, and how the work area gets contained to protect the rest of the home during the process.
