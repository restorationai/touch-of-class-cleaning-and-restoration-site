A burst pipe or kitchen fire doesn't end when the water is extracted or the soot is wiped down. What's left behind is often a house with open wall cavities, missing subfloor, exposed framing, and a punch list that the mitigation crew was never scoped to touch. That gap between "the damage is stopped" and "the house is whole again" is where reconstruction services come in, and it's where a lot of homeowners get stuck waiting on contractors who don't talk to their insurance adjuster or their original restoration crew.

## What reconstruction services actually involve

Post-damage reconstruction covers everything required to rebuild a structure back to pre-loss condition after water, fire, or storm damage has been mitigated. That can mean replacing drywall and insulation after a Category 2 water loss, reframing a section of wall after fire damage took out studs, rebuilding a bathroom subfloor after a slow leak rotted the joists, or a full room rebuild after a roof failure let weather into the structure for days. The scope ranges from cosmetic (paint, trim, flooring) to structural (framing, load-bearing repairs, code-driven upgrades), and the right scope depends on what the demolition phase actually exposed, not what was visible before the work started.

Timelines vary widely. A single-room drywall and flooring rebuild might run one to two weeks. A multi-room reconstruction involving framing, electrical, and permitting can run six to ten weeks depending on inspection scheduling and material lead times. Reconstruction work in Lakewood often follows mitigation work directly, which is why continuity between the two phases matters: the crew doing the rebuild should know exactly what was removed, why, and what moisture or structural testing cleared the space for closing walls back up.

## Our process

1. **Scope of work.** We document existing damage, confirm what mitigation already removed, and write a detailed scope covering materials, labor, and code requirements before any rebuild work starts.
2. **Permits.** Structural work, electrical, and plumbing typically require permits through the local building department. We pull what's required and schedule inspections around the rebuild timeline rather than treating them as an afterthought.
3. **Framing and rough-in.** Studs, subfloor, and any load-bearing repairs go in first, followed by rough electrical, plumbing, or HVAC work that was displaced during demolition.
4. **Insulation and drywall.** Once rough inspections pass, we insulate, hang and finish drywall, and prep surfaces for final finishes.
5. **Finishing.** Flooring, trim, paint, fixtures, and final touch-ups bring the space back to a livable, move-in condition, followed by a final walkthrough against the original scope.

## What separates a good reconstruction response from a bad one

The most common failure point is a reconstruction crew that never saw the original mitigation report. If the drying logs, moisture readings, or demolition photos aren't reviewed before framing goes back in, hidden problems get sealed behind new drywall instead of fixed. Adjusters look for exactly this continuity: documented moisture levels before closing walls, photos of framing before insulation, and a scope that matches what was actually removed, not a generic estimate.

Another common issue is under-scoping to win the job cheap, then submitting change orders mid-project once hidden damage surfaces. A thorough scope of work accounts for the likelihood of additional findings once walls are open, and a licensed contractor should be able to explain code-driven requirements (like GFCI updates or insulation R-values) that weren't part of the original structure but are now required for permit sign-off.

## What does reconstruction cost?

Reconstruction costs depend heavily on scope: whether the work is cosmetic or structural, how many trades are involved, and whether permits and inspections are required. Every loss is different, which is why we provide a written scope of work before any rebuild begins rather than a ballpark number. Homeowners insurance typically covers reconstruction that restores a covered loss back to pre-loss condition, though code-upgrade costs and betterment items are sometimes handled separately depending on the policy.

| Scenario | Typical range |
|---|---|
| Single room, drywall and paint only | $2,500 - $6,000 |
| Bathroom rebuild after water damage (subfloor, drywall, fixtures) | $8,000 - $18,000 |
| Kitchen reconstruction after fire or water loss | $15,000 - $40,000 |
| Structural framing repair (wall or floor section) | $5,000 - $15,000 |
| Multi-room whole-floor reconstruction | $30,000 - $80,000+ |

## Seasonal & regional considerations

In the South Puget Sound area, reconstruction schedules often compete with the regional wet season between fall and early spring, when exterior work (roofing, siding, exterior framing) slows due to rain and limited dry windows. Permitting timelines through local building departments can also add weeks to a project, so starting the permit process early in the scope phase helps keep a reconstruction on schedule rather than idling after demolition is already done.
