After a fire, flood, or sewage backup, the structure gets most of the attention, but it's often the contents that carry the most financial and emotional weight. Furniture soaked in Category 3 water. Photo albums coated in smoke residue. Electronics that look fine on the outside but have soot packed into every vent. Contents restoration is a separate discipline from structural drying or smoke mitigation, and when it gets folded into the general cleanup rather than treated as its own process, items that could have been saved get thrown away, and items that look clean still smell like smoke six months later.

## What contents restoration & storage actually involves

Contents restoration begins with a decision that most homeowners aren't equipped to make alone: what can be restored, what needs professional cleaning, and what is a total loss. That triage matters, both for what ends up back in your home and for what your insurance carrier will pay.

Salvageable items go through one or more cleaning methods depending on the type of contamination. Ultrasonic cleaning uses high-frequency sound waves in a water-and-solution bath to remove soot, smoke residue, and microbial contamination from hard surfaces, electronics housings, china, hardware, tools, without abrasion. Ozone chambers and hydroxyl generators neutralize odor molecules embedded in soft goods, books, and documents that can't be submerged. Dry-cleaning and wet-cleaning methods are applied to textiles based on fabric type and the chemistry of the contaminant.

Storage is the other half of this service. When a home is uninhabitable during structural repairs, contents need to go somewhere climate-controlled, inventoried, and secured. Items packed out without a proper inventory, box by box, photographed and logged, create disputes at claim time and make it nearly impossible to confirm what came back versus what was lost in transit.

Timeline varies by volume and contamination type. A single-room pack-out and cleaning can turn around in days. A whole-house loss with smoke-saturated soft goods and water-damaged furniture may take several weeks, particularly if specialty items like antiques, artwork, or electronics require vendor-specific restoration.

## Our process

1. **On-site triage and documentation.** Before anything is moved, every affected room is photographed and every item is logged, condition noted, category assigned (restorable, total loss, or undetermined). This documentation is the foundation of the insurance claim and protects you if anything is disputed later.

2. **Pack-out and chain-of-custody inventory.** Items are packed systematically, labeled by room and box number, and inventoried on a contents list that matches the photos. Fragile and high-value items are flagged and packed separately. You receive a copy of the full inventory before the truck leaves.

3. **Cleaning and decontamination.** Items go through the appropriate cleaning method for their material and contamination type, ultrasonic tanks for hard goods, ozone or hydroxyl treatment for odor, dry or wet textile cleaning for fabric. Porous items with Category 3 (sewage or floodwater) contamination that cannot be fully decontaminated are documented as non-restorable rather than cleaned and returned.

4. **Climate-controlled storage.** Cleaned and in-process items are stored in a secured, climate-controlled facility. Temperature and humidity are maintained to prevent secondary mold growth on items that were wet, a real risk if contents are warehoused in an uncontrolled environment while repairs drag on.

5. **Delivery and placement.** When the structure is ready, contents are returned, unpacked, and placed per your direction. A final walkthrough confirms every inventoried item is accounted for.

## What separates a good contents response from a bad one

The most common failure in contents restoration is skipping the pre-pack inventory. Without it, there is no baseline, no way to prove what condition items were in before the loss, and no way to confirm everything came back. Insurance adjusters increasingly require photo documentation at the item level, not just a handwritten list.

The second common failure is applying the wrong cleaning method. Protein smoke residue from kitchen fires is oily and nearly invisible on surfaces, it responds poorly to dry-sponge wiping and requires chemical sponges and degreasers. Synthetic smoke from burning plastics or electronics leaves a different residue with different pH chemistry. Treating both the same way produces inconsistent results and often leaves odor behind even when surfaces look clean.

A third issue is inadequate odor treatment. Masking sprays cover smoke odor temporarily. Hydroxyl and ozone treatment, run for sufficient dwell time and at the right concentration, break down the odor molecules. Cutting that process short, because a homeowner wants items back quickly, or because a crew is moving to the next job, is the most common reason smoke smell returns weeks after restoration.

Finally, total-loss documentation matters as much as restoration. Items that cannot be restored need to be photographed, described, and submitted with replacement cost values. Gaps in that documentation leave money on the table at claim time.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

In the South Puget Sound region, the wet season runs roughly October through April, and that extended damp period affects contents as much as structure. Items stored in garages, basements, or outbuildings during that window, even items that were dried after a water loss, are at elevated risk for secondary mold growth if storage isn't climate-controlled. Lakewood's older housing stock, much of it built in the mid-20th century, often includes uninsulated crawl spaces and single-pane windows that allow condensation to migrate into adjacent rooms, meaning contents near exterior walls can absorb ambient moisture even without a direct water event.
