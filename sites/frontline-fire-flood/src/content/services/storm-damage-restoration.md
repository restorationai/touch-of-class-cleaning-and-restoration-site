A storm doesn't finish its damage when the wind stops. The branch that punched through your roof at 2 a.m. left an opening that soaked your attic insulation, your ceiling drywall, and the wall cavity below it, all before morning. By the time you're standing in your living room watching the ceiling bubble, secondary water damage is already underway. Storm damage restoration is the work of stopping that chain reaction: sealing the structure, extracting what's wet, drying what can be saved, and rebuilding what can't.

## What storm damage restoration actually involves

Storm losses are almost always multi-system events. A single severe weather incident can combine structural breach (roof, windows, siding), wind-driven rain intrusion, flooding from overwhelmed drainage, and debris impact, sometimes all at once. That's different from a burst pipe or an appliance leak, where the source is contained and the affected area is predictable.

On a storm job, the first task is understanding the full extent of the loss before any restoration work begins. That means getting eyes on the roof, the attic, the wall assemblies, and the crawl space or basement, not just the rooms that look wet from the inside. Wind-driven rain travels horizontally and finds gaps that gravity-fed water never would. Moisture meters, thermal imaging cameras, and pin probes are used to map saturation in materials that read dry at the surface.

Equipment on a storm job typically includes industrial air movers, low-grain refrigerant (LGR) dehumidifiers, and sometimes desiccant dehumidifiers for large structural cavities or cold-weather drying. Tarping and board-up materials are staged immediately to stop ongoing intrusion. If tree debris is involved, coordinating removal before interior work begins is essential, you can't dry a room that still has a gap in the roof.

Timeline depends on the extent of structural breach and how much water entered before the opening was sealed. Drying a saturated wall assembly typically takes three to five days of continuous equipment operation, with daily moisture readings to confirm progress.

## Our process

1. **Emergency stabilization**, The structure gets secured first. Tarps over roof penetrations, plywood over broken windows and doors, and debris cleared from entry points. Nothing else happens until the building envelope is closed and weather can't add to the loss.

2. **Damage mapping and documentation**, Every affected surface is photographed, measured, and logged before anything is moved or removed. Moisture readings are taken at multiple depths in walls, floors, and ceilings. This documentation is the foundation of your insurance claim, adjusters work from it, and gaps in documentation are the most common reason supplements get delayed.

3. **Water extraction and debris removal**, Standing water is extracted using truck-mounted or portable extraction units. Saturated insulation, ceiling tile, and non-salvageable materials are removed to expose the structural assembly for drying. Storm debris is bagged and removed from the interior.

4. **Structural drying**, Air movers and dehumidifiers are positioned according to a drying plan calibrated to the affected square footage and material types. Drying logs are recorded daily. Drying is complete when moisture readings return to baseline for the material type and region, not when things feel dry to the touch.

5. **Reconstruction scope and repair**, Once the structure is dry and the loss is fully documented, reconstruction begins: framing, sheathing, roofing, drywall, insulation, and finish work. Because Frontline Fire & Flood handles both mitigation and reconstruction, there's no handoff gap where things fall through.

## What separates a good storm damage response from a bad one

The most common mistake in storm damage work is treating it like a simple water loss. A contractor who extracts visible water and sets a few fans has addressed maybe half the problem. Wind-driven rain soaks wall cavities from the top down, saturates insulation that holds moisture for weeks, and wicks into floor assemblies that show no surface signs. If those assemblies aren't mapped with moisture meters and dried to documented baseline readings, mold colonization typically begins within 24 to 48 hours in a warm, humid environment.

A second common failure is incomplete documentation at the time of loss. Insurance adjusters, and increasingly, AI-assisted claim review systems, look for moisture readings tied to specific locations, photographs of materials before removal, and a written scope that matches the documented damage. Adjusters routinely reduce or deny supplements when early documentation is missing or inconsistent. A restoration crew that photographs everything, logs readings by room and material, and writes a scope that matches the photos is worth significantly more to you than one that moves fast and documents later.

For tree damage specifically, the structural impact zone is often larger than it appears. A large limb on a roof can transfer load to rafters and ridge members well beyond the visible breach point. Structural assessment before reconstruction, not just patching the visible hole, is the standard that protects you and satisfies most carrier requirements.

Typical costs for storm damage restoration vary widely based on how much of the structure was breached, how long water had to migrate before the opening was sealed, and whether reconstruction is needed. ## Seasonal and regional considerations

The Pacific Northwest's storm season runs roughly October through April, with the most severe wind events typically arriving in November and December. Western Washington's combination of saturated soils, mature tree canopy, and aging housing stock creates specific risk patterns: older homes in Lakewood, Tacoma, and the surrounding South Sound area often have cedar shake or aging composition roofing that fails under the sustained wind loads of a Puget Sound windstorm before the structure itself is compromised. Attic ventilation gaps and aging caulking around dormers and skylights are common secondary entry points that only become visible after a storm event.

The region's mild, wet winters also mean that drying conditions indoors are naturally poor from November through March. Ambient humidity in an unheated or partially heated structure can stall drying significantly, which is why equipment sizing and drying plan calibration matter more here than in drier climates.
