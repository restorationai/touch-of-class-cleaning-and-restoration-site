**Large loss and catastrophic response** is what happens when a single-room water job turns into a five-story apartment complex, when a warehouse fire takes out 40,000 square feet of inventory and structure, or when a storm event damages a dozen commercial properties in the same afternoon. These losses do not follow the timeline or staffing model of a typical residential claim. They require simultaneous crews, a general contractor's license, direct coordination with large loss adjusters, and someone on-site who can run a multi-phase project without losing control of documentation. Frontline Fire & Flood is based in Lakewood and answers 24/7 for exactly this kind of job.

## What large loss and catastrophic response actually involves

A large loss is defined less by the cause of damage and more by its scale and complexity: multi-unit residential buildings, commercial and industrial properties, hospitals, schools, government facilities, or any structure where losses are projected to run into six or seven figures. These jobs typically involve multiple simultaneous work streams, structural drying across tens of thousands of square feet, extensive demolition and reconstruction, and a claims file that several adjusters and a loss consultant will scrutinize line by line. Equipment needs scale accordingly: desiccant dehumidification for large open floor plates, negative air machines for contained demolition zones, and industrial extraction units running around the clock instead of a single truck-mount unit for a flooded basement. Timeline is measured in weeks and months, not days, and the mitigation phase alone can involve dozens of pieces of equipment logged and monitored daily.

## Our process

1. **Rapid mobilization and triage.** On notification, crews and equipment are dispatched to assess the full scope of the loss, prioritizing life safety, structural stability, and preventing secondary damage from spreading to unaffected areas.
2. **Multi-site scoping and documentation.** Every affected area is measured, photographed, and moisture-mapped before any demolition begins, building the documentation package adjusters and loss consultants will need to validate the claim.
3. **Phased mitigation across the structure.** Drying, extraction, and contents stabilization run in parallel across multiple zones rather than sequentially, which is the difference between a four-week dry-out and a four-month one on a large structure.
4. **Contents and specialty handling.** Inventory, electronics, documents, and equipment are assessed, packed out, and either restored or scheduled for replacement documentation depending on condition and client priorities.
5. **Reconstruction handoff and project close-out.** Once the structure is dry and demolition is complete, reconstruction scope is finalized with permits pulled as required, and the project moves to rebuild under continuous project management rather than a new vendor relationship.

## What separates a good large loss response from a bad one

The most common failure on catastrophic jobs is fragmentation: multiple vendors handling different phases with no single party tracking total scope, which leads to gaps in drying logs, missed secondary damage, and a claims file that does not hold together under adjuster review. A capable large loss team keeps one point of contact and one documentation system from mitigation through reconstruction. Adjusters on these files are looking for daily moisture logs, equipment placement records, psychrometric readings, and clear photo documentation tied to dates, not estimates reconstructed after the fact. Less experienced operators also underestimate drying timelines on dense commercial assemblies like concrete and masonry, pulling equipment too early and allowing moisture to resurface weeks later as secondary mold growth, which turns a contained loss into a second claim.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Western Washington's wet season runs roughly October through March, and that is when catastrophic losses tend to cluster: wind and atmospheric river storms that overwhelm aging commercial roofing, and freeze events that split sprinkler lines and domestic water piping in large multi-unit buildings built before current code. Pierce County's older commercial stock, much of it built mid-century with limited roof drainage capacity, is particularly exposed to cascading water damage once a single roof section fails. Summer brings a smaller but real wildfire smoke exposure risk for large commercial HVAC systems, which can carry soot and ash deep into ductwork across an entire building footprint.
