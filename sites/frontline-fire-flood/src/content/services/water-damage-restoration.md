Water you can see is only part of the problem. Within the first hour after a pipe bursts, a supply line fails, or a washing machine hose lets go, water is already moving, wicking into drywall, traveling under flooring, pooling inside wall cavities where no one will think to look. By 24 to 48 hours, wet cellulose materials are primed for mold colonization. Water damage restoration is the work of stopping that clock: extracting standing water, pulling moisture out of structural materials before it causes secondary damage, and verifying, with instruments, not guesswork, that the building is actually dry.

## What Water Damage Restoration actually involves

Restoration starts the moment the water stops flowing and doesn't end until a calibrated moisture meter confirms that affected materials have returned to normal dry standard. Between those two points, the work is technical and equipment-intensive.

Extraction comes first. Truck-mounted and portable extractors pull standing water from carpet, pad, and hard flooring at a rate that mopping or shop-vac work simply cannot match. Submersible pumps handle any significant depth in basements or crawlspaces.

Once visible water is gone, the harder work begins. Structural drying uses high-velocity air movers and commercial-grade desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers to drive evaporation out of framing, subfloor, drywall, and concrete, materials that hold moisture long after the surface feels dry to the touch. Thermal imaging cameras and penetrating moisture meters map the actual wet zone, which is almost always larger than it looks.

For Category 2 (gray water from appliances or overflows) or Category 3 (sewage, floodwater) losses, containment, antimicrobial treatment, and selective demolition of non-salvageable materials are part of the scope. Clean water losses (Category 1) that sit long enough can degrade to Category 2, source classification matters, and it changes what the remediation requires.

The drying phase typically runs three to five days for a standard residential loss, though concrete slabs, multilayer flooring assemblies, and dense framing can extend that window. Daily moisture monitoring documents the drying curve and gives your insurance carrier the data it needs.

## Our process

1. **Emergency water removal.** Extraction equipment is deployed immediately to remove standing water from all affected areas. Furniture is blocked or moved; carpet and pad are evaluated for salvageability based on water category and saturation time.

2. **Moisture mapping.** Thermal imaging and penetrating moisture meters establish a full picture of the wet zone before any drying equipment is placed. This step prevents the common mistake of drying only what's visible while hidden moisture continues to damage structure.

3. **Structural drying setup.** Air movers are positioned to create a directed airflow pattern across wet surfaces; dehumidifiers are sized to the affected volume. For Category 2 or 3 losses, containment barriers and negative air pressure are established before drying begins.

4. **Daily monitoring and documentation.** Moisture readings are logged at each visit. Equipment is adjusted as materials dry at different rates. This documentation record is what your adjuster uses to verify the scope, and what protects you if a dispute arises later.

5. **Final clearance and scope closeout.** Drying is not complete until affected materials reach their dry standard, confirmed by instrument readings. A written drying log and photo documentation accompany every completed job, and any materials that required removal are noted for the reconstruction phase.

## What separates a good water damage response from a bad one

The most common failure in water damage restoration is declaring a structure dry based on surface feel rather than instrument readings. Plaster and lath construction, common in Lakewood's older bungalows and mid-century homes, can read bone-dry at the surface while the lath behind it stays saturated for weeks. Hardwood flooring over a concrete slab is another notorious example: the wood cups, the installer says it's dry, and six months later the subfloor shows active mold growth.

A second common problem is under-scoping the wet zone. Water follows the path of least resistance, which is rarely a straight line. It travels along top plates, drops through electrical chases, and pools in the lowest corner of a crawlspace. Operators who skip thermal imaging miss these migration paths and leave wet material inside a structure that's been signed off as dry.

Insurance adjusters look for a complete drying log, daily moisture readings tied to specific locations, equipment placement records, and a final clearance reading. A job documented this way is far easier to close without a dispute. Frontline Fire & Flood holds IICRC certifications in Water Restoration (WRT) and Applied Structural Drying (ASD), and drying protocols follow the IICRC S500 standard, which is the benchmark most carriers reference when reviewing a claim.

Typical costs depend heavily on the size of the affected area, the water category, how long the water sat before extraction began, and whether building materials need to come out. Most homeowners pay somewhere in the ranges below, these are industry-typical figures, not quotes.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

The Pacific Northwest's wet season runs roughly October through April, and Western Washington's clay-heavy soils don't drain quickly. That combination means crawlspace intrusion and foundation seepage are consistent seasonal problems for Lakewood homeowners, particularly in neighborhoods where the housing stock dates to the 1950s and 60s and crawlspace vapor barriers have degraded or were never installed. Freeze-thaw events, while less frequent here than in colder climates, do occur: a hard freeze after a wet stretch can split supply lines in uninsulated exterior walls or garage spaces. When temperatures drop into the mid-20s, those calls tend to come in clusters.
