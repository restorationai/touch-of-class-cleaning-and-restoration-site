A broken window or a hole in your roof left open overnight isn't just an inconvenience, it's an invitation. Rain soaks into exposed framing within hours. Looters and wildlife follow structural openings that go unaddressed. After a fire, smoke and soot continue migrating through an unsealed structure long after the flames are out. Emergency board-up and tarping is the work that stops a bad situation from becoming a catastrophic one, and the window for doing it right is measured in hours, not days.

## What emergency board-up and tarping actually involves

Board-up and tarping isn't stapling plastic sheeting over a hole and calling it done. Effective emergency securing means assessing every compromised opening, windows shattered by heat or impact, doors blown off frames, walls breached by fire or vehicle strike, roof decking exposed by storm damage, and matching the right closure method to each one.

For window and door openings, crews cut and fasten plywood (typically ¾" OSB or CDX) directly to the surrounding structural framing, not just the window casing. For roof damage, heavy-duty polyethylene tarps rated for UV and wind load are stretched tight, anchored with lumber battens screwed into undamaged decking, and lapped well past the damage perimeter so wind can't lift an edge. In fire-damaged structures, containment boarding also limits airflow that would otherwise keep drawing soot deeper into wall cavities and HVAC systems.

The work is physical and methodical. A crew typically carries a full complement of cut plywood panels, tarps in multiple sizes, cordless fastening tools, ladders rated for roof pitch, and fall-protection equipment for anything above single-story. On a straightforward residential loss, a few windows and a partial roof breach, a competent crew can have a structure secured in two to four hours.

## Our process

1. **Damage assessment and documentation**, Before a single panel goes up, the crew photographs every opening and affected surface. This documentation is what your insurance adjuster will use to verify the scope, so it has to be thorough: wide shots, close-ups, measurements, and notes on cause of loss.

2. **Structural triage**, Not every breach carries the same risk. The crew identifies which openings are allowing water intrusion, which are security vulnerabilities, and which are accelerating smoke or soot migration. Priority sequencing matters when a structure has multiple failure points.

3. **Board-up of windows, doors, and wall openings**, Plywood panels are cut to fit and fastened into structural framing members, not just trim. Panels are labeled with the opening location so adjusters and reconstruction crews can match them to the photo documentation.

4. **Roof tarping**, Tarps are positioned to shed water away from the structure, secured with batten boards screwed into sound decking on both sides of the damaged area. A tarp that pulls free in the next windstorm has accomplished nothing, proper anchoring is what separates a temporary fix that holds from one that fails at 2 a.m. during the next rain event.

5. **Secondary perimeter check**, Once primary openings are closed, the crew walks the full perimeter and checks the attic if accessible. Gaps at soffits, damaged vents, and compromised crawlspace access points are common secondary vulnerabilities that get missed on a rushed job.

## What separates a good board-up response from a bad one

The most common failure in emergency board-up is speed prioritized over documentation. A crew that boards windows without photographing the damage first creates a claims problem, adjusters need to see the pre-board condition to authorize coverage. Missing that step can delay or reduce your payout.

On the tarping side, the critical mistake is undersizing the tarp or anchoring it only at the edges. A tarp needs to extend at least two feet past the visible damage on all sides to account for water running along the underside of decking. Battens must be screwed into solid wood, not just nailed through the tarp field, nail-only attachment pulls through in wind. Insurance adjusters reviewing storm claims look specifically at whether tarping was installed in a workmanlike manner; a tarp that failed and allowed additional water intrusion can complicate coverage for the secondary damage.

For fire-damaged structures, boarding must also account for the fact that the building is a crime scene until the fire marshal clears it. Crews need to coordinate access and avoid disturbing evidence near the origin area.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Lakewood and the broader South Sound sit in a climate where the gap between a storm and the next rain event is often measured in hours, not days. Western Washington averages significant rainfall from October through April, which means a roof breach that might go a week without rain in a drier climate can take on water the same night here. The urgency of tarping is higher in this region than the national average suggests.

Older housing stock in Lakewood and neighboring Tacoma, much of it built in the mid-20th century, often has aging cedar shake or composition roofing that is already near the end of its service life. Storm damage to these roofs tends to be more extensive than it appears from the ground, and a thorough assessment before tarping is especially important.
