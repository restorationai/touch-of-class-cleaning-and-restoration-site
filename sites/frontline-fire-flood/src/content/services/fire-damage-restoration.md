Smoke doesn't stop moving when the flames go out. Within hours of a house fire, soot particles migrate through HVAC ducts, settle into closet corners, and bond chemically to painted walls, turning a contained kitchen fire into a whole-house odor problem. The window to limit secondary damage is short, and the materials involved (synthetic fabrics, engineered wood, modern insulation) produce residues that behave very differently from the wood-ash soot of older construction. Getting fire cleanup right means understanding what burned, not just what burned down.

## What fire damage restoration actually involves

Fire damage is three overlapping problems: structural damage from heat and flame, residue damage from soot and char, and chemical damage from smoke gases that penetrate porous materials long after the fire is out.

Soot itself comes in distinct types. Wet soot from slow, smoldering fires (upholstered furniture, rubber) is sticky and smears easily, it requires chemical sponges and specific cleaning agents, not dry wiping. Dry soot from fast-burning paper or wood is powdery and easier to vacuum but travels farther. Protein soot, the nearly invisible film left by kitchen fires involving meat or grease, has almost no visual presence but produces a persistent, acrid odor that intensifies when surfaces are heated. Each type requires a different cleaning approach, and a crew that treats all soot the same will leave odor and staining behind.

Beyond residue, structural fire damage can compromise framing, sheathing, and load-bearing elements in ways that aren't obvious from street level. Drywall that looks intact may have lost structural integrity from heat exposure. Subfloor adhesives can fail. Electrical wiring insulation degrades at temperatures well below visible charring. A thorough post-fire restoration scope accounts for all of it, not just what's visibly black.

The IICRC FSRT (Fire and Smoke Restoration Technician) standard guides the technical approach Frontline Fire & Flood's certified team follows on every loss.

## Our process

1. **Emergency stabilization and board-up.** Before cleaning begins, the structure needs to be secured. Broken windows and compromised doors are boarded, tarps are placed over roof openings, and utilities are confirmed safe. This step protects both the structure and the contents from weather, vandalism, and further contamination while the full scope is developed.

2. **Soot characterization and scope development.** A technician walks every affected room, including areas with no visible damage, to identify soot types, map smoke migration through the HVAC system, and flag materials that need pack-out versus on-site cleaning. This assessment drives the written scope of work and the documentation your insurance carrier will require.

3. **Contents pack-out and inventory.** Salvageable contents are photographed, inventoried, and moved to a clean environment for cleaning and storage. Items that have absorbed smoke odor but are structurally intact, clothing, documents, electronics, furniture, often respond well to ozone treatment or ultrasonic cleaning off-site. Items that cannot be restored are documented for the claim.

4. **Structural cleaning and odor neutralization.** Surfaces are cleaned in the correct sequence, ceilings before walls, walls before floors, using the chemistry appropriate to the soot type present. Thermal fogging pushes a deodorizing agent into the same porous pathways that smoke traveled, neutralizing odor at the molecular level rather than masking it. In cases with heavy protein or synthetic soot, ozone treatment may follow in unoccupied spaces to break down residual odor compounds.

5. **Reconstruction and clearance.** Once cleaning is complete and the structure is confirmed dry (fire suppression water is a secondary water damage event that runs concurrently), reconstruction begins, framing, drywall, insulation, paint, and finish work, to return the space to pre-loss condition. Final walkthrough confirms no residual odor before the job is closed.

## What separates a good fire response from a bad one

The most common failure in fire and smoke restoration is treating odor as a cleaning problem when it's actually a chemistry problem. Wiping soot off a wall removes the visible residue but leaves the volatile organic compounds that cause smell embedded in the drywall paper, the insulation behind it, and the framing beyond that. Homeowners who hire general contractors or cleaning crews without fire-specific training often find the odor returns as soon as the heat comes on the following winter.

A second common gap is incomplete HVAC cleaning. Smoke travels the path of least resistance, and in a forced-air system that means ducts carry soot to every room in the house, including rooms that appear unaffected. Adjusters reviewing a claim will look for duct cleaning documentation; without it, odor callbacks are almost guaranteed.

Insurance documentation is its own discipline. Adjusters need itemized scope lines, pre-cleaning photographs, material samples for soot type, and a clear separation between fire damage and pre-existing conditions. A restoration company that doesn't produce this documentation creates delays, underpayment, and disputes that fall on the homeowner to resolve.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

In the South Puget Sound area, fire losses carry a secondary complication that's easy to underestimate: suppression water in a wet climate. Lakewood and the surrounding Pierce County communities see significant rainfall from fall through spring, and a structure opened up for fire damage, broken windows, roof penetrations, compromised siding, can accumulate additional moisture within days. Mold colonization can begin in as little as 48 to 72 hours in unheated, wet conditions. Coordinating board-up, structural drying, and fire cleanup as a single integrated scope, rather than sequential handoffs between separate contractors, is especially important here.

Older housing stock in established Lakewood neighborhoods, particularly homes built before the 1980s, may also involve lead paint and asbestos-containing materials that become friable and airborne when disturbed by fire and cleanup activity. Frontline Fire & Flood is EPA Lead-Safe Certified and OSHA trained, and the scope development process includes identifying regulated materials before work begins.
