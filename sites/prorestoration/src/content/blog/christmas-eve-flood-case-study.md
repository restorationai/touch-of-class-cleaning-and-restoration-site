A flood on a holiday weekend is one of the worst-timed losses a homeowner can face. Contractors are harder to reach, insurance adjusters are out of office, and every hour of standing water adds to the repair bill. Here's what one homeowner experienced when her house flooded on Christmas Eve, in her own words.

>, Angela, verified Google review

## Why Calling Right Away Matters on a Holiday Flood

Standing water starts causing secondary damage within hours, not days. Drywall wicks moisture upward, wood flooring starts to cup and swell, and by the 24 to 48 hour mark conditions are right for mold to begin growing behind walls and under flooring. Angela called the morning after Christmas, as soon as a crew could reasonably be reached, and that decision kept the loss from compounding over an extended holiday stretch, when many contractors simply don't pick up.

For anyone facing a similar situation, [what to do in the first 24 hours after water damage](/blog/what-to-do-first-24-hours-water-damage/) matters almost as much as who you call. Shutting off the water source if it's still running, moving valuables off wet flooring, and getting a restoration crew on-site starts the drying clock instead of letting the clock run against you.

## What "Showed Up Within 2 Hours" Actually Looks Like

A two-hour flood response means a crew arrives with extraction equipment already staged, not scrambling to figure out what's needed once they're on scene. ProRestoration Services runs 24/7 dispatch, so a call placed the day after Christmas goes to a live person, not a voicemail that gets returned after the holiday.

On arrival, the first steps look the same on nearly every flood job: confirm the water source is off, extract standing water with truck-mounted equipment, then map the wet areas with a moisture meter so nothing gets missed behind baseboards, under cabinetry, or beneath flooring. Only after that assessment does drying equipment, air movers and dehumidifiers, get placed room by room. Skipping the mapping step is how homes end up with hidden mold months later.

## Why "Several Days" to Dry Out Is Normal, Not a Delay

Structural drying after a whole-house flood typically runs three to five days, sometimes longer depending on how much material absorbed water and how many rooms were affected. That timeline follows the [IICRC](https://www.iicrc.org) S500 water damage standard, which sets drying targets based on daily moisture readings rather than a fixed calendar. A crew doesn't guess when a house is dry, they measure it, log it, and pull equipment only once the numbers confirm it. Homeowners who want the fuller picture on what drives that timeline can read [how long water damage restoration actually takes](/blog/how-long-does-water-damage-restoration-take/).

Angela's "several days" lines up with what a typical whole-house flood requires. Rushing that process to save a day usually just means moisture gets trapped inside wall cavities or subfloor, and it resurfaces later as odor or mold.

## What It Means When the Owner Checks In Every Day

Daily site visits from ownership aren't standard practice everywhere, but they matter on a job carrying this much stress. Jack stopping by every day gave Angela a direct line to someone accountable for the outcome, not just a rotating crew reading moisture logs off a clipboard. That kind of oversight is what keeps a multi-day drying job from quietly turning into a multi-week one: equipment gets repositioned when readings plateau, and small issues get caught before they turn into change orders.
