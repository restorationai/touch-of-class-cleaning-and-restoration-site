The smell hits before you see the damage. Even after the flames are out and the fire department clears the scene, smoke particles have already traveled through your HVAC system, settled into wall cavities, and bonded to every porous surface in the house — sometimes rooms away from where the fire burned. Fire and smoke restoration is not cleaning. It is a technical process of identifying what soot types are present, what materials are salvageable, and what sequence of treatments will stop secondary damage from compounding the loss.

## What fire damage restoration actually involves

Fire leaves three overlapping problems: char and structural damage where the fire burned, soot and smoke residue that spreads far beyond the fire's origin, and water and chemical damage from suppression. Each requires a different approach, and treating them out of sequence — or missing one entirely — is how restoration jobs fail.

Soot is not uniform. Dry, ashy soot from wood and paper responds to dry-cleaning methods and HEPA vacuuming. Wet, smeared soot from plastics and synthetics requires chemical sponges and alkaline cleaners. Protein residue from kitchen fires is nearly invisible but produces a persistent, rancid odor and bonds tightly to painted surfaces and cabinetry. Misidentifying the soot type and applying the wrong cleaning agent can permanently set staining into drywall, cabinetry, and hardwood.

Smoke odor that isn't fully neutralized at the molecular level will return — especially when Sacramento's summer heat drives indoor temperatures up and reactivates residue embedded in framing and insulation. Thermal fogging and hydroxyl or ozone treatment address odor at the structural level, not just at the surface.

Contents — furniture, clothing, documents, electronics — are assessed for salvageability, inventoried, and either cleaned on-site or packed out to a controlled environment for specialized restoration. A thorough pack-out protects contents from ongoing soot exposure and gives the crew unobstructed access to affected surfaces.

## Our process

1. **Emergency stabilization and safety assessment.** Before any cleaning begins, the structure is assessed for electrical hazards, compromised load-bearing elements, and air quality. Utilities may be isolated. Temporary board-up or tarping is installed if the envelope is breached.

2. **Soot characterization and scope development.** Technicians identify soot types room by room — dry, wet, or protein — and map the spread of smoke migration through the structure. This determines which cleaning protocols apply to which surfaces and whether contents can be cleaned in place or require pack-out.

3. **Contents inventory and pack-out.** Salvageable contents are documented, photographed, and removed to a climate-controlled facility for cleaning and storage. This step protects your belongings and creates the itemized inventory your insurance adjuster needs.

4. **Structural cleaning and odor neutralization.** Affected surfaces are cleaned using the protocol matched to the soot type present. Thermal fogging penetrates cavities and porous materials to neutralize odor at the same depth smoke reached. Hydroxyl generators or ozone treatment may follow, depending on the severity and material types involved. HEPA air scrubbers run throughout to capture airborne particulates.

5. **Clearance and reconstruction handoff.** Once cleaning and odor neutralization are complete, the structure is documented and — where reconstruction is needed — a detailed scope is handed off for permitting, structural repair, drywall, painting, and finish work. ## What separates a good fire response from a bad one

The most common failure in fire and smoke restoration is treating the job as a cleaning project rather than a remediation project. Surface cleaning without addressing smoke migration into wall cavities, subfloor assemblies, and HVAC ductwork leaves residue that will off-gas for months. Homeowners often notice the odor returning weeks after a contractor has signed off — that is almost always residue that was never reached.

Insurance adjusters look for itemized documentation: a room-by-room soot characterization, a contents inventory with pre-loss value estimates, and a written scope that separates cleaning costs from structural repair costs. Adjusters also look for evidence that affected HVAC systems were addressed — smoke travels through ductwork and can recontaminate a cleaned space if the system is run before the ducts are cleaned or replaced.

TDI® USA technicians hold IICRC FSRT (Fire and Smoke Restoration Technician) certification, which means soot typing, cleaning chemistry, and odor neutralization protocols are applied by the standard — not by guesswork.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Sacramento's dry summers and the surrounding foothills create elevated wildfire risk from late spring through early fall. Homes near the urban-wildland interface — Rancho Murieta, El Dorado Hills, Auburn, and communities along the Sierra Nevada foothills — face the additional challenge of ash and smoke infiltration from nearby fires even when a structure itself is not directly burned. Fine particulate from wildfire smoke penetrates HVAC systems and settles on interior surfaces, and the cleanup protocol for that exposure differs from a structural fire response. The Central Valley's heat also means smoke odor embedded in framing and insulation becomes more volatile in summer — a reason to address residue thoroughly before temperatures climb.
