The first sign is usually subtle — wood floors that feel spongy in winter, a musty smell that lingers no matter how often you clean, or HVAC bills that keep climbing without explanation. What those symptoms share is a source: an open, uncontrolled crawl space pulling humid ground air up through your subfloor and into your living space. Crawl space encapsulation seals that pathway permanently, replacing a damp, vapor-permeable dirt floor and uninsulated rim joists with a continuous, airtight membrane system that keeps ground moisture where it belongs — in the ground.

## What crawl space encapsulation actually involves

Encapsulation is not the same as adding a vapor barrier. A thin poly sheet stapled to the dirt is a vapor retarder — it slows moisture migration but leaves the perimeter walls, rim joists, and vents as open conduits. Full encapsulation treats the entire crawl space envelope: floor, walls, piers, and any penetrations, and it typically pairs that membrane with a controlled drainage and dehumidification system.

The work involves removing existing debris, damaged insulation, and old poly sheeting; cleaning and treating any wood that shows early fungal growth or elevated moisture content; installing a reinforced polyethylene liner (typically 12–20 mil) that runs continuously from the center of the floor up the perimeter walls and is sealed at every seam and penetration with compatible tape; closing and sealing foundation vents; insulating rim joists with closed-cell spray foam or rigid board; and installing a crawl space dehumidifier sized to the square footage with a condensate drain to daylight or a sump.

A properly encapsulated crawl space should read below 55% relative humidity at equilibrium. Wood framing that has been chronically damp will take several weeks to stabilize to its new environment — that drying period is part of the process, not a sign something went wrong.

## Our process

1. **Moisture and structural assessment.** Before any material goes in, the crawl space is measured and photographed. Wood moisture content is logged at multiple points using a pin-type meter. Any standing water, active leaks, or compromised joists are documented — encapsulation over an unresolved water intrusion problem will trap moisture rather than control it.

2. **Debris removal and surface prep.** Old insulation, deteriorated vapor barriers, and biological growth are removed and bagged. Wood surfaces with surface mold or staining are treated per IICRC AMRT protocols before the liner goes down. The dirt floor is graded to eliminate low spots where condensation could pool under the membrane.

3. **Liner installation and sealing.** The reinforced poly liner is laid across the floor and run up the foundation walls, overlapping at seams by a minimum of 12 inches and taped with a compatible butyl or acrylic seam tape. The liner is mechanically fastened to the wall above the anticipated moisture line and sealed around every pier, pipe, and penetration. No gaps, no shortcuts at the corners — those are the failure points inspectors look for.

4. **Vent sealing and rim joist insulation.** Foundation vents are closed with rigid foam plugs sealed in place. Rim joists — the single largest thermal and vapor bypass in most crawl spaces — are insulated with closed-cell spray foam to achieve both an air seal and an R-value that meets California Energy Code minimums for conditioned or semi-conditioned crawl spaces.

5. **Dehumidifier installation and baseline reading.** A crawl space-rated dehumidifier is installed, plumbed to drain, and set to maintain relative humidity below 55%. A final moisture and humidity reading is logged and provided to the homeowner as a baseline for future reference.

## What separates a good encapsulation from a bad one

The most common failure point is treating encapsulation as a cosmetic fix rather than a building science correction. Contractors who skip the moisture assessment and go straight to liner installation are sealing problems in rather than out — if there is a slow foundation leak or a plumbing drip above the crawl space, the membrane will hold that moisture against the framing indefinitely.

The second most common failure is liner weight and seam integrity. A 6-mil poly sheet will tear around piers and puncture under foot traffic during future HVAC or plumbing service calls. Reinforced liners in the 12–20 mil range are the industry standard for a reason. Seams that are overlapped but not taped will separate as the liner shifts seasonally.

Insurance adjusters reviewing encapsulation as part of a mold or water damage claim will look for documentation of pre-existing conditions, a written scope that distinguishes encapsulation from remediation, and moisture readings before and after. Bundling encapsulation into a mold claim without that documentation is one of the most common reasons supplemental claims get denied.

OSHA-trained crews also recognize that crawl spaces can accumulate CO, radon, and biological hazards — proper ventilation and PPE during the work are not optional, particularly in Sacramento-area homes built before 1980 that may have vermiculite insulation or legacy pest treatments in the soil.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Sacramento's climate creates a crawl space moisture problem that runs in two distinct phases. Winter and early spring bring sustained ground saturation from rainfall and a high water table in low-lying neighborhoods — areas near the American and Sacramento Rivers can see standing water under homes after a wet January even without any plumbing failure. Summer brings the opposite pressure: hot, dry air above grade and relatively cooler, humid soil below, which drives condensation onto cold water pipes and the underside of the subfloor during the hottest months.

Homes in the Delta-adjacent communities and in older Sacramento neighborhoods built on expansive clay soils are particularly prone to foundation movement that opens gaps in the crawl space envelope over time — gaps that no vapor barrier addresses unless the liner is run continuously up the wall and sealed at the top.
