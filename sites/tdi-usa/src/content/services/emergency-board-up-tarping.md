A broken window at 2 a.m. or a roof torn open by a falling tree doesn't wait for business hours — and neither does the secondary damage that follows. Within hours of a breach, rain, wind, and opportunistic theft can turn a contained loss into a far larger claim. Emergency board-up and tarping is the work that stops that clock: physically sealing the structure so that everything inside — your belongings, your framing, your flooring — stays dry and secure while the longer restoration process gets organized.

## What emergency board-up and tarping actually involves

Board-up and tarping is hands-on, physical work that requires the right materials, the right fastening systems, and an understanding of what the structure actually needs — not just what's visible from the driveway.

**Board-up** typically means cutting and securing plywood panels (usually ¾-inch OSB or CDX) over broken windows, compromised doors, garage openings, or any wall section that's been breached by fire, impact, or forced entry. Panels are secured with structural screws or through-bolted frames, not staples or tape, because the goal is weather resistance and a reasonable deterrent against unauthorized entry — both of which matter to your insurance adjuster.

**Roof tarping** involves laying heavy-duty polyethylene tarps (typically 6-mil or heavier) over damaged or missing sections of roofing, then securing them with cap nails, furring strips, or weighted lumber runs along the ridge and eaves. A tarp that's simply draped and weighted at the corners will lift in 30 mph wind — a common failure that leaves the structure exposed again before morning. Proper installation routes the tarp over the ridge when possible, so water sheds away from the breach rather than pooling at it.

On fire-damaged structures, board-up also serves a contamination-control function: sealing the building limits the spread of soot and smoke odor to the exterior and reduces the risk of weather driving additional moisture into char-damaged materials.

## Our process

**1. Damage assessment and hazard check**
Before any material goes up, the crew walks the perimeter and interior (where safe) to identify every breach — not just the obvious one. A fire that burned through a soffit may have also compromised an attic vent or a dormer. Missed openings mean the job isn't done.

**2. Structural stability confirmation**
On fire or impact losses, load-bearing walls, roof decking, and floor systems are checked before anyone climbs. If a section is unsafe to access, the crew works from ladders or lifts rather than walking compromised decking.

**3. Material cutting and staging**
Panels are cut to fit each opening on-site. Pre-cut or generic-size panels leave gaps at corners and frames — gaps that let in water, pests, and wind. Each panel is labeled and staged before installation begins so the sequence is efficient and nothing is left open longer than necessary.

**4. Installation and fastening**
Windows and door openings are boarded with panels secured into the surrounding framing, not just the window frame itself. Roof tarps are laid with a minimum 12-inch overlap at seams and run over the ridge wherever the breach is within a few feet of it. All fasteners are verified before the crew comes down.

**5. Documentation for the insurance claim**
Every opening is photographed before and after boarding. Measurements, materials used, and labor time are recorded in a format that supports your claim. TDI® USA works with all major insurance carriers and handles the claim documentation directly, so you're not translating between a crew and an adjuster.

## What separates a good board-up response from a bad one

The most common failure in emergency board-up is incomplete coverage — a crew boards the front window that's obviously broken and misses the attic vent that burned through, or tarps the main roof section and leaves a valley exposed. Insurance adjusters reviewing a subsequent water intrusion claim will look at whether the board-up was thorough, and gaps in coverage can complicate what the carrier will pay for.

A second common problem is inadequate fastening. Tarps secured only with sandbags or loose lumber will shift or lift in the wind gusts that accompany most Sacramento Valley storms. Panels screwed only into window trim rather than wall framing will pull free under sustained wind load. Proper fastening adds time to the job — but it's the difference between a board-up that holds for two weeks while the claim processes and one that fails overnight.

Finally, documentation quality matters more than most homeowners realize. Adjusters want to see the pre-board-up condition of every opening, the materials used, and the scope of work — not a single photo of a finished panel. Thorough photo and measurement documentation is what connects the emergency service to the rest of the claim.

## What does emergency board-up and tarping cost?

Typical costs depend heavily on the number of openings, roof pitch and accessibility, and whether the loss involves fire damage (which adds PPE and contamination considerations). Most homeowners pay somewhere in the ranges below — these are industry-typical figures, not TDI® USA quotes, and every loss is different; TDI® USA provides a written scope before work begins.

Homeowners insurance typically covers emergency board-up and tarping as part of the "protection from further damage" obligation in most standard HO-3 policies — meaning the cost is generally included in your claim rather than paid out of pocket, subject to your deductible.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Sacramento's dry summers mean that roof damage from heat-stressed materials or wildfire ember cast can sit undetected until the first fall rain arrives — often as an atmospheric river event that delivers several inches in 24 hours rather than a gradual onset. A small breach that seemed inconsequential in August can admit significant water by October.

The Valley's tule fog season (late fall through early spring) also creates sustained moisture conditions at ground level; a boarded structure that isn't fully sealed can accumulate condensation inside, which matters if there's already smoke or fire damage to porous materials. Board-up on fire losses should account for vapor management, not just wind and rain exclusion.

Wildfires in the Sierra Nevada foothills — communities like Auburn, Grass Valley, and El Dorado Hills — often require board-up under active ember conditions, which changes the crew's PPE requirements and the materials selected.
