The contractors have packed up, the dumpster is gone, and the space looks finished — until you run your finger along the windowsill and come away with a gray smear of drywall dust. Construction debris doesn't disappear when the last nail is driven; it migrates. Fine particulate from cutting, sanding, and spraying settles into HVAC returns, coats cabinet interiors, and embeds in grout lines that were never meant to hold it. Post-construction and specialty cleaning is the work that bridges a completed build or renovation and a space that's actually ready to occupy.

## What post-construction and specialty cleaning actually involves

Post-construction cleanup is not a pass with a mop. The scope depends on the phase of work — rough clean, final clean, or touch-up clean — and each phase has a different target.

A **rough clean** happens mid-project or immediately after major trades finish: large debris removal, sweeping, and protecting finished surfaces before the next subcontractor arrives. A **final clean** is the occupancy-ready pass that most homeowners and property managers care about: streak-free glass, grout haze removal from tile, adhesive residue off hardwood, paint overspray buffed from fixtures, and HVAC vents vacuumed before the system runs for the first time. A **touch-up clean** follows punch-list corrections — the small repairs and repaints that leave fresh dust and scuff marks on surfaces that were already cleaned.

Specialty cleaning within this service category extends to post-renovation deep cleaning after fire or water restoration reconstruction, move-in/move-out cleans for commercial tenant improvements, and high-dusting in warehouses or facilities after overhead work. Equipment on a professional final clean includes HEPA-filtered vacuums rated for fine construction particulate, microfiber systems that trap rather than redistribute dust, commercial-grade glass polishing compounds, and grout haze removers matched to the tile type. Drywall dust is alkaline — the wrong cleaning chemistry on certain stone or glass can etch the surface.

## Our process

1. **Site walk and scope confirmation.** Before any cleaning begins, the crew walks the space with the general contractor's punch list (if available) and documents existing conditions — scratches, chips, or staining that predate the clean. This protects everyone and sets a clear baseline.

2. **Debris and gross contamination removal.** Loose construction debris, packaging, tape, and protective film are removed first. HVAC registers are covered or pulled and vacuumed to prevent fine dust from recirculating once HEPA equipment starts moving air.

3. **Top-down HEPA vacuuming and high-dusting.** Ceilings, light fixtures, tops of door frames, and exposed structural elements are addressed before any horizontal surface. Drywall dust is light enough to stay airborne for hours — cleaning floors before ceilings simply means cleaning the floors twice.

4. **Surface-specific detail cleaning.** Windows are cleaned in sequence (frames, tracks, glass) with appropriate compounds. Tile receives grout haze treatment matched to the grout type and age. Cabinetry interiors are wiped, hardware is polished, and appliances are cleaned inside and out. Hardwood and LVP floors are cleaned with pH-neutral products — alkaline construction residue left on a wood floor finish causes premature dulling.

5. **Final inspection and sign-off.** The crew walks the space a second time using raking light (a flashlight held at a low angle to the surface) to catch streaks, dust shadows, and missed adhesive. Any items flagged during the walk are corrected before the space is released.

## What separates a good post-construction clean from a bad one

The most common failure point is sequence. Crews that clean floors before high-dusting, or polish glass before wiping frames, create rework. The second most common failure is chemistry mismatch: using an all-purpose cleaner on grout haze that requires an acidic dissolver, or applying a solvent-based adhesive remover to a floor finish it will cloud.

HVAC contamination is the failure that homeowners notice months later. Construction particulate that enters ductwork during the clean — because registers weren't protected — circulates every time the system runs, coating surfaces that were just cleaned and eventually clogging filters faster than normal. A professional crew treats duct protection as a first step, not an afterthought.

For commercial tenant improvements and insurance-funded reconstruction projects, documentation matters as much as the cleaning itself. Adjusters and property managers want before-and-after photographs, a written scope of what was cleaned and with what products, and sign-off records. Cleaning without documentation leaves the contractor and the property owner exposed if a dispute arises over condition at turnover.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

In Sacramento's Central Valley climate, construction activity peaks in spring and fall when temperatures are moderate and concrete and paint cure predictably. Summer heat accelerates adhesive cure and can make grout haze harder to remove if tile was grouted in high temperatures without adequate moisture retention — a detail that affects which chemistry the cleaning crew needs to bring. Winter rains mean tracked-in mud and silica-laden soil on freshly finished floors is a real risk during the final construction weeks; protective floor coverings and a dedicated entry-point clean are worth specifying in the scope.

Older Sacramento neighborhoods — the grid streets of Midtown, Curtis Park, East Sacramento, and Land Park — frequently see gut-and-restore renovations on Craftsman bungalows and early postwar homes. These projects often disturb lead paint and asbestos-containing materials, which means the post-construction clean may follow abatement work and requires awareness of residual hazards. TDI® USA's team is OSHA trained and works within the protocols established by the abatement contractor's clearance report.
