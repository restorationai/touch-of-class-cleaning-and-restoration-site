After a fire, flood, or sewage backup, the structure gets most of the attention — but it's your belongings that carry the real weight of the loss. The smell locked into a wool rug, the soot ground into upholstery fibers, the waterlogged photo albums sitting in a box on the floor: these don't wait. Within 48 to 72 hours, secondary damage accelerates — mold colonizes saturated textiles, smoke residue etches into glass and chrome, and dye transfer stains fabrics that were untouched by the original event. Contents restoration and storage is the discipline of stopping that clock.

## What contents restoration and storage actually involves

Contents restoration is not cleaning. It is a structured process of inventorying, categorizing, treating, and storing personal property — furniture, electronics, clothing, documents, art, appliances — while the structure behind it is being dried, deodorized, or rebuilt.

The work begins with a detailed pack-out: every item is photographed, logged, and assigned a condition code before it leaves the property. That documentation matters for your insurance claim and for returning the right item to the right room when the job is done. Items are then transported to a climate-controlled facility where they are sorted by material type and loss category — what was touched by smoke, what was touched by water, what was touched by both.

Treatment methods vary by material and contamination type. Ultrasonic cleaning tanks use high-frequency sound waves to pull soot and residue from hard surfaces, electronics housings, and non-porous items without abrasion. Ozone chambers and hydroxyl generators address odor at the molecular level in textiles and soft goods that cannot be immersed. Freeze-drying (lyophilization) is used for water-damaged documents and photographs when time allows. Thermal fogging penetrates porous furniture surfaces where surface wiping alone cannot reach.

Storage runs concurrent with restoration. Items that are clean and dry but cannot return to the home yet — because drywall is open, floors are being refinished, or the structure is still being rebuilt — are held in a secure, climate-controlled warehouse. Every item remains inventoried and retrievable.

## Our process

1. **On-site inventory and condition assessment.** Before a single box is packed, every item in the affected area is photographed and logged. Items are sorted into three categories: restorable, questionable (needs evaluation at the facility), and non-restorable. The non-restorable list becomes part of your insurance documentation.

2. **Careful pack-out and chain-of-custody transport.** Furniture, art, and fragile items are wrapped and packed using materials appropriate to their condition — wet items are not sealed in plastic, smoke-affected textiles are not bundled with clean ones. Each box and item is labeled to a room-level location map so reinstallation is systematic, not a guessing game.

3. **Facility triage and material-specific treatment.** At the climate-controlled facility, items are separated by material type and contamination category. Hard goods go through ultrasonic cleaning where appropriate. Soft goods and upholstered furniture are treated for odor using hydroxyl or ozone technology. Electronics are evaluated and dried under controlled conditions before any power-on testing. Documents and photographs are assessed for freeze-drying candidacy.

4. **Monitored climate-controlled storage.** Clean and treated items are stored in a secured, temperature- and humidity-regulated facility. Your inventory log is maintained throughout — you can request a specific item if you need it before the job is complete.

5. **Coordinated return and reinstallation.** When the structure is ready, contents are returned according to the original room map. Final condition is documented at reinstallation, and any items that did not survive the restoration process are noted for the adjuster's final review.

## What separates a good contents response from a bad one

The most common failure in contents restoration is the absence of a pre-pack-out inventory. Without it, there is no baseline for the insurance claim, no way to prove an item was present and damaged, and no accountability if something is lost or damaged during transport. Adjusters flag missing inventories immediately — it slows settlements and creates disputes that fall on the homeowner.

The second failure is treating all soot the same way. Protein soot — from cooking fires or small organic burns — is nearly invisible, extremely adhesive, and carries a persistent odor that standard wiping spreads rather than removes. Synthetic soot from burning plastics, foam, or electrical insulation is oily and penetrates porous surfaces quickly. The cleaning chemistry and method differ between them, and using the wrong approach can permanently set the residue into upholstered furniture or finished wood.

A third failure is rushing the odor phase. Masking sprays create the impression of cleanliness without addressing the source. Proper deodorization requires that the item be clean first — odor treatment applied over residue is ineffective. IICRC FSRT-trained technicians understand this sequence; crews without fire and smoke restoration training often skip it.

Insurance adjusters also look for itemized treatment logs — not just a line item for "contents cleaning" but a record of what method was applied to which item and why. That documentation supports the claim and protects the homeowner if a dispute arises later.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Sacramento's dry summers and wet winters create two distinct contents risk windows. From November through March, atmospheric river events can push water into homes quickly — often through roof penetrations, subfloor gaps, or overwhelmed drainage — leaving contents on lower floors exposed before the source is even identified. Saturated upholstered furniture and area rugs in a 60°F interior can begin showing mold growth within 48 hours if not removed and treated.

In summer, the inverse problem appears after wildfire smoke events. Even homes that are not directly affected by fire can accumulate fine particulate and odor compounds through HVAC systems and open windows. Soft goods — curtains, bedding, upholstered furniture — absorb smoke odor readily, and the longer they sit in a smoke-affected environment, the deeper the penetration. Early pack-out and treatment in these situations significantly improves restorability.
