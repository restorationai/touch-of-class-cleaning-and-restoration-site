The fastest way to get burned by a restoration company is to hire the first one that calls you back after a disaster. When water is spreading across your hardwood floors or smoke smell is soaking into your walls, urgency is real — but so is the risk of signing a contract with a company that overcharges, cuts corners on drying, or disappears before the job is finished. Here is what to look for, what to ask, and what to walk away from.

## Why the Restoration Industry Attracts Bad Actors

Restoration work sits at an uncomfortable intersection: desperate property owners, insurance money, and work that happens mostly inside walls where nobody can see it. That combination draws in storm-chasing contractors who follow disasters from city to city, unlicensed crews who underbid legitimate companies, and firms that do a fast surface dry and hand you a certificate before the moisture readings actually clear.

In Sacramento specifically, the pattern shows up after atmospheric river events that push water through crawl spaces and foundation walls, and after the dry-season wildfires that push smoke and ash into HVAC systems across the region. When a loss event hits a large area, your phone may ring with unsolicited offers before you have even called your insurance company. That is almost always a sign to slow down.

The restoration industry has legitimate credentialing bodies — the IICRC (Institute of Inspection, Cleaning and Restoration Certification) is the most widely recognized — and legitimate companies carry them. But a logo on a website is easy to fake. You need to verify.

## What to Check Before You Sign Anything

Treat the first conversation with any restoration company as an interview, not a rescue. Here is what to confirm before a crew sets foot in your property:

1. **Verify the license.** In California, most restoration work requires a contractor's license through the CSLB (Contractors State License Board). You can look up any license number at cslb.ca.gov in about 30 seconds. If the company cannot give you a license number, stop there.

2. **Ask for proof of insurance.** General liability and workers' compensation are both required. Ask for a certificate of insurance naming you as the certificate holder. A legitimate company will send it without hesitation.

3. **Check IICRC certification.** The IICRC maintains a public directory at iicrc.org where you can search by company name. Certification means technicians have been trained on industry-standard drying protocols — the S500 for water damage, the S520 for mold. It does not guarantee quality, but its absence is a red flag.

4. **Read the contract before equipment arrives.** Some companies place dehumidifiers and air movers within the first hour and then present a contract that locks you in. Once equipment is running, you are in a much weaker negotiating position. Read the scope of work, the daily equipment rental rates, and the authorization language before anything is signed.

5. **Understand the assignment of benefits clause.** Some restoration contracts include an AOB (assignment of benefits) provision that transfers your insurance claim rights directly to the contractor. California has restrictions on AOB in certain contexts, but the language varies. If you see it, ask your insurance adjuster before signing.

## Red Flags During the Estimate and Inspection

A trustworthy restoration company will show you their moisture readings. They will use a thermal imaging camera or a penetrating moisture meter to map where water has actually traveled — not just where it is visible. If an estimator eyeballs the damage and writes a scope without taking any measurements, that is a problem in both directions: they may miss saturated areas behind walls, or they may claim damage that does not exist.

Other things that should give you pause:

- Pressure to sign immediately, especially with language like "we can only hold this price for today."
- Vague line items on the estimate ("water mitigation — $4,200") with no breakdown of equipment, labor, or square footage.
- No mention of a drying goal or a projected timeline. Industry standard drying for a typical water loss runs three to five days with proper equipment; anyone promising overnight results or quoting six weeks is worth questioning.
- Reluctance to communicate directly with your insurance adjuster. Legitimate companies do this routinely.
- No written protocol for mold-affected materials. If a water loss is more than 24 to 48 hours old, mold colonization is already a possibility. A company that does not address this in their scope is either cutting corners or uninformed.

## Questions to Ask About the Actual Work

Once you have cleared the baseline checks, get specific about how the work will be done:

**For water damage:** Ask what drying standard they follow. The IICRC S500 is the industry benchmark. Ask how they will verify that structural materials — subfloor, wall cavity framing, insulation — have reached drying goals before they close anything up. Ask who monitors the equipment daily and how readings are documented.

**For fire and smoke damage:** Ask whether they do a full contents inventory before any items are removed. Ask how they handle HVAC cleaning, since smoke particles travel through duct systems and re-contaminate a space if the system is not properly cleaned. Ask about odor treatment — ozone, hydroxyl, thermal fogging — and which method they plan to use and why.

**For mold remediation:** Ask whether they follow the IICRC S520 protocol. Ask whether they will provide post-remediation verification (PRV) testing, and whether that testing is done by a third party independent of the remediation company. In a legitimate mold job, the company doing the cleanup should not be the same entity certifying that the cleanup worked.

## The Insurance Piece

Most property owners go into a restoration claim without understanding that they have the right to choose their own contractor. Your insurance company may recommend a preferred vendor — and those vendors are not necessarily bad — but you are not obligated to use them. The carrier pays the claim; you choose who does the work.

Get your own estimate. If it differs significantly from the carrier's estimate, a licensed public adjuster or your contractor can negotiate on your behalf. Document everything: photograph the damage before any work begins, keep all receipts, and ask the restoration company for daily moisture logs that can be submitted with the claim.

One thing to avoid: do not let a contractor tell you what your policy covers. That is your adjuster's job. A contractor who makes coverage promises — "don't worry, insurance will cover all of this" — is either guessing or setting you up for a dispute later.
