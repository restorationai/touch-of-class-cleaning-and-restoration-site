## Why Ballwin Properties See Repeated Storm Damage

Much of Ballwin's residential housing stock was built during the suburban expansion of the 1970s and 1980s, which means a significant share of homes carry original or once-replaced roofing systems that are now approaching the end of their rated service life. Asphalt shingles from that era are more brittle in cold weather and more prone to granule loss after repeated hail strikes — damage that isn't always visible from the ground but shows up clearly in a close-up inspection. The area's mature tree canopy, one of the things that makes neighborhoods along Claymont and the Kehrs Mill corridor so appealing, also means storm events regularly bring large limbs — and occasionally whole trees — down onto rooflines, gutters, and fence lines.

West St. Louis County also experiences a wide temperature swing between seasons, which accelerates the freeze-thaw cycle in roofing materials. A shingle that took a marginal hit during a summer hailstorm may not fail visibly until January ice works into the micro-crack left behind. By then, the interior damage from slow water infiltration can be well underway.

## Our Storm Damage Restoration Process in Ballwin

When ProCraft Exteriors responds to a storm damage call in Ballwin, the first step is always a thorough damage assessment — roof, siding, gutters, fascia, and any penetration points where water may have already entered the structure. From there, the process moves in a logical sequence:

- **Material matching and replacement** — we work to source shingles and siding that match your existing profile and color, which matters both aesthetically and for HOA compliance in many Ballwin communities
- **Final inspection and documentation** to close out the claim file

For homes where water has already entered through storm-created openings, interior drying and moisture mapping are part of the scope — we don't hand off that problem to a separate contractor and leave you coordinating two different companies.

## Local Note

One pattern we see consistently in Ballwin's 1970s-era split-level and ranch homes: the original roof-to-wall flashing at dormers and chimney bases is often galvanized steel that has corroded to the point where a moderate wind event is enough to lift it. The shingles nearby can look intact from the street, but the flashing failure is where water actually enters — typically showing up first as a stain on an interior ceiling well away from where the breach actually is. If your home was built before 1985 and you've had any recent storm activity, that flashing deserves a close look even if your shingles appear undamaged.
