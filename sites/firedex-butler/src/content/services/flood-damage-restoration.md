## What happens in the first 24 hours matters more than most homeowners realize

Floodwater doesn't just wet surfaces — it migrates. Within hours of a basement flooding or a storm surge pushing through a foundation wall, water wicks into drywall, travels along floor joists, and saturates insulation that will hold moisture for weeks if left alone. The visible puddle on the floor is rarely the whole story. What's hiding inside wall cavities, beneath hardwood, and under slab-on-grade flooring is what determines whether a home dries out cleanly or develops a secondary mold problem that costs more to fix than the flood itself.

## What flood damage restoration actually involves

Flood cleanup is not pumping out water and running a fan. A proper post-flood restoration starts with identifying the water category — groundwater and sewage-contaminated floodwater are Category 3 (grossly contaminated) and require a materially different response than a clean-water pipe burst. Category 3 water means affected porous materials like drywall, carpet, and insulation typically cannot be dried in place; they have to come out.

Once contaminated materials are removed, the structural drying phase begins. This involves industrial-grade truck-mount and portable extractors to pull standing water, followed by a calculated array of low-grain refrigerant (LGR) dehumidifiers and high-velocity air movers positioned to create a drying chamber around affected assemblies. Technicians use thermal hygrometers and penetrating moisture meters to establish a drying goal — a target moisture content based on unaffected reference materials in the same structure — and return daily to log readings and adjust equipment placement. Drying a flooded home typically takes three to five days under active equipment; structures with concrete block foundations or brick veneer common in older Western Pennsylvania housing stock can run longer.

For homes with finished basements — which describes a large share of the residential inventory in Cranberry Township and the surrounding townships — flood restoration also means evaluating whether the vapor barrier behind the drywall is intact, whether the sump system failed or was overwhelmed, and whether the HVAC system pulled contaminated air into the ductwork during the event.

## Our process

1. **Emergency water extraction and safety assessment.** The moment we arrive, standing water comes out. We use truck-mount extractors for volume removal and follow with portable units to reach tight spaces — under stairs, inside wall cavities accessed through flood cuts, beneath floating floor systems. Simultaneously, we assess structural safety and identify electrical hazards before any equipment goes down.

2. **Water category and contamination classification.** We determine whether the source was clean water, gray water, or Category 3 floodwater. For storm flooding and sewer backups — both common in the Cranberry Township area during heavy rain events that overwhelm municipal storm infrastructure — we treat the loss as Category 3 by default until testing or source confirmation says otherwise. This classification drives every downstream decision about what materials stay and what goes.

3. **Controlled demolition and materials removal.** Contaminated drywall, insulation, carpet, and pad are removed to clean structural lines. We perform flood cuts at a consistent height above the waterline to expose wall cavities for drying, document all removed materials with photographs and itemized logs for your insurance adjuster, and bag and remove debris according to applicable disposal requirements.

4. **Structural drying with daily moisture monitoring.** LGR dehumidifiers and air movers are set to a calculated drying configuration — not just placed randomly. Each day, a technician returns to log moisture readings at every monitoring point, adjusts equipment as assemblies dry, and documents progress. We do not pull equipment until readings reach the established drying goal, not just until things feel dry to the touch.

5. **Post-drying inspection and clearance documentation.** Before reconstruction begins, we conduct a final moisture survey across all affected assemblies and provide a written drying report. This documentation is what your insurance carrier and any future buyer's home inspector will want to see — it proves the structure was dried to standard, not just dried enough.

## What separates a good flood response from a bad one

The most common mistake in residential flood cleanup is pulling equipment too early. A basement that reads dry at the surface can still carry elevated moisture in the bottom plate, the subfloor, or the concrete block wall behind the framing. Reconstruction over wet framing traps moisture and produces exactly the mold problem the remediation was supposed to prevent.

A second common failure is misclassifying the water source. Homeowners and less-experienced operators sometimes treat a storm flooding event as clean water because the basement "just filled up from the window well." Groundwater that has traveled through soil, picked up lawn chemicals, and entered through a foundation crack is not clean water. Treating it as such means skipping the antimicrobial application and material removal steps that prevent microbial growth.

Insurance adjusters reviewing flood claims look for a moisture log with daily readings, a clear materials removal inventory with photographs, and a drying report that shows the structure reached goal. Claims that lack this documentation are harder to close and more likely to result in disputes over scope.
