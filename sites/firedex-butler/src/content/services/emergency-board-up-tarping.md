A missing window, a collapsed section of roof, or a fire-weakened wall isn't just an eyesore. It's an open door. Within hours, wind-driven rain soaks insulation and drywall, raccoons and birds find their way into exposed attic space, and an unsecured property becomes an easy target. Emergency board-up and tarping stops that second wave of damage while the real repair plan gets sorted out with your insurance adjuster.

## What Emergency Board-Up and Tarping actually involves

This is temporary, structural-grade securing of a damaged property, not a patch job with scrap plywood. For openings, that means exterior-grade plywood cut to the exact dimensions of the window, door, or wall breach, fastened into the framing (not just the siding) so it holds against wind load. For roofs, that means industrial polyethylene tarping, typically 6 mil or heavier, mechanically fastened with furring strips and screws rather than staples or nails alone, so it survives wind and doesn't tear loose in the next storm.

We handle broken window board up after break-ins or storm impact, roof tarping after wind or fire damage burns through decking, and fire damage board up on walls and openings where structural members are exposed. Most jobs are completed same-day once a crew is on site, because the priority is sealing the structure before weather or darkness makes it worse.

## Our process

1. **Hazard check first.** Before anyone climbs a roof or touches a damaged wall, we check for compromised framing, electrical hazards, and loose debris that could fall. A board-up crew that skips this step is the crew that gets someone hurt.
2. **Measure and cut on site.** Openings from fire, storm, or forced entry are rarely square. We measure each gap individually and cut plywood or OSB to fit tight, rather than forcing a stock panel that leaves gaps for water or pests.
3. **Fasten into structural framing.** Board-up panels get screwed into studs, headers, or rafters, not just surface material. On roofs, tarps are run past the damaged area onto solid decking and fastened with batten strips so wind can't get underneath and peel it.
4. **Seal the seams.** Tarp edges and board-up perimeters get sealed against wind-driven rain. This is the step that separates a tarp that lasts through a storm from one that's flapping loose by morning.
5. **Document for the claim.** Every board and tarp placement is photographed before and after, with measurements and materials noted, so the work supports your insurance file instead of becoming a question mark on it.

## What separates a good board-up and tarping response from a bad one

The most common failure is fastening into the wrong material. Plywood screwed into vinyl siding or into char-damaged framing will hold for a day, then fail in wind. A thorough crew checks what's actually structurally sound underneath before fastening anything.

The second common failure is undersized tarping. A tarp cut to the exact size of the hole leaves no margin for wind lift or water runoff; it needs to extend well past the damaged area and tie down to solid roof deck on all sides. Adjusters also look for whether the work was photographed and measured at the time of mitigation, since that documentation is what justifies the line item on the claim. Sloppy or missing documentation is one of the more common reasons a mitigation invoice gets questioned later.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Western Pennsylvania winters bring heavy, wet snow loads and freeze-thaw cycles that stress any exposed roof deck, so tarps need to be fastened to handle weight, not just water. In the warmer months, Cranberry Township sees fast-moving summer storms with high wind gusts, which is when loose or undersized tarping fails fastest. Older housing stock in the area, especially homes built before plywood sheathing was standard, sometimes has roof decking that needs extra support before a tarp can be properly secured.
