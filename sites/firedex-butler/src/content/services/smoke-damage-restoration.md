## What smoke damage restoration actually involves

Three days after a kitchen fire is extinguished, a homeowner in Cranberry Township opens a closet two rooms away and finds their winter coats smell like charred wood. That's not a coincidence — smoke travels through HVAC ducts, migrates through wall cavities, and deposits acidic soot on surfaces that never saw a flame. The visible char is the easy part. The invisible residue, the odor locked into porous materials, and the ongoing chemical damage to metals and fabrics are what smoke damage restoration is actually about.

Smoke residue is not just dirty — it's corrosive. Within hours of a fire, dry soot begins etching chrome fixtures, tarnishing copper, and yellowing plastics. Within days, wet smoke residue (common in low-heat, smoldering fires) penetrates drywall and wood grain deeply enough that surface cleaning alone won't remove it. Restoration work on a smoke-damaged home involves identifying the type of residue present, selecting the correct cleaning chemistry, deodorizing at the molecular level, and verifying that odor-causing compounds are gone — not just masked.

The timeline from fire to completed smoke restoration typically runs 3 to 10 days depending on the size of the affected area, the soot type, and whether contents need to be packed out for off-site cleaning. Structural materials like drywall, insulation, and subfloor sheathing that cannot be cleaned are removed and replaced.

## Our process

**1. Soot characterization and scope mapping**
Before any cleaning begins, the type of soot present is identified. Dry smoke residue from fast, high-temperature fires (wood, paper) behaves differently than wet smoke residue from slow, low-oxygen smoldering (upholstery, synthetic materials). Protein residue from kitchen fires — grease and vaporized organic matter — is nearly invisible but produces some of the most persistent odor. The wrong cleaning method on the wrong residue type can drive it deeper into the substrate. We document affected zones room by room, photograph residue patterns, and note HVAC contamination pathways before touching a surface.

**2. Containment and HVAC isolation**
Smoke residue that has settled can be re-suspended by air movement. Before cleaning begins, the HVAC system is isolated or shut down to prevent cross-contamination. Affected areas are contained from clean zones using poly barriers and negative air pressure where needed. This step is frequently skipped by less-experienced operators, which is how soot ends up in bedrooms that were untouched by the original fire.

**3. Dry soot removal and surface cleaning**
Dry soot is removed first using HEPA-filtered vacuums and dry chemical sponges — never wet wiped first, which smears residue and sets stains. Once loose particulate is removed, surfaces are cleaned with chemistry matched to the residue type: alkaline cleaners for protein and wet smoke, dry-cleaning solvents for certain synthetic residues. Ceilings are worked before walls, walls before floors. Contents that can be restored are inventoried and either cleaned on-site or packed out.

**4. Structural deodorization**
Surface cleaning removes visible residue, but odor molecules embedded in drywall, wood framing, and insulation require a different approach. Thermal fogging — a process that uses heated solvent to generate a fog that penetrates the same pathways smoke traveled — neutralizes odor compounds at the molecular level rather than masking them. Hydroxyl generators or ozone treatment may be used for deep structural deodorization in unoccupied spaces. Air scrubbers with activated carbon filtration run continuously throughout this phase.

**5. Post-cleaning verification and documentation**
Restored surfaces are inspected under controlled lighting for residue, and odor levels are assessed before containment is removed. All work is documented with before-and-after photographs, a written scope of work, and a materials inventory — the same documentation your insurance adjuster will need to process the claim.

## What separates a good smoke damage response from a bad one

The most common failure in smoke cleanup is treating it like a cleaning job rather than a restoration job. Painting over soot without proper surface preparation causes bleed-through within weeks. Wiping wet smoke residue with a general-purpose cleaner spreads it. Running the HVAC system during cleanup distributes fine particulate throughout the home.

Insurance adjusters reviewing smoke damage claims look for a documented scope that distinguishes between restorable and non-restorable materials, line-item pricing for cleaning chemistry and equipment, and evidence that deodorization was performed — not just noted in the estimate. A restoration company that can provide that documentation speeds up your claim. One that can't may leave you disputing a settlement.

Protein smoke residue from kitchen fires is the most frequently underestimated. It leaves almost no visible soot but penetrates painted surfaces and produces odor that returns months later if the underlying residue isn't removed. Identifying it requires knowing where to look and what chemistry to use.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Western Pennsylvania's winters affect smoke damage response in a few ways. Cold temperatures slow the off-gassing of smoke odor compounds, which can make a home feel acceptable in January and noticeably smoky again in April when the house warms up. Heating season also means fires are more likely to involve fuel oil or natural gas appliances, which produce residue with different chemical profiles than wood fires. Wildfire smoke cleanup has become a seasonal concern in the region as well — fine particulate from distant fires enters homes through HVAC systems and settles on surfaces in ways that require the same residue-identification approach as a structural fire, even when there was no fire on the property.
