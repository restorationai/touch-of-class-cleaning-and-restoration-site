## Why Evans City Properties See Frozen Pipe Damage

The geography here matters. The borough sits low in the Breakneck Creek valley, and that topography does two things: it channels cold air down from the surrounding ridges on still winter nights, and it means many basements and crawl spaces are partially below grade on the creek-facing side. Older homes along and off Evans City Main Street were built with block or stone foundations that wick cold directly into the structure. Supply lines — especially those serving exterior hose bibs, kitchen sinks on outside walls, and older galvanized runs in unheated basements — can freeze even when interior temperatures seem adequate.

Out in Forward Township, the pattern shifts slightly. Farm properties and outbuildings often have separate well systems and pressure tanks housed in structures that go unheated for days at a time. A frozen well line or a burst pressure tank fitting in a barn can go unnoticed until the main house loses water pressure — by which point significant damage may already be underway.

The housing stock throughout the 16033 ZIP code is predominantly pre-1950 construction. That means original galvanized or early copper supply lines, undersized pipe diameters, and routing decisions that made sense when homes were heated with coal but leave cold pockets now. These pipes don't always burst dramatically; they split along a seam or crack at a fitting and drip for hours inside a wall cavity.

## Our Frozen Pipe Restoration Process in Evans City

The first priority on arrival is stopping active water intrusion — locating the shutoff, confirming the pipe has fully thawed, and assessing whether the break is isolated or part of a longer run that may have additional weak points. In older Evans City homes, shutoffs are often original gate valves in the basement that haven't been turned in decades; we carry the tools to work around a stuck valve without making the situation worse.

Once the water source is controlled, the focus shifts to extraction and documentation. We use thermal imaging cameras to trace moisture migration inside wall cavities and under flooring — critical in plaster-and-lath construction, where water can travel several feet from the break point before showing any visible staining. Moisture readings are logged at each measurement point to establish a drying baseline and support the insurance claim.

Drying in these older structures takes longer than in modern drywall construction. Plaster releases absorbed moisture slowly, and stone or block foundation walls hold cold that slows evaporation. We size the drying equipment — desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers and directed airflow — to the actual conditions in the space rather than applying a one-size formula.
