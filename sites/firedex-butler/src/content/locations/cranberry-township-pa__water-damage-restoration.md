## Why Cranberry Township Properties See Water Damage More Than You'd Expect

Butler County's fastest-growing township sits at the convergence of I-79 and the Pennsylvania Turnpike, which means its housing stock spans six decades of construction styles — and each era carries its own failure points.

The older Fernway plats, laid out in the 1960s, still carry original galvanized steel supply lines in many homes. Galvanized pipe corrodes from the inside out; by the time a pinhole leak appears in a wall cavity, the surrounding framing has often been wet for weeks. ## Our Water Damage Restoration Process in Cranberry Township

Every job starts with moisture mapping, not guesswork. We use thermal imaging cameras and calibrated moisture meters to trace water migration through wall cavities, under flooring, and into structural members before a single piece of equipment is placed. In Cranberry Township's newer construction, water from a second-floor bathroom failure can travel laterally through engineered-wood subfloor for 15 to 20 feet before dropping — finding the full boundary of the loss on day one prevents callbacks and secondary mold growth later.

Once the scope is documented, high-capacity truck-mounted extraction removes standing water from carpet, pad, and hard flooring. Structural drying follows: industrial desiccant and refrigerant dehumidifiers pull moisture vapor out of the air while high-velocity air movers accelerate evaporation from framing, drywall, and concrete. Drying targets are set to pre-loss moisture baselines and verified with daily readings — we don't pull equipment because a surface feels dry to the touch; we pull it when the numbers confirm it.

For Fernway-area homes with older plaster construction, drying timelines run longer than modern drywall. Plaster releases absorbed moisture slowly, and rushing the process leads to hidden residual dampness that feeds mold within days of job closure.
