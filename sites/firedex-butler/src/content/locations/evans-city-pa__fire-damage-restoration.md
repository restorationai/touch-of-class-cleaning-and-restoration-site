## Why Evans City Properties See Elevated Fire Damage Risk

The borough's building stock tells the story. The majority of homes in Evans City Borough and the surrounding Forward Township were constructed between roughly 1900 and 1950, a period when balloon framing was standard and insulation was minimal or absent. When fire enters those wall cavities, it channels upward rapidly, and char damage to structural members is often discovered well above the room of origin once drywall is opened. Stone and block foundations — common on the older blocks near Breakneck Creek — also complicate restoration: they absorb smoke odor into porous mortar joints, and that odor can resurface months later if the masonry isn't treated directly rather than just the finished surfaces above it.

Forward Township farm properties add another layer. Outbuildings — pole barns, equipment sheds, detached garages — frequently lack the same fire-separation standards as primary dwellings, and a fire that starts in a shop can expose the main house to radiant heat and windborne embers before the fire department arrives. Well systems on those properties also affect our water supply planning during cleanup, since municipal pressure isn't available for our equipment.

## Our Fire Damage Restoration Process in Evans City

When we arrive at a fire-damaged property, the first priority is a structural safety assessment — no restoration work begins until we know the building is safe to enter. From there, the process moves through board-up and tarping to secure the structure, then into controlled demolition of unsalvageable materials. In Evans City's older homes, that demolition phase often reveals surprises: original horsehair plaster, lead-based paint on woodwork, or asbestos-containing floor tile beneath linoleum. We flag those materials and handle them according to Pennsylvania DEP requirements before any reconstruction begins.

Smoke and soot removal in pre-1950 homes requires more than surface wiping. Dry soot from fast-burning fires behaves differently than the wet, oily residue from a slow smolder, and both types penetrate porous materials — plaster, brick, unfinished wood — more deeply than they do modern drywall. We use thermal fogging and hydroxyl generation to address odor at the molecular level in the air and structural cavities, not just on visible surfaces. Dehumidification runs concurrently, because firefighting water soaks into wood framing and subfloors and will promote mold growth within days if it isn't extracted and dried.
