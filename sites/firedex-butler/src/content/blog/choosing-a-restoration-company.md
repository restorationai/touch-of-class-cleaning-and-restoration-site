Choosing the wrong restoration company after a flood, fire, or mold discovery can cost you more than the original damage. Fly-by-night contractors, inflated scopes, and shoddy drying jobs are real problems in this industry — and when you're stressed, displaced, and dealing with an insurance claim, it's easy to sign something you'll regret. The short answer: verify credentials before anyone touches your property, get the scope of work in writing, and never let urgency replace due diligence. The sections below walk you through exactly how to do that.

## Why the Restoration Industry Attracts Bad Actors

Restoration work is largely invisible. A contractor can claim they dried your subfloor, pulled the mold, or neutralized smoke odor — and unless you know what to look for, you have no way to verify it until months later when the smell comes back or the floor buckles.

After a major weather event or a burst pipe in a neighborhood, "storm chasers" — contractors who follow disasters from market to market — will knock on doors within hours. They often ask homeowners to sign an Assignment of Benefits (AOB) form, which transfers your insurance claim rights directly to them. Once you sign, you lose control of the claim. Some legitimate companies use AOB arrangements, but you should never sign one under pressure or without reading it.

The Cranberry Township and broader Butler County area sees its share of basement flooding from heavy spring rains and the freeze-thaw cycles that crack foundation walls every winter. That seasonal pattern means local demand for water damage restoration spikes predictably — and so does the opportunity for shortcuts.

## The Five Things to Check Before You Hire Anyone

These aren't nice-to-haves. Each one filters out a different category of problem.

1. **IICRC S500/S520 standards.** The Institute of Inspection, Cleaning and Restoration Certification (IICRC) publishes the S500 water-damage and S520 mold standards the industry runs on — the S500 for water damage, the S520 for mold remediation, the S700 for fire and smoke. Ask specifically how the *technicians who will be on your job* are trained against those S500 and S520 standards — not just whether the company name carries a membership logo. A membership badge and hands-on training to the standard are not the same thing.

2. **Verifiable local presence.** A physical address you can look up, a phone number that reaches a human, and reviews that span more than a few months. A company that appeared six weeks ago with dozens of glowing ratings and nothing critical anywhere is a warning sign. Look for patterns in the review text — real reviews mention specific details (the crew lead's name, the equipment used, how long the job took).

3. **Proof of insurance.** Ask for a certificate of insurance showing general liability and workers' compensation. If a technician is injured in your home and the company carries no workers' comp, you may be liable. A legitimate company will provide this without hesitation.

4. **A written scope of work before work begins.** This document should list the affected areas, the equipment being deployed, the drying standard they're working toward, and the timeline for moisture readings. Verbal promises are unenforceable. "We'll take care of everything" is not a scope of work.

5. **Direct insurance billing experience.** This matters because the restoration company and your insurance adjuster will need to agree on the scope. A company that routinely works with carriers knows how to document losses in a format adjusters accept — photographs, moisture logs, equipment placement records. If they can't describe that process clearly, they may leave you holding a disputed claim.

## Red Flags During the Estimate and Inspection

The estimate visit tells you a lot about how a company operates.

- **They don't use moisture meters or thermal imaging.** Water damage behind drywall or under flooring is invisible to the naked eye. If a contractor is quoting a water damage job without instruments, they're guessing — and they'll either miss affected areas or inflate the scope.
- **They push you to sign immediately.** Legitimate contractors understand you need time to review documents. High-pressure tactics — "we can only hold this price for two hours," "your insurance company will deny the claim if we don't start tonight" — are manipulation, not urgency.
- **The quote is dramatically lower than others.** In restoration, a low bid often means a shorter drying time, fewer dehumidifiers, or skipped containment for mold work. Cutting corners on a mold remediation job, for example, can spread spores to unaffected areas of the house.
- **They can't explain their process.** Ask: "How will you know when the structure is dry enough to close up?" A trained technician will reference target moisture content levels and daily monitoring. If the answer is vague, the work will be too.

## What to Do While You're Still in Research Mode

If you're reading this before an emergency — good. File these steps away.

If you're reading this because something just happened:

1. **Stop the source first.** For water damage, find the main shutoff valve (usually in the utility room or crawl space) and turn it off. For a fire, don't re-enter until the fire department clears the structure.
2. **Document everything before anyone touches it.** Photos and video of every affected surface, from multiple angles. This is your evidence for the insurance claim and your protection if a contractor later disputes what the original damage looked like.
3. **Call your insurance company to open a claim.** You are not obligated to use a contractor your insurance company recommends, but you should notify them before major work begins. Most policies require it.
4. **Get at least two estimates.** Even under time pressure, a few hours spent comparing scopes can save you thousands and protect your claim.

For mold specifically: if you're seeing discoloration on drywall after a slow leak, or smelling something musty in a finished basement, resist the urge to scrub it with bleach and call it done. Surface cleaning doesn't address mold that has colonized inside wall cavities — and disturbing a mold colony without containment can spread it. A proper mold remediation job involves containment barriers, air scrubbers with HEPA filtration, and post-remediation verification testing.

## The Longer Recovery: What Happens After the Crew Leaves

Restoration isn't finished when the equipment gets picked up. Here's what the follow-through looks like on a legitimate job:

- **Moisture documentation.** You should receive a final moisture report showing readings at or below the target levels for your material types. Keep this document. If you sell the house or a problem recurs, it's your proof the job was done correctly.
- **Reconstruction coordination.** Many restoration companies also handle the rebuild — replacing drywall, flooring, cabinetry — or they work with a general contractor. Clarify this before the mitigation phase starts so there's no gap between drying and rebuilding.
- **Supplement claims.** Adjusters sometimes miss items in their initial estimate. A restoration company experienced in insurance work will identify and document supplemental items and submit them to your carrier. Ask whether they do this as part of their service.
- **Post-remediation testing for mold.** After mold remediation, an independent industrial hygienist (not the same company that did the remediation) should conduct clearance testing. This is the only way to verify the work was effective.
