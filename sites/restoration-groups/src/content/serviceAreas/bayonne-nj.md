## Restoration emergencies common in Bayonne

Bayonne sits on a narrow peninsula pinched between Newark Bay to the west and the Kill Van Kull to the south, and that geography shapes almost every restoration call we receive here. The waterfront and Constable Hook neighborhoods are directly storm-surge exposed — the flooding that followed Sandy in 2012 still informs how low-lying blocks dry out and how long residual moisture lingers in slab-on-grade slabs and finished basements. The water table is shallow enough that a slow foundation crack can become a standing-water problem within hours of a heavy rain.

The housing stock compounds the risk. Bayonne's dense grid of early 20th-century two- and three-family frame homes and brick rowhomes — concentrated through Midtown and Downtown Bayonne — were built with cast-iron drain stacks and original galvanized supply lines that are now well past their service life. Failures tend to be quiet and slow: a pinhole leak behind a bathroom wall, a corroded drain coupling under a kitchen stack, a sump that loses power during a nor'easter. By the time a tenant notices the smell or the ceiling stain, mold has had the 24–48 hours it needs to begin colonizing drywall paper. Uptown's postwar capes and split-levels add finished basement losses to the mix — carpeted recreation rooms and storage areas that absorb water silently until the damage is extensive.

On the commercial side, the Bayonne Crossing shopping center and the Route 440 corridor generate their own pattern of losses: overnight roof leaks into retail interiors, sprinkler activations in warehouse bays, and storm-driven façade water intrusion that soaks insulation and framing before anyone opens for business in the morning.
