When a water, fire, or storm claim moves from "the damage is stopped" to "now what," reconstruction is the phase that turns a gutted, tarped, or stripped-down structure back into a livable home or functioning building. This is the work that happens after extraction crews pull out, after demolition exposes studs and subfloor, after the insurance adjuster has documented the loss. It covers everything from replacing a single section of drywall and flooring to full structural rebuilds following a fire or major flood, and it's where a project either gets closed out cleanly or drags on for months over mismatched permits, missed code requirements, and finish work that doesn't match the rest of the house.

## What reconstruction services actually involves

Reconstruction picks up wherever mitigation leaves off. In a lot of cases that means framing repairs where fire or structural water damage compromised joists, studs, or subfloor; drywall and insulation replacement; flooring, cabinetry, and trim work; and painting and finish carpentry to match existing materials. On larger losses, it can mean full room or addition rebuilds, roof and exterior envelope repair, or bringing older Kenilworth housing stock, much of it early-to-mid-20th-century construction, up to current code during the repair rather than just patching it back the way it was. Timelines vary widely: a single-room drywall and flooring job might run one to two weeks, while a fire-damaged structure with load-bearing repairs and permitting can take several months from scope approval to final walkthrough.

## Our process

1. **Scope of work and documentation.** We assess what mitigation and demolition left behind, measure affected areas, and write a detailed scope that itemizes materials, labor, and sequencing, typically cross-referenced against the insurance estimate.
2. **Permits and code review.** Structural repairs, electrical, plumbing, and anything touching load-bearing elements get pulled through the local permitting process before work starts, not after.
3. **Framing and rough-in.** Damaged structural members are sistered or replaced, electrical and plumbing rough-in is corrected or restored, and the space is inspected before it gets closed up.
4. **Insulation, drywall, and finish work.** Walls and ceilings go back up to match existing textures and profiles, flooring and trim are installed, and painting is matched to adjoining rooms rather than treated as an isolated patch.
5. **Final walkthrough and punch list.** We walk the job with the homeowner or property manager against the original scope, closing out any remaining items before calling the project complete.

## What separates a good reconstruction response from a bad one

The most common failure point isn't the visible finish work, it's what happens before the walls close up. Framing that gets covered without a code inspection, moisture readings that weren't confirmed dry before drywall goes back, or electrical rough-in that doesn't match current code are the things that turn into disputes later, either with an inspector or with the insurance carrier. Adjusters reviewing a reconstruction claim are typically looking for an itemized scope that matches their estimate line by line, photo documentation of structural repairs before they're covered, and permit records for anything that required one. A rebuild that skips the paperwork to move faster usually costs more time in the end, either through failed inspections or a claim that gets kicked back for missing documentation.

## What does reconstruction services cost?

Reconstruction costs depend almost entirely on scope: how much demolition preceded it, whether structural elements need replacement, and how much of the finish work has to match existing materials. Every loss is different, and The Restoration Group provides a written scope of work with itemized pricing before any rebuild work begins. Homeowners insurance typically covers reconstruction tied to a covered peril, such as a burst pipe or fire, though code-upgrade costs and some finish-level choices may fall outside the policy and are worth confirming with the adjuster early.

| Scenario | Typical range |
|---|---|

| Whole-structure rebuild after major fire or flood | $75,000 and up |

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Kenilworth's housing stock is largely early-to-mid-20th-century construction, which means reconstruction jobs here often turn up older framing dimensions, knob-and-tube remnants, or plaster-and-lath walls that don't match modern drywall assemblies without some adaptation. Winter rebuilds can be slowed by curing times for compounds and paints in unheated spaces, so interior reconstruction scheduled through the colder months sometimes needs supplemental heat to stay on timeline.
