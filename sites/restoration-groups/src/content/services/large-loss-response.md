A single-family water loss and a 40-unit apartment fire are not the same problem scaled up. Large loss and catastrophic response applies when damage spans multiple units, multiple structures, or crosses into six and seven figures of reconstruction cost, and when the building has to keep functioning (or get tenants back in) while the work happens. That means multiple drying crews running in parallel, a general contractor on standby for structural repairs, and documentation built from day one to survive scrutiny from a large loss adjuster or third-party administrator.

## What large loss and catastrophic response actually involves

A catastrophic loss rarely has one cause or one affected area. A burst main in a mixed-use building can hit a ground-floor retail space, the units above it, and shared mechanical rooms all at once. A commercial fire can leave smoke damage in sections of a structure the flames never reached. Large loss response treats the property as a single project with multiple fronts: structural stabilization, water or fire mitigation, content handling, and reconstruction scheduling, all tracked against one scope of work instead of several disconnected ones.

The work typically runs longer than a standard residential claim, sometimes weeks instead of days, and it requires equipment deployed at a different scale: trailer-mounted desiccant dehumidifiers instead of a handful of box fans, generator power when utilities are compromised, and enough extraction and air-moving equipment to cover tens of thousands of square feet without pulling resources off other active jobs.

## Our process

1. **Rapid mobilization and site assessment.** Crews are deployed to assess the full footprint of the damage, not just the most visible area, before any equipment goes in. Because The Restoration Group operates 24/7, that initial assessment can start the moment the loss is reported.
2. **Scope documentation for the carrier or TPA.** Detailed photos, moisture readings, and line-item estimates are compiled in a format large loss adjusters expect, so the claim moves without repeated requests for missing information.
3. **Structural stabilization.** Board-up, temporary power, roof tarping, and shoring happen before mitigation equipment is set, protecting the building envelope and preventing secondary damage while the larger plan is finalized.
4. **Multi-zone mitigation.** Extraction, drying, or soot and smoke removal runs across every affected area simultaneously, with moisture logs and air quality checks tracked by zone so progress is verifiable, not estimated.
5. **Reconstruction handoff.** Once the structure is dry and stable, reconstruction scope, permitting, and phased rebuild work are coordinated so the property owner isn't managing multiple separate contractors for one loss.

## What separates a good large loss response from a bad one

The most common failure on catastrophic losses isn't a missed repair, it's a documentation gap. When moisture readings, photos, and equipment logs aren't tracked by zone from day one, adjusters have no way to verify drying progress, and payment stalls while the building sits wet. A good large loss response treats documentation as part of the mitigation work, not paperwork that happens afterward.

The second common mistake is understaffing. A crew sized for a single-family water loss cannot dry a commercial building or multi-unit property on the same timeline, and delayed drying on a large footprint means a much higher risk of secondary mold growth across multiple units before remediation even starts. Scaling crew size and equipment to the actual square footage, not the first 24 hours of visible damage, is what keeps a catastrophic loss from turning into a second, mold-driven claim.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Union County's mix of older multi-family buildings, aging commercial strips, and industrial properties means catastrophic losses here often involve shared mechanical systems, common walls, and plumbing stacks that affect multiple units from a single failure point.
