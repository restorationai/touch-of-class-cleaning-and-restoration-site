If you've noticed a musty smell coming up through your floor vents, insulation sagging and falling away from the subfloor, or a spike in humidity on the first floor that your HVAC can't seem to fix, the source is often sitting a few feet below your living space. Vented crawl spaces in older Kenilworth and Union County homes pull in warm, humid outside air all summer, which condenses on cooler floor joists and ductwork. Left alone, that moisture cycle rots wood, invites mold, and feeds pests. Encapsulation seals the space off from that exchange entirely.

## What Crawl Space Encapsulation actually involves

Encapsulation means lining the crawl space floor, walls, and sometimes the ceiling with a heavy-gauge vapor barrier (typically 10 to 20 mil reinforced polyethylene), sealing all vents and penetrations, and conditioning the space so it behaves like part of the building envelope rather than a vented crawl exposed to outside air. Depending on the home's moisture load and grading, that can include a sump pump or French drain for bulk water, a dehumidifier sized to the cubic footage of the space, and spray foam or rigid insulation at the rim joist. For an average single-family crawl space in this area, the work typically runs one to three days once access, framing condition, and moisture source are confirmed.

## Our process

1. **Moisture and structural assessment.** We check for standing water, active leaks, wood rot, and existing mold before anything gets sealed in. A moisture meter reading on joists and subfloor tells us whether we're dealing with humidity alone or a bulk water source that needs addressing first.
2. **Source correction.** If grading, a downspout, or a plumbing leak is feeding the problem, that gets fixed before encapsulation, not after. Sealing moisture inside an unresolved leak just traps it.
3. **Vapor barrier installation.** The liner is fitted to the floor and up the foundation walls, seams taped, and the material mechanically fastened or adhered at the walls so it doesn't shift or tear over time.
4. **Vent and penetration sealing.** Foundation vents, old duct penetrations, and gaps around pipes are sealed so the space is no longer exchanging air with the outside.
5. **Dehumidification and verification.** A dehumidifier is installed and sized to the space, and we verify relative humidity has dropped to a stable range before closing out the job.

## What separates a good Crawl Space Encapsulation response from a bad one

The most common shortcut is installing a vapor barrier without addressing the actual moisture source, whether that's a grading issue, a cracked foundation wall, or an undersized sump pump. The liner traps that moisture instead of stopping it, and homeowners end up with a sealed, humid crawl space that's worse than the vented one they started with. Another frequent miss is skipping the rim joist insulation, which leaves the one place where conditioned and unconditioned air still meet. Adjusters reviewing a related water or mold claim will also want documentation of the moisture reading before and after the work, photos of the sealed vents and seams, and a sump pump that's rated for the actual water volume on-site, not a generic undersized unit.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

New Jersey's humid summers are the main driver of crawl space moisture here, with outside dew points regularly exceeding the temperature of framing members below grade, which is exactly the condition that produces condensation. Winter brings the opposite risk: homes with older, unsealed foundations lose more heat through an open crawl space, which shows up as cold floors and higher heating bills. Encapsulation addresses both ends of that cycle at once, which is part of why it tends to pay for itself over a few heating and cooling seasons rather than just solving a summer odor problem.
