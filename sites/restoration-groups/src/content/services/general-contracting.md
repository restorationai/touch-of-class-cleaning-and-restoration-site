A water or fire loss doesn't end when the drying equipment comes out. Cut-open drywall, mismatched flooring where cabinets used to sit, a bathroom subfloor that failed moisture testing, these are the gaps between "the damage is stabilized" and "the house is livable again." That rebuild phase is general contracting, and it's also where homeowners renovate on purpose: a kitchen that's finally functional, a bathroom that doesn't feel like 1987, a layout that makes sense for how the house is actually used now. Whether the work starts with an insurance claim or a homeowner's own project list, it runs through the same disciplines: scope, permits, framing, and finish work that passes inspection the first time.

## What renovations, remodels and general contracting actually involves

General contracting covers everything from a single-room post-damage rebuild to a full kitchen or bathroom remodel. On the reconstruction side, that means replacing what was removed during mitigation: drywall, insulation, subfloor, trim, cabinetry, and whatever finishes the room had before the loss. On the remodel side, it's homeowner-driven: new layouts, updated plumbing and electrical rough-in, cabinetry, tile, and fixtures. Both tracks require the same sequence of trades (framing, rough mechanicals, insulation, drywall, paint, finish carpentry) and the same attention to local code, because a kitchen remodel that moves plumbing or a rebuild that touches electrical will trigger an inspection regardless of why the work started. Timelines vary widely: a single bathroom rebuild might run two to four weeks, while a full kitchen remodel with a layout change can run six to ten.

## Our process

1. **Scope of work.** Every job starts with a written scope: what's being replaced, what's being upgraded, materials, and a line-item cost breakdown. For insurance-driven rebuilds, this scope is built to match the adjuster's estimate where it's accurate and flagged where it isn't.
2. **Permits and code review.** Kenilworth and surrounding Union County municipalities require permits for structural, electrical, and plumbing work. We pull what's needed before framing starts, not after, so there's no stop-work order mid-project.
3. **Framing and rough mechanicals.** Walls, subfloor, and structural repairs go in first, followed by rough plumbing and electrical where layouts changed. This is also when hidden damage, rot, old wiring, undersized joists, gets addressed before it's closed up again.
4. **Insulation, drywall, and finish carpentry.** Once rough inspections pass, insulation and drywall close the walls, followed by trim, doors, and cabinetry installation.
5. **Final finishes and walkthrough.** Paint, flooring, fixtures, and a final walkthrough against the original scope, so the homeowner is signing off on what was actually agreed to, not what was convenient to install.

## What separates a good general contracting response from a bad one

The most common failure point is skipping or rushing the scope-of-work step, which leads to change orders, cost overruns, and disputes with adjusters over what was actually agreed to. A second common mistake is closing up walls before rough inspections pass, which forces a contractor to cut back open drywall that's already been hung, costing time and material twice. On insurance-driven jobs, adjusters are specifically looking for matching material grades (replacing like-for-kind rather than downgrading), documented permit pulls, and photos of concealed conditions before they're covered over. A contractor who doesn't photograph the open walls, subfloor, and rough mechanicals before closing them up leaves the homeowner with no record if a dispute comes up later. Good general contracting also means flagging problems that weren't part of the original scope, like water-damaged framing discovered mid-demo, instead of building over them.

## What does renovations, remodels and general contracting cost?

Costs vary by scope, square footage, material selection, and whether plumbing or electrical layouts are changing. A post-damage rebuild of a single room tends to track lower than a full remodel because it's restoring to existing conditions rather than upgrading finishes. Every project is different, which is why The Restoration Group provides a written scope of work with line-item pricing before any work begins; for insurance-related rebuilds, homeowners insurance typically covers restoring damaged areas to their pre-loss condition, though upgrades beyond that scope are usually an out-of-pocket addition.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Kenilworth's housing stock is largely mid-20th-century construction, which means remodels and rebuilds often uncover older plumbing, knob-and-tube remnants, or undersized electrical panels that need addressing before new finishes go in. Winter projects can slow exterior-adjacent work (roofing tie-ins, siding repairs tied to interior rebuilds) due to cold-weather material limitations, so timelines built in December through February should account for that. Permit review times through local building departments can also shift project start dates, which is factored into the written scope rather than promised as a fixed date upfront.
