## Why East Orange Properties See Repeated Flood Losses

East Orange's housing stock is one of the densest and oldest in New Jersey. The three-story walk-ups and converted Victorians that line streets in Elmwood and Doddtown were built between roughly 1905 and 1940, when galvanized steel supply lines and flat or low-pitched roofs were standard. Galvanized pipe corrodes from the inside out — it doesn't burst dramatically, it weeps, and that slow leak behind a plaster wall can saturate floor joists for weeks before anyone sees a stain on the ceiling below.

Below grade, the picture is different. Basement mechanical rooms and finished cellars throughout the 07017 and 07018 ZIP codes flood during sustained rain events because the combined sewer infrastructure serving much of the city can't always handle peak flow. During Tropical Storm Ida in 2021, tributaries feeding the Second River pushed water into low-lying streets in the eastern sections of the city, affecting ground-floor units and parking garages that had never flooded before. That event was a reminder that flood risk here isn't limited to buildings near obvious waterways.

The Central Avenue commercial corridor adds another category: sprinkler activations, roof-drain overflows, and overnight pipe failures in mixed-use buildings where a retail space on the ground floor sits directly below residential units. A single event on that corridor can affect multiple tenants, multiple insurance policies, and a landlord who needs coordinated documentation for all of them.

## Our Flood Damage Restoration Process in East Orange

The first priority on any flood call is stopping ongoing intrusion — whether that means identifying the source, coordinating with the building super to shut a main, or sandbagging a window well. Once the water source is controlled, the extraction phase begins. In East Orange's multi-unit buildings, that often means working across more than one unit simultaneously, because water follows gravity through shared floor assemblies.

After extraction, the process follows the IICRC S500 standard for water damage restoration: moisture mapping with thermal imaging and pin meters to trace exactly how far water has migrated into walls, subfloors, and ceiling cavities. In buildings with original plaster-over-lath construction — common throughout Elmwood and Presidential Estates — that mapping step is especially important, because plaster holds moisture differently than drywall and can read as dry on the surface while the lath behind it is still saturated.

Drying equipment — commercial-grade desiccant dehumidifiers and high-velocity air movers — is placed based on the moisture map, not by room count. Daily monitoring adjusts the equipment layout as materials dry. Affected materials that can't be dried in place (saturated insulation, buckled hardwood, compromised subfloor sections) are documented, removed, and logged for the insurance claim before any reconstruction begins. The Restoration Group is an IICRC Certified Firm (#210213) and a licensed NJ Home Improvement Contractor, so the documentation we produce meets carrier requirements.
