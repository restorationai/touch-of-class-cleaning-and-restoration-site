## Why Paterson Properties See Basement Flood Problems

Paterson's relationship with flooding is structural, not just seasonal. The city sits directly on the Passaic River, and its topography funnels stormwater toward the lowest-lying blocks with very little natural drainage buffer. The events of Hurricane Irene in 2011 and then Ida in 2021 put this on full display — streets near the Great Falls National Historical Park and the downtown corridor were submerged for days, and basements in the surrounding blocks took on water that overwhelmed even functioning sump pumps.

Beyond river flooding, the housing stock itself creates vulnerability. Much of Paterson was built during the silk-mill era of the late 19th and early 20th centuries — dense brick rowhouses, two- and three-family frame structures, and converted industrial buildings that were never designed with modern waterproofing in mind. In neighborhoods like Sandy Hill and the Eastside, it's common to find cast-iron drain lines that are decades past their service life, clay-tile lateral connections that crack under soil movement, and basement walls made of rubble stone or unreinforced brick that wick groundwater through the mortar joints. These aren't cosmetic problems — they're the reason a single heavy rain can turn a dry basement into a foot of standing water within hours.

Rental density adds another layer. Paterson has a large stock of occupied multi-family rentals where basement utility spaces are shared, unventilated, and rarely inspected. Slow leaks go unreported for months, and by the time a tenant notices, mold has already colonized the framing behind the walls.

## Our Basement Flood Cleanup Process in Paterson

Every basement flood is different, but the sequence of decisions matters — especially in older Paterson construction where what you see on the surface rarely tells the full story.

We start with moisture mapping using thermal imaging and calibrated meters. In a rubble-stone or brick foundation wall, the surface can feel dry to the touch while moisture is migrating through the masonry several inches deep. Getting that reading right determines how long drying will actually take and prevents a callback weeks later when mold shows up behind a finished wall.

Standing water is extracted with truck-mounted and portable units, then we set drying equipment — industrial air movers and desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers — positioned for the specific geometry of the space. Low-ceiling Paterson basements with concrete block walls and minimal airflow require a different equipment layout than an open-plan finished basement. We document daily psychrometric readings so there's a drying log for your insurance carrier.

If sewage was involved in the backup — which is common when municipal lines surcharge during heavy rain on Paterson's combined sewer system — affected materials are handled as a Category 3 loss. That means contaminated porous materials come out, affected surfaces are treated with EPA-registered antimicrobials, and the area is cleared before any rebuild begins. We're an IICRC Certified Firm (#210213) and follow the S500 standard for water damage and the S520 for mold, so the documentation holds up with adjusters and code inspectors.

## Local Note

In Paterson's older brick multi-family buildings — particularly the three-story walkups common in Sandy Hill and parts of the Eastside — basement walls are often built with lime-mortar joints rather than modern Portland cement. Lime mortar is softer and more permeable, which means it absorbs and releases moisture more slowly than the brick itself. When we're drying these structures, standard drying timelines based on drywall or concrete block don't apply. We extend the monitoring period and adjust dehumidifier placement to account for the slower moisture release from the mortar, rather than pulling equipment the moment the brick surface reads dry. Skipping that step is how mold shows up three weeks after a crew packs out.
