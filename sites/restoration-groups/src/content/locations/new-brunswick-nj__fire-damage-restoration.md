## Why New Brunswick Properties Face Distinct Fire Damage Risks

Balloon-frame construction — common in the rowhouses and multifamily buildings that line streets throughout the Fifth Ward and Edgebrook — lacks the fire-blocking that platform-frame homes built after the 1950s rely on. In a balloon-frame structure, a kitchen fire can push heat and smoke from the first floor to the attic in minutes through continuous stud bays, scorching framing that looks intact from the outside. That hidden damage is easy to miss and dangerous to leave.

Dense student-rental occupancy near Rutgers University adds another layer of complexity. Multi-unit buildings often have shared attic spaces, stacked mechanical chases, and decades of patchwork electrical work — conditions that let a single-unit fire affect two or three neighboring units before the New Brunswick Fire Department arrives. Smoke and soot migrate through shared HVAC returns, coating surfaces in units that never saw a flame.

The George Street business district and the restaurant corridor around downtown present a different profile: commercial suppression systems that discharge thousands of gallons during a kitchen or electrical fire, leaving a combined fire-and-water loss that has to be triaged simultaneously. ## Our Fire Damage Restoration Process in New Brunswick

The first hour on-site is documentation. Before a single piece of debris moves, the crew photographs and measures every affected surface — walls, ceilings, subfloors, HVAC components — to build the scope that your insurance adjuster will rely on. In older New Brunswick buildings, that documentation often turns up secondary damage: smoke-stained joists in an unfinished basement, soot deposits inside cast-iron radiator covers, or char hidden behind original wainscoting.

Once the scope is established, the work moves in sequence:

- **Board-up and tarping** to secure the structure and prevent weather intrusion through compromised windows or roof sections
- **Debris removal and selective demolition** of charred materials, with careful attention to load-bearing elements common in pre-war construction
- **Dry ice blasting or HEPA-filtered soda blasting** on exposed framing to remove soot without saturating wood that is already stressed
- **Thermal fogging and hydroxyl generation** to neutralize smoke odor molecules embedded in porous surfaces — plaster, brick, original hardwood — that standard cleaning cannot reach
- **Structural drying** if suppression water is present, using desiccant dehumidifiers calibrated for the higher moisture loads in dense urban buildings
- **Reconstruction** scoped to match original materials where required by New Brunswick's historic preservation guidelines or the property owner's preference

The IICRC Certified Firm standard (Firm #210213) guides our drying and cleaning protocols throughout.

## Local Note

In New Brunswick's older rowhouse blocks — particularly in the Fifth Ward and along streets that border the Highland Park line — original plaster walls are typically three-coat lime plaster over wood lath, sometimes two inches thick. After a fire, that plaster absorbs smoke odor at a molecular level and releases it slowly for weeks. Standard odor-blocking primer applied over the surface will mask the smell for a few days and then fail as the plaster off-gasses. The correct approach is to remove the plaster to the lath, treat the lath and framing, and rebuild with modern drywall — or, if the owner wants to preserve the original finish, apply a penetrating encapsulant before any topcoat. Skipping that step is the single most common reason a New Brunswick homeowner calls a second contractor six months after a fire was supposedly "finished."
