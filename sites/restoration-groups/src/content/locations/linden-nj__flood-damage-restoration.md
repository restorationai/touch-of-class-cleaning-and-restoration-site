## Our Flood Damage Restoration Process in Linden

The first priority when we arrive is stopping active water migration. Before any drying equipment goes down, we use thermal imaging and moisture meters to map exactly where water has traveled — under hardwood, behind baseboards, inside wall cavities. In Linden's older homes, water routinely travels further than it appears to on the surface because original subfloor materials and plaster lath absorb and channel moisture in ways modern OSB doesn't.

Once the scope is mapped, extraction comes first: truck-mounted and portable units pull standing water from flooring and carpet, followed by targeted structural drying with industrial-grade desiccant and refrigerant dehumidifiers and high-velocity air movers calibrated to the affected square footage. Drying is documented daily with psychrometric readings — moisture content in walls and floors is logged against the IICRC S500 standard until every affected material reaches its dry standard. Nothing gets closed up until the numbers confirm it.

For finished basements — which are nearly universal in Linden's split-levels and colonials — we assess whether flooring, insulation, and lower wall sections can be dried in place or need to be removed. Wet fiberglass batt insulation holds moisture for weeks and becomes a mold substrate; we don't leave it in the wall hoping it will dry on its own.

## Linden Insurance Coordination

Flood losses in Linden often involve both homeowners insurance (for sudden and accidental discharge) and NFIP flood policies (for rising water events tied to the Rahway River or storm surge). The distinction matters because documentation requirements differ — NFIP adjusters want specific photo evidence of water entry points and affected elevations, while standard HO carriers focus on scope and unit pricing. We photograph and document losses to satisfy both, and we work directly with adjusters to submit moisture logs, equipment records, and scope reports so you're not translating technical data on your own.

In Linden's postwar split-levels, the intermediate floor level — the "lower level" that sits partially below grade — is often the first space to flood and the last to dry. Because that level typically has concrete block walls rather than poured concrete, water infiltrates through the mortar joints and the block face itself, not just through obvious cracks. Homeowners sometimes see the visible water stop and assume the problem is resolved, but the block continues to release moisture into the air for days.
