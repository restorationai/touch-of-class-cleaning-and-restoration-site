## Why East Brunswick Commercial Properties Face Distinct Restoration Risks

East Brunswick's commercial landscape sits at an unusual intersection of postwar infrastructure and modern retail density. The Route 18 corridor packs restaurants, multi-tenant retail, and service businesses into buildings that range from 1960s construction to recent renovations — often with aging suppression systems, original cast-iron drain lines, and flat or low-slope roofs that pool water before it finds the drain. When those systems fail, losses tend to be large-footprint: a single discharge event can saturate 3,000 square feet of finished retail space before the monitoring system triggers an alert.

The Lawrence Brook watershed compounds the risk for commercial properties in lower-lying sections of town. Businesses near the Farrington Lake drainage corridor have experienced repeated flood intrusion — not just from major named storms, but from the kind of sustained overnight rainfall that overwhelms storm infrastructure in a built-out suburb. Concrete slab-on-grade construction in older commercial strips holds moisture longer than it appears to on the surface, and moisture meters routinely read elevated readings days after the visible water is gone.

Multi-unit commercial condo associations — common in the Village of the Golden Triangle and similar mixed-use developments — add a coordination layer that purely residential work doesn't require. A water loss originating in one unit's mechanical room can migrate through shared wall cavities into three adjacent tenant spaces, each with a different owner and a different insurance carrier.

## Our Commercial Restoration Process in East Brunswick

Every commercial job starts with a documented scope before a single piece of equipment is staged. We photograph and moisture-map the affected area, identify the loss origin, and produce a written drying plan calibrated to the building's construction type — because a 1970s masonry retail shell dries on a different timeline than a 2010 steel-stud office buildout.

Extraction comes first: truck-mounted and portable units pull standing water from carpet, concrete, and subfloor assemblies. We then deploy commercial-grade desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers and high-velocity air movers in configurations based on the IICRC S500 standard for water damage restoration. Psychrometric readings are logged at each visit so the drying record is defensible for insurance purposes — something adjusters handling large commercial losses require before authorizing final payment.

For fire and smoke losses, structural cleaning and deodorization follow a room-by-room protocol. Smoke residue behaves differently in commercial kitchens — where grease in the HVAC system carries odor compounds far beyond the origin room — than in office or retail spaces. We assess ductwork separately and coordinate with mechanical contractors when cleaning extends into the air-handling system.

## Local Note

One pattern we see repeatedly on Route 18 commercial losses: flat-roof buildings in this corridor were often built with interior roof drains that tie into the same cast-iron stack serving the restrooms. When those stacks corrode — and in 1960s and 1970s construction, they corrode on schedule — a blocked drain during a heavy rain event backs up into the ceiling plenum before anyone notices. By the time water appears at the drop-ceiling tiles, the plenum above has been holding moisture for hours. On these buildings, we always inspect above the tile grid before finalizing the drying scope, because the visible damage understates the actual affected area by a significant margin.
