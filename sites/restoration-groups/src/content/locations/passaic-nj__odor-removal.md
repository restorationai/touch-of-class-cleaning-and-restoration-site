## Why Passaic Properties See Persistent Odor Problems

Passaic is one of the most densely populated small cities in New Jersey, and its housing stock reflects more than a century of continuous use. The two- and three-family frame houses that line the side streets off Main Avenue were built when ventilation meant opening a window. Basements are often unfinished, poorly ventilated, and prone to moisture intrusion — conditions that let mold colonize within 24 to 48 hours of a water event and produce musty odors that embed themselves in concrete block and wood framing.

The flooding Passaic experienced during Hurricane Irene in 2011 and again during Ida in 2021 left riverfront blocks and low-lying properties saturated for days. In many cases, water receded but the odor didn't — organic material trapped behind walls and under flooring continued to off-gas long after surfaces dried. Sewer backups are a separate but related problem: aging cast-iron drain lines in older rental stock fail regularly, and the sulfur and biological compounds from a sewage event penetrate subfloor assemblies and wall cavities in ways that require professional-grade deodorization, not just cleaning.

Smoke odor presents its own challenge in Passaic's attached and semi-attached buildings. A fire in one unit of a multi-family walkup can push smoke residue into adjacent units through shared wall chases, plumbing penetrations, and HVAC pathways. Tenants in units that never saw flames still end up with the acrid smell of char embedded in their belongings and finishes.

## Our Odor Removal and Deodorization Process in Passaic

Effective deodorization starts with identifying every odor source — not just the obvious ones. We begin with a structured walkthrough, probing wall cavities, subfloor spaces, and HVAC returns where odor compounds concentrate. In Passaic's older building stock, that often means checking behind original plaster, inside floor joist bays, and along cast-iron drain runs where sewer gas can seep through deteriorated oakum packing.

Once source materials are identified and removed or treated, we select the deodorization method based on the odor type and the building's construction:

- **Thermal fogging** — a petroleum-based or water-based deodorizing fog that penetrates the same porous surfaces smoke traveled through, neutralizing odor compounds at the molecular level. Effective in plaster-walled rooms and finished basements where smoke has deeply penetrated.
- **Hydroxyl generation** — UV-light-driven oxidation that destroys odor molecules in occupied or semi-occupied spaces without requiring evacuation. Useful in multi-unit buildings where displacing all tenants isn't practical.
- **Ozone treatment** — high-concentration ozone floods an unoccupied space and oxidizes biological and smoke compounds. Highly effective for severe cases, but the space must remain unoccupied during treatment and ventilated thoroughly before re-entry.
- **Encapsulant sealers** — applied to masonry, concrete, or wood framing after deodorization to lock in any residual compounds that can't be physically removed.

The method — or combination of methods — depends on what we find. A sewer backup in a 07055 basement calls for a different approach than smoke damage in a top-floor unit off the Main Avenue corridor.

## Local Note: Plaster Walls and Odor Absorption in Passaic's Pre-War Housing

One thing that catches some contractors off guard in Passaic's older neighborhoods is how differently horsehair plaster behaves compared to modern drywall when it comes to odor absorption. Plaster is alkaline and highly porous — it absorbs smoke and biological compounds deeply and releases them slowly over weeks. In a Third Ward walkup or a Rutherford Heights two-family, a single thermal fogging pass may not be sufficient. We typically run a second treatment cycle and allow 48 to 72 hours before final odor verification, because plaster off-gasses gradually as it re-equilibrates with ambient humidity. Rushing the clearance in these buildings leads to callbacks.
