## Why Edison Properties Are Prone to Hidden Mold Growth

Edison is a township built in waves — postwar capes and split-levels in neighborhoods like Stelton and Oak Tree, colonials from the 1970s and 1980s in North Edison, and large multifamily complexes scattered throughout the 08820 and 08837 ZIP codes. Many of these homes were finished with original cast-iron drain lines and galvanized supply plumbing that is now decades past its expected service life. When those pipes fail inside a finished basement ceiling or behind a tiled bathroom wall, the leak can persist for weeks before anyone notices.

The township's southern edge along the Raritan River adds another layer of risk. Low-lying streets near Mill Brook tributaries experienced significant flooding during Hurricane Ida in 2021, and homes that took on water — even a few inches — often developed mold problems in wall cavities and subfloor assemblies that weren't fully dried out. Flood water carries organic material that accelerates mold colonization, and fiberglass batt insulation in basement rim joists holds moisture long after the visible water is gone.

The Route 1 corridor adds a commercial dimension. Retail and restaurant tenants along that stretch, as well as warehouse and logistics operations near Menlo Park Mall, deal with sprinkler-discharge events and roof-leak losses that can saturate large square footages overnight. Commercial mold issues in those settings often require air quality documentation before a tenant can reopen.

## What a Mold Inspection and Testing Visit Actually Involves

A mold inspection is not a visual walk-through with a flashlight. The process starts with a structured interview about the property's moisture history — past leaks, flooding, HVAC issues, any previous remediation — followed by a room-by-room survey using a calibrated moisture meter and thermal imaging camera to locate elevated readings behind surfaces that look dry.

Where moisture anomalies are found, or where visible staining or odor suggests active growth, surface samples (tape-lift or swab) and air cassette samples are collected. Air samples capture the spore count and species composition in the living space and compare it against an outdoor baseline sample taken the same day. That comparison is what tells you whether elevated indoor spore levels are a building problem or just a reflection of outdoor seasonal conditions — an important distinction in central New Jersey, where outdoor mold counts run high from late summer through fall.

All samples are sent to an accredited third-party laboratory. The written report identifies species, spore concentrations, and affected areas, and it serves as the documentation baseline if remediation is needed and an insurance claim is filed.

## Local Note: What Edison's Postwar Basements Tend to Hide

One pattern that comes up repeatedly in Edison's older neighborhoods is mold growth inside the hollow cores of concrete block foundation walls. Many of the capes and ranches built in the 1950s and 1960s used concrete masonry unit construction for their basement walls, and those hollow cavities can trap condensation and organic debris for years without any visible surface growth. Standard moisture meters read the surface, not the cavity. During inspections in these homes, thermal imaging during temperature differential conditions — early morning in summer, or after the heat kicks on in fall — is often the only way to detect the temperature anomaly that signals moisture inside the block. It's a detail that matters specifically in Edison's older housing stock and one that changes how we approach the inspection protocol.
