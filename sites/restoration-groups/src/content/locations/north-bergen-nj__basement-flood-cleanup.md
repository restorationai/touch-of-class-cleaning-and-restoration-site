## Why North Bergen Basements Flood the Way They Do

North Bergen is built on two very different geographies, and both create problems. On the western side, the flats near the Tonnelle Avenue corridor sit closer to the old Meadowlands drainage basin. Those soils hold water, and when the water table rises after a sustained storm, it finds the path of least resistance: floor drains, sump pits, and the cracks in century-old poured concrete walls.

## Our Basement Flood Cleanup Process in North Bergen

The first thing we do on arrival is water classification — not every basement flood is the same, and the source determines everything that follows. Clean water from a burst supply line is handled differently than groundwater intrusion, which is handled differently than a sewage backup. In North Bergen's older rental stock, we frequently encounter mixed sources: a storm event that overwhelmed the drain system and pushed contaminated water up through a floor drain that was already backing up from a clogged lateral.

Once we've classified the loss, we extract standing water with truck-mounted and portable units, then begin the moisture mapping process. In buildings with plaster-and-lath walls — common in the pre-1950 construction you'll find throughout the Bergenline and Guttenberg border neighborhoods — the wall assembly holds moisture differently than modern drywall. The plaster face can read close to dry on a surface meter while the wood lath behind it stays saturated for days. We use thermal imaging alongside pin and pinless meters to find those hidden wet pockets before they become mold colonies.

Structural drying follows: commercial-grade desiccant and refrigerant dehumidifiers, high-velocity air movers positioned to create the airflow patterns the IICRC S500 standard requires, and daily moisture readings to document drying progress. We don't call a job dry because it looks dry — we call it dry when the numbers confirm it.
