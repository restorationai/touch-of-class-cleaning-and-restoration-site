## Why Clark Properties See Water Damage Issues

The housing stock here tells the story. Most of Clark's residential neighborhoods — from the ranches near Brant Lake to the split-levels along the Westfield Avenue corridor — were built between 1950 and 1970. That means original cast-iron drain lines that are now corroding from the inside out, copper supply lines with soldered joints that fatigue under decades of pressure cycling, and finished basements that were never designed to drain quickly. When a supply line lets go at 2 a.m. or a sump pump loses power during a summer downpour, water spreads across finished flooring, wicks into fiberglass batt insulation, and climbs drywall before most homeowners realize anything is wrong.

The Rahway River floodplain adds a layer of risk that doesn't exist in higher-elevation Union County towns. Properties near the Robinson's Branch drainage corridor can see groundwater intrusion even when there's no visible surface flooding — hydrostatic pressure pushes moisture through foundation walls and floor slabs in ways that a shop vac and a box fan simply cannot address. The commercial properties along Raritan Road face their own version of this: restaurant grease traps and floor drains that back up during heavy rain events, contaminating finished spaces with Category 2 or Category 3 water that requires a different extraction and sanitization protocol than a clean-water pipe break.

## Our Water Damage Restoration Process in Clark

Every job starts with moisture mapping. Before any equipment goes down, the crew walks the affected area with thermal imaging cameras and calibrated moisture meters to find water that has migrated behind walls, under flooring, or into subfloor assemblies — places that look dry to the eye but will grow mold within 48 to 72 hours if left untreated. In Clark's older split-levels, that often means checking the wall cavities between the entry-level and the basement ceiling, where water from an upstairs supply line can pool unseen.

Extraction comes next. Truck-mounted extraction units pull standing water from carpet, hardwood, and concrete far more effectively than portable units, and in finished basements — which are nearly universal in the 07066 ZIP code — speed matters. After extraction, the crew establishes a drying system calibrated to the square footage, material types, and ambient conditions: commercial-grade desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers, high-velocity air movers positioned to create directed airflow across wet surfaces, and daily moisture readings logged against the IICRC S500 drying standard to confirm the structure is actually drying, not just feeling dry.

If materials — drywall, insulation, subfloor panels — have absorbed enough water that they cannot be dried in place without trapping moisture, they are removed. That's a judgment call made with moisture data, not a default. In Clark's postwar homes, the original hardwood floors in living areas are often salvageable with aggressive drying; the OSB subfloor underneath a water-saturated carpet pad usually is not.

## Local Note

One thing worth knowing about Clark's 1950s and 1960s split-levels: the original interior wall construction in many of these homes used a thin plaster-over-metal-lath finish rather than modern drywall. Plaster absorbs water more slowly than drywall but releases it more slowly too — which means a wall that reads "wet" on a moisture meter on day one may still read elevated on day five even with aggressive airflow. Crews who aren't familiar with this construction type sometimes pull equipment too early, leaving residual moisture that feeds mold growth behind intact wall surfaces. The drying timeline for a plaster-wall basement in Clark is genuinely longer than for a comparable drywall-finished space, and the monitoring schedule needs to reflect that.
