## Our Flood Damage Restoration Process in New Brunswick

Every flood job starts with a moisture map, not a mop. When we arrive, we use thermal imaging and pin-type moisture meters to trace exactly where water has migrated — under hardwood floors, inside wall cavities, beneath tile on concrete slabs. In New Brunswick's older buildings, water routinely travels farther than it appears to because plaster walls and original hardwood subfloors wick moisture laterally before it becomes visible. Skipping this step means drying equipment gets placed where the water was, not where it is.

Once the scope is documented, we extract standing water with truck-mounted and portable extraction units, then establish a drying system calibrated to the actual material load — not a generic room count. Structural drying in a 1920s rowhouse with plaster-and-lath walls requires different dwell times and airflow than a poured-concrete basement in a postwar building. We monitor psychrometric readings daily and adjust equipment placement until materials reach the dry standard defined by the IICRC S500, the industry's governing protocol for water damage restoration. We are an IICRC Certified Firm (#210213), and our documentation follows the format most major carriers require for direct billing.

For flood events with contaminated water — river intrusion, sewer backups, drain overflows — we treat the affected area as a Category 3 loss. That means antimicrobial treatment of structural surfaces, controlled demolition of non-salvageable materials, and containment protocols to prevent cross-contamination to unaffected areas of the building.
