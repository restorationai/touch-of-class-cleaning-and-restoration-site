## Why Passaic's Building Stock Shapes Every Renovation

The housing in 07055 was largely built between 1900 and 1940, and it shows in the walls, floors, and mechanical systems. Knob-and-tube wiring still runs through the cavities of many Third Ward and Fourth Ward walkups — not always active, but present enough that opening a wall for a kitchen remodel or bathroom addition triggers an electrical assessment before any finish work begins. Cast-iron drain stacks in these buildings corrode from the inside out; a bathroom remodel that ignores a weeping horizontal run behind the tile is a callback waiting to happen.

The city's density compounds this. In a three-family on a 25-foot lot, a second-floor bathroom remodel directly affects the unit below and the shared stack serving both. Coordinating access, protecting occupied spaces, and sequencing trades so tenants aren't without water for more than a few hours at a time requires a general contractor who has worked in exactly this kind of building — not one who mostly does suburban single-family additions.

Passaic also sits in a flood-prone corridor. Blocks near the riverfront and low-lying areas throughout Downtown Passaic saw repeated inundation during Irene and Ida. Post-flood rebuilds in these zones often involve elevating mechanicals, replacing concrete block foundations that have been saturated multiple times, and selecting finish materials rated for periodic moisture exposure. That's a different spec than a standard remodel, and the permitting conversation with the city reflects it.

## Our Renovation and General Contracting Process in Passaic

Every project starts with a walkthrough that accounts for what the building actually is — not what the listing or the previous permit says it is. In older Passaic multifamilies, that means probing walls before framing new ones, checking subfloor conditions under existing tile, and confirming that the electrical panel can carry the load a kitchen or bathroom upgrade requires.

From there, the process runs in a defined sequence:

1. **Scope and permit documentation** — We pull permits through Passaic's building department and coordinate with the city's inspection schedule. Older homes in dense urban lots sometimes require variance conversations; we handle that communication directly.
2. **Demolition and hazard assessment** — Pre-1978 buildings in Passaic commonly contain lead paint and, in some cases, asbestos-containing materials in floor tile or pipe insulation. As an IICRC Certified Firm (#210213) and NJ Licensed Home Improvement Contractor, we follow the protocols required before any demo proceeds.
3. **Structural and mechanical rough-in** — Framing, plumbing, and electrical rough-in are sequenced so inspections happen before walls close. In multifamily buildings, this phase is coordinated around tenant schedules.
4. **Finish work and final inspection** — Tile, cabinetry, fixtures, flooring, and paint are installed to the agreed spec. We don't consider a job complete until it passes final inspection and the client has walked through every item on the punch list.

For post-damage rebuilds — the category that makes up a significant share of work in flood-affected neighborhoods — the process integrates with insurance documentation from the start, so scope changes driven by hidden damage don't stall the claim.

## Local Note: What Flood-Cycle Renovations in Passaic Actually Require

Contractors who haven't worked in Passaic's riverfront-adjacent neighborhoods sometimes underestimate what repeated flood exposure does to a structure over time. A basement that flooded during Ida and again during a smaller event a year later doesn't just need new drywall — the concrete block or parged masonry has likely absorbed enough moisture over the years that standard interior waterproofing paint is a cosmetic fix, not a solution. In those situations, the honest scope includes a drainage assessment, possible exterior grading correction, and finish materials — like cement board, closed-cell foam, and moisture-resistant flooring — that can survive the next high-water event without a full tear-out.
