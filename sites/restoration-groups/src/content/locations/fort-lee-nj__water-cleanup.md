## Why Fort Lee Properties See Water Cleanup Issues

Fort Lee's geography does a lot of the explaining. The borough sits atop the Palisades cliffs, and when a storm rolls in from the Hudson, runoff has nowhere to go but fast — steep grades push water into below-grade parking garages, mechanical rooms, and the garden-level units that anchor many of the towers near the George Washington Bridge. Tropical Storm Ida in 2021 demonstrated exactly how quickly Bergen County's drainage systems can be overwhelmed, and Fort Lee's cliff-side topography made it one of the harder-hit communities in the region.

## Our Water Cleanup Process in Fort Lee

The first step on any water loss is containment — stopping the source if it hasn't been stopped, and understanding exactly where the water went. In a high-rise building, that means tracing the migration floor by floor using thermal imaging and calibrated moisture meters, not just looking at what's visibly wet. Water behind concrete and tile reads dry at the surface while the substrate underneath stays saturated for days.

Once the scope is mapped, we extract standing water with truck-mounted and portable extraction units — portable equipment matters in elevator buildings where hose runs from a parking garage to a 12th-floor unit aren't practical with a truck mount alone. Drying follows the IICRC S500 standard: we calculate the psychrometric conditions in each affected space, place industrial desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers accordingly, and monitor moisture readings daily until all structural materials reach their dry standard. Every reading is logged and timestamped, which matters when you're coordinating with a building's property manager, an HOA board, and two or three separate insurance adjusters at once.

For contents — furniture, cabinetry, personal belongings — we document, photograph, and either dry in place or move to a staging area depending on the severity of saturation and the layout of the unit.

## Fort Lee Insurance & HOA Coordination

Water losses in Fort Lee's condominium towers almost always involve layered coverage: the unit owner's HO-6 policy, the building's master policy (which typically covers common areas and the building envelope), and sometimes a neighbor's policy if their unit was the source. Sorting out which carrier covers which scope of damage is one of the more time-consuming parts of the job, and it's where disorganized documentation creates real delays.

We provide a complete written scope, moisture logs, and photo documentation formatted for carrier review. We bill most major insurance carriers directly. If the building's HOA requires pre-approval for work in common areas or has a preferred vendor list for certain trades, we communicate with the property management office before work begins — not after.

## Local Note

In Fort Lee's prewar buildings — particularly the older stock in Coytesville — plaster-and-lath wall systems absorb and hold water very differently than modern drywall. Plaster is dense enough that surface readings can look acceptable while the lath and framing behind it remain wet. We use penetrating probes rather than surface-only sensors in those structures, and we build extra drying time into the schedule because plaster releases moisture slowly and unevenly. Rushing the drying phase in those buildings is the most common reason a water loss turns into a mold remediation job six weeks later.
