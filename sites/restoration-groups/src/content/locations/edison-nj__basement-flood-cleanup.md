## Our Basement Flood Cleanup Process in Edison

The work starts with water classification and containment, not extraction. Knowing whether the source is a clean supply line, a backed-up drain, or exterior groundwater intrusion determines the personal protective equipment the crew wears, the disposal protocol for saturated materials, and what your insurance carrier will need documented. We photograph and moisture-map the space before a single piece of equipment is placed.

Extraction comes next — truck-mounted or portable units depending on access — followed by the placement of commercial desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers and air movers calibrated to the square footage and construction type of your specific basement. Drying is monitored against the IICRC S500 standard, with readings logged daily so there is a defensible record for your adjuster. Affected drywall, insulation, and flooring are removed to the extent necessary to reach the wet substrate — not more, not less. When the structure reads dry, we provide a written moisture report before any reconstruction begins.

## Edison Insurance Coordination

Most standard homeowners policies cover sudden and accidental water discharge — a burst pipe, a washing machine supply line failure, a water heater that lets go. What they typically do not cover without a separate rider is groundwater intrusion or sewer backup, both of which are common in Edison's lower-lying areas and older neighborhoods. We document the loss origin carefully because that distinction matters enormously to your claim outcome. We work directly with major carriers, provide the photo documentation and moisture logs adjusters require, and can communicate with your adjuster on scope so you are not translating between two technical vocabularies.

## Local Note

In Edison's older split-levels and capes — particularly in neighborhoods like Clara Barton and Stelton where homes were built on slab-on-grade or shallow crawlspace foundations — the bottom plates of interior partition walls are often set directly on the concrete floor with no pressure-treated lumber and no capillary break. When a basement floods, those plates absorb water like a wick and the moisture travels up into the wall cavity before it ever shows on the surface. Thermal imaging during the initial assessment routinely reveals wet cavities behind walls that feel dry to the touch.
