## Why Scotch Plains Homes Are Prone to Pipe Breaks

The housing stock tells most of the story. The bulk of Scotch Plains was built between 1950 and 1970 — split-levels, raised ranches, and center-hall colonials with finished basements and galvanized or early-copper supply lines that are now at or past their service life. Galvanized pipe corrodes from the inside out; by the time a homeowner notices reduced water pressure, the pipe wall may be thin enough that a single cold night or a water-hammer event from a fast-closing valve is enough to split it open.

Older homes closer to Downtown Scotch Plains and the Frazee House corridor add another layer: original cast-iron drains that no longer seat tightly against newer PVC connections, and supply lines that run through exterior walls with minimal insulation. When a pipe breaks in a finished basement, water can travel under laminate flooring, behind drywall, and into wall cavities before it surfaces anywhere visible. The Park Avenue and Route 22 commercial corridors face a different but related problem — sprinkler-system pipe breaks and supply-line failures in older retail and mixed-use buildings where plumbing hasn't been updated since original construction.

## Our Burst Pipe Cleanup and Repair Process in Scotch Plains

When a call comes in from a Scotch Plains address, the first priority is stopping the loss. For finished basements common in Scotch Plains split-levels, that typically means truck-mounted extraction on hard surfaces and weighted extraction on carpet, followed by targeted drying with commercial-grade desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers and high-velocity air movers positioned to dry wall cavities from the inside. Structural drying typically runs three to five days depending on material depth and ambient humidity. We check readings daily and don't pull equipment early. Once materials reach target moisture content, we assess what can be saved and what needs controlled demolition before reconstruction begins.
