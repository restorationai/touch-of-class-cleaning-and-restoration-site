## Our Basement Flood Cleanup Process in Roselle

Every job starts with moisture mapping. Before a single piece of equipment is placed, a technician walks the basement with a thermal camera and pin-type meters to find where water has traveled — behind finished wall panels, under slab-on-grade sections, into the base of stair framing. In Roselle's older homes, this step matters more than usual because plaster-and-lath walls absorb water differently than modern drywall. The surface can read close to dry while the wood lath behind it stays saturated, and a drying plan that ignores that will fail the final clearance reading.

Once the scope is clear, extraction comes first — truck-mounted units for standing water, then weighted extraction tools pressed against carpet and pad. Contaminated materials (sewage-affected drywall, insulation, flooring) are bagged and removed before drying equipment is ever staged. Structural drying follows the IICRC S500 standard: calibrated air movers and desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers positioned to create directed airflow through wall cavities and across concrete. Daily moisture readings track progress and drive equipment adjustments until the structure reaches its dry standard.

As an IICRC Certified Firm (#210213) and NJ Licensed Home Improvement Contractor, every phase of the work is documented — photographs, moisture logs, equipment placement records — in a format that translates directly into an insurance claim file.

## Local Note

Roselle's finished basements from the 1940s and 1950s often used a thin coat of hydraulic cement directly over the block foundation wall before paneling was installed — no true vapor barrier, no drainage mat. When those walls take on water, the cement coat traps moisture between the block and the panel framing. Drying equipment positioned only in the open basement space won't reach it. Technicians working in this housing stock need to open the wall cavity — removing the lower course of paneling — to get airflow against the block face. Skipping that step is the most common reason a basement in this part of Union County fails a re-inspection after drying.

If your home is showing efflorescence streaks or a white mineral crust on the lower block courses, that's a sign water has been moving through the wall repeatedly, not just during the most recent event. It's worth noting to the technician before work begins.
