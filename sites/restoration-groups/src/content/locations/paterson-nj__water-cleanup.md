## Why Paterson Properties See Repeated Water Damage

Paterson's housing stock is among the oldest in New Jersey. Dense 19th-century mill-worker rowhomes, brick three-family walkups, and aging frame houses with cast-iron drain lines dominate neighborhoods from the Eastside to South Paterson. These buildings were not designed with modern waterproofing in mind, and decades of deferred maintenance compound the risk.

Cast-iron drain stacks corrode from the inside out. By the time a homeowner notices a slow drain, the pipe may already be partially collapsed — and a single heavy rain event can push sewage back through floor drains into finished basement spaces. In multi-unit buildings, a leak on the second floor travels through plaster and lath to the unit below before anyone realizes there's a problem. Brick exterior walls wick groundwater laterally into basement walls during sustained rain, a pattern especially common in the Sandy Hill and Lakeview sections where older masonry foundations sit close to grade.

Converted industrial buildings — former silk-era factories now operating as apartment complexes — carry their own risk profile. Sprinkler systems and standpipe risers in these structures can fail at fittings, releasing large volumes of water into spaces that were never designed as residential interiors. When that happens, the water migrates through concrete subfloors and into units below before the building super even locates the shutoff.

## Our Water Cleanup Process in Paterson

Every water cleanup job starts with moisture mapping, not assumptions. We use thermal imaging cameras and calibrated moisture meters to trace where water has traveled — behind plaster walls, under original hardwood flooring, inside the cavities of brick exterior walls — before any drying equipment is placed. In Paterson's older housing stock, surface readings are routinely misleading: a wall can read dry at the face while the lath behind it holds significant moisture.

Once the affected area is fully mapped, we extract standing water with truck-mounted or portable extraction units, then position commercial-grade desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers and high-velocity air movers calibrated to the room's cubic footage and material types. Drying targets follow the IICRC S500 standard. We monitor daily and adjust equipment placement as materials dry at different rates — plaster releases moisture more slowly than drywall, and brick releases it more slowly still.

For losses involving sewage backup or contaminated floodwater — common in basement units across the 07501 and 07503 ZIP codes — we follow Category 3 water protocols: affected porous materials are removed rather than dried in place, and all contacted surfaces are treated before reconstruction begins.

## Local Note: What Old Plaster Walls Mean for Drying Time

Paterson's pre-1940 rowhomes and multi-family buildings were almost universally finished with three-coat plaster over wood lath — a combination that behaves very differently from the paper-faced drywall in newer construction. Plaster is denser and more alkaline, which slows microbial growth slightly, but it also holds moisture deep in the substrate long after the surface feels dry to the touch. In practice, this means drying timelines in these buildings run longer than industry averages, and equipment needs to stay in place until subsurface readings confirm the lath cavity has reached equilibrium — not just the plaster face. Pulling equipment too early is one of the most common reasons mold appears weeks after a water loss in Paterson's older housing stock. We account for this on every job in the city.
