## Our Flood Damage Restoration Process in Englewood

Every job starts with a moisture mapping walk-through. Technicians use thermal imaging and pin-type moisture meters to trace exactly how far water has traveled — behind baseboards, under hardwood floors, into adjacent rooms. In Englewood's older housing stock, water frequently wicks further than it appears on the surface, so we map before we cut.

Extraction comes next: truck-mounted and portable units pull standing water and surface saturation from carpet, hardwood, concrete, and crawl spaces. Once bulk water is removed, the drying phase begins using commercial-grade desiccant and refrigerant dehumidifiers alongside high-velocity air movers, positioned according to the IICRC S500 standard for structural drying. Moisture readings are logged at every visit so the drying curve is documented — a record that matters both for insurance adjusters and for confirming the structure is genuinely dry before any reconstruction begins.

For contaminated flood water — Category 2 or Category 3 losses involving sewage backup or exterior floodwater — affected porous materials are removed and bagged on-site, and antimicrobial treatment is applied to framing and concrete before drying equipment is set. We hold IICRC Certified Firm status (#210213) and are licensed as an NJ Home Improvement Contractor through the NJ Division of Consumer Affairs, so the paperwork trail is clean from first call through final inspection.

## Local Note

One pattern that comes up repeatedly in Englewood's East Hill and older Fourth Ward properties: finished basements with drop ceilings installed over original plaster or concrete block. When flood water enters, it pools invisibly above the drop-ceiling tiles and saturates the space between the suspended grid and the structural ceiling — sometimes for days before the homeowner notices. By that point, mold colonization can begin on the organic material in ceiling tiles and any wood framing above. On those jobs, we always remove ceiling tiles and inspect the cavity above before setting drying equipment, even when the visible damage looks minor. Skipping that step is one of the most common reasons a flood job gets called back.
