## Why Clark Properties See Flood Damage So Often

Clark's geography sets the stage. The Rahway River traces the township's northern and western borders, and Robinson's Branch cuts through low-lying ground near Lake Avenue before feeding into it. The housing stock compounds the risk. Most Clark homes were built before modern waterproofing standards, with cast-iron floor drains and copper supply lines that are now 60-plus years old. Cast iron corrodes from the inside out, and a partially collapsed drain line means that even modest groundwater infiltration has nowhere to go. ## Our Flood Damage Restoration Process in Clark

Every job starts with a moisture map, not a mop. When a crew arrives at a Clark address, the first step is probing walls, subfloors, and framing cavities with thermal imaging and pin-type moisture meters to understand exactly where water traveled — because in a split-level or cape, it rarely stays where it entered. Water that comes in through a basement window well migrates under the slab, wicks into the base of partition walls, and can reach the first floor without ever being visible.

Once the scope is documented, extraction comes first: truck-mounted vacuums pull standing water, then weighted extraction tools pull residual moisture from carpet and padding. Affected drywall is cut to the flood line (typically 12 to 24 inches above the highest moisture reading), insulation is removed, and the framing is treated with an EPA-registered antimicrobial to interrupt mold colonization — which can begin within 24 to 48 hours on wet organic material.

Drying is calibrated to the structure. Clark's older homes often have plaster-over-wood-lath ceilings in finished basements; plaster holds moisture longer than modern drywall and requires extended drying cycles with directed airflow rather than simply running dehumidifiers in the center of the room. Drying logs are recorded daily and shared with the homeowner and their insurance adjuster throughout the process.
