## Why Highland Park Properties Are Especially Vulnerable to Flood Damage

Highland Park's housing stock is one of the oldest in Middlesex County. The colonials, bungalows, and two-family homes built between roughly 1910 and 1945 were constructed long before modern waterproofing standards existed. Many of these homes have finished basements — common throughout Livingston Manor and the Triangle neighborhood — that sit below the water table during a significant rain event. Original cast-iron drain lines and galvanized supply plumbing, still in service in a surprising number of these properties, are prone to joint failure and backflow when the municipal system is overwhelmed.

The borough's topography compounds the problem. Highland Park slopes gently toward the Raritan River along its entire southern edge, meaning that stormwater from higher elevations in North Highland Park and the Meadow area drains toward the same low corridors that already absorb river overflow. During a 100-year storm event, those two water sources — surface runoff and river backflow — can meet inside a basement simultaneously.

The Raritan Avenue business district adds a commercial dimension. Restaurants and retail spaces along that corridor run commercial dishwashers, grease traps, and fire-suppression sprinkler systems, any of which can contribute to significant interior flooding independent of weather.

## Our Flood Damage Restoration Process in Highland Park

Every flood loss is different, but the sequence we follow is consistent and calibrated to what we actually find in Highland Park homes.

**Rapid assessment and water extraction** come first. We use truck-mounted and portable extraction units to remove standing water from basements, finished living areas, and crawl spaces. In older homes with original hardwood subfloors — common in the pre-war stock throughout Downtown Highland Park — we probe moisture levels at multiple depths before assuming extraction is complete, because water wicks laterally through tongue-and-groove boards in ways that aren't visible on the surface.

**Structural drying** follows extraction. We place industrial dehumidifiers and high-velocity air movers in a calculated pattern based on the square footage and construction type of the affected space. Drying timelines are validated against the IICRC S500 standard, and we log moisture readings daily so you have a documented record for your insurance adjuster.

**Affected material removal** is handled selectively. Not every wet wall cavity requires demolition. We use thermal imaging and pin-type moisture meters to identify exactly where water has migrated before cutting, which limits unnecessary damage to original plaster, trim, and flooring that would be costly or impossible to replicate in a 1920s bungalow.

**Post-drying documentation and scope** closes out the mitigation phase and hands off a clear picture to your insurance carrier or, if you're self-paying, to our reconstruction team.

## Local Note

One pattern we've seen repeatedly in Highland Park's finished basements: original 1930s and 1940s concrete block foundation walls absorb floodwater through their cores rather than just at the surface. When water recedes, that moisture stays trapped inside the block cavities and continues releasing humidity into the space for days after the visible flooding is gone. Standard moisture readings taken only at the wall surface will miss this entirely.
