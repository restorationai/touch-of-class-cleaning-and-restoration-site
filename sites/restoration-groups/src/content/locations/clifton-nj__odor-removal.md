## Our Odor Removal and Deodorization Process in Clifton

Every job starts with a source assessment — not just masking what you smell, but tracing it to the material holding the odor compound. We use moisture meters and thermal imaging to find hidden saturation in wall cavities and under flooring before any deodorization equipment is placed. Treating odor over a wet substrate is wasted effort; the source has to be dry and clean first.

Once the source material is addressed, we select the method that matches the structure and the odor type:

- **Thermal fogging** — a solvent-based fog that follows the same pathways smoke traveled, reaching inside wall cavities, ductwork, and above drop ceilings. Effective for fire and smoke losses in Clifton's older homes where smoke migrated through unsealed framing bays.
- **Hydroxyl generation** — UV-light units that produce hydroxyl radicals, oxidizing odor molecules in the air and on surfaces. Safe to run in occupied or partially occupied spaces, which matters in multi-family buildings where adjacent units need to stay accessible.
- **Ozone treatment** — high-concentration ozone placed in unoccupied structures for a timed cycle. Highly effective on deep-seated pet and smoke odors in vacant units, but the space must remain unoccupied during treatment and for a clearance period after.
- **Injection and surface application** — direct injection of deodorizing agents into wall cavities and subfloor voids, combined with surface-applied encapsulants on concrete or masonry where odor compounds have bonded to the substrate.

For fire losses, we coordinate deodorization with structural cleaning so smoke residue is removed from surfaces before fogging — otherwise residue continues off-gassing and recontaminates the air.

## Local Note

Clifton's 1940s and 1950s construction used horsehair plaster on wood lath in many capes and colonials — a wall system that behaves very differently from modern drywall when exposed to smoke or flood water. Plaster absorbs odor compounds deeply and releases them slowly over weeks, which means a room can smell clean two days after a fire and then return to a noticeable smoke odor as temperature and humidity fluctuate. We account for this by extending the deodorization dwell time in plaster-wall structures and scheduling a follow-up air quality check before closing out the job — a step we skip in newer drywall construction but consider standard practice in Clifton's older housing stock.
