A house fire leaves more than charred wood behind. In Garfield's dense blocks of early-1900s two- and three-family frame homes — the kind stacked tightly along the Belmont Avenue corridor and throughout the Plauderville neighborhood — smoke travels fast through shared wall cavities, climbs into finished attics, and settles into every porous surface before the fire department clears the scene. The odor you smell three days later is not just unpleasant; it is soot particulate and volatile organic compounds embedded in plaster, subfloor, and HVAC ductwork. Getting it out requires more than airing out a room.

## Why Garfield's Housing Stock Complicates Smoke Damage

Garfield (ZIP 07026) is a city built before modern building codes standardized fire stops and compartmentalization. The two- and three-family frames that line streets from the Harrison Avenue district to the Passaic Street corridor were constructed with balloon framing — a method where wall cavities run continuously from the basement sill plate to the roof ridge with no horizontal blocking. In a balloon-framed home, smoke and hot gases travel vertically through those open cavities the moment a fire starts on any floor. By the time the fire is out, the odor-causing compounds have already migrated two or three stories away from the room where the fire originated.

That matters for restoration because standard smoke cleanup protocols designed for platform-framed postwar capes will underperform in these older structures. Cavity access, targeted negative-air pressure, and thermal fogging into wall interiors are often necessary steps that a surface-only cleaning will miss entirely. Garfield's older plaster walls also absorb smoke differently than drywall — the lime-based substrate is porous and can hold odor compounds deep in the substrate, requiring longer dwell times for encapsulants and sealers.

## Our Smoke Damage Restoration Process in Garfield

Every job starts with a structured scope assessment, not a visual walk-through. We use particle counters and thermal imaging to map where smoke traveled beyond the visible burn zone — including inside wall cavities, above drop ceilings, and into HVAC return plenums. In Garfield's multifamily buildings, we also assess adjoining units when shared walls are present, because smoke migration into a neighbor's unit is a liability issue that needs to be documented early.

From there, the process follows a defined sequence:

1. **Content pack-out and inventory** — salvageable belongings are removed, catalogued, and transported for off-site ozone or ultrasonic cleaning before the structure work begins.
2. **Dry soot removal** — HEPA-filtered vacuuming of all surfaces before any wet cleaning, because wiping dry soot without vacuuming first drives it deeper into porous materials.
3. **Wet cleaning and chemical sponging** — surfaces are cleaned with pH-appropriate agents matched to the soot type (protein smoke from kitchen fires behaves very differently from synthetic-material smoke).
4. **Cavity treatment** — where balloon-frame cavities are confirmed or suspected, we introduce thermal fog or hydroxyl treatment into the wall interiors through controlled access points.
5. **Odor neutralization and sealing** — affected structural surfaces receive an odor-blocking encapsulant rated for smoke, followed by a final air quality check before any reconstruction begins.
6. **Reconstruction coordination** — as an NJ Licensed Home Improvement Contractor, we can carry the job through drywall, paint, and finish work without the homeowner managing a second contractor.

The team is IICRC Certified (Firm #210213), and work is available around the clock — a meaningful detail when a fire happens at 2 a.m. and the adjuster wants a scope started before the weekend.

## Insurance Coordination for Garfield Homeowners and Landlords

Smoke damage claims in multifamily buildings often involve more than one policy — the building owner's policy, a tenant's renter's insurance, and sometimes a neighboring unit's carrier if smoke migrated through shared walls.
