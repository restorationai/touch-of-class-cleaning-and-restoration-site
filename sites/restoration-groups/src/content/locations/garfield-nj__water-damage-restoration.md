## Why Garfield Properties See Water Damage So Often

Garfield's housing stock is one of the oldest in Bergen County. Most of the two- and three-family frame homes lining the Belmont Avenue corridor and the streets off Passaic Street were built between 1910 and 1950, when cast-iron supply and drain lines were standard. Those pipes are now well past their design life. Pinhole leaks are common; full-bore failures happen without warning. Finished basements — nearly universal in this housing stock — convert what would be a crawlspace drip into a flooded living space within minutes.

The geography compounds the problem. The Passaic River runs the entire western edge of the city, and its flood history is not abstract. When Hurricane Ida hit in September 2021, riverfront blocks along River Drive and low-lying basements throughout the city took on water that in some cases exceeded three feet. Saddle River tributaries add secondary flood risk: after a hard two-inch storm, low spots in Plauderville and the Dahnert's Lake area can sheet-flood before municipal storm drains catch up. Multifamily buildings along the Passaic Street business district face an additional exposure — a single overhead pipe failure on an upper floor can track water through multiple units before anyone notices.

## Our Water Damage Restoration Process in Garfield

Every job starts with a moisture map, not a mop. When a crew arrives at a Garfield property, the first step is thermal imaging and moisture metering across walls, subfloor, and ceiling cavities — because in a wood-framed row home, water travels further than the visible stain suggests. That baseline reading drives every decision that follows.

**Water extraction** comes next. Truck-mounted extraction units pull standing water from finished basements and living areas; portable units handle tight spaces common in Garfield's older floor plans. Once bulk water is removed, **structural drying** begins: industrial desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers and high-velocity air movers are positioned according to the IICRC S500 standard for psychrometric drying, not by guesswork. Moisture readings are logged daily so drying progress is documented — a record your insurance adjuster will want.

For homes with plaster-and-lath walls — still common in pre-1940 Garfield construction — drying timelines run longer than they would in a modern drywall home. Plaster is dense and releases absorbed moisture slowly. We account for that in the drying plan rather than pulling equipment prematurely and leaving hidden moisture behind.
