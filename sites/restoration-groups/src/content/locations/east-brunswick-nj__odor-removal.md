## Our Deodorization Process, Calibrated to East Brunswick Conditions

Effective odor elimination follows a sequence — skipping steps is why DIY attempts fail. The process begins with source identification and removal. No deodorization technology works if the odor-generating material is still present. That means removing saturated drywall, contaminated insulation, or charred framing before any treatment begins.

Once the source is gone, the choice of treatment method depends on the structure and the odor type:

- **Thermal fogging** — a petroleum- or water-based deodorizing fog is heated and dispersed to penetrate the same microscopic pathways that smoke traveled. Particularly effective in East Brunswick's older wood-framed homes where smoke has followed wall cavities and attic spaces.
- **Hydroxyl generation** — UV-light-driven hydroxyl radicals break odor molecules apart at the chemical level without requiring occupants or pets to vacate for extended periods. Useful in occupied multi-unit buildings or condo clusters where ozone treatment isn't practical.
- **Ozone treatment** — high-concentration ozone is highly effective for severe smoke or biological odors, but it requires full evacuation of the space and careful post-treatment ventilation. We calculate dwell time based on room volume and odor severity, not a flat number.
- **HEPA air scrubbing and activated carbon filtration** — runs concurrently to capture airborne particulates and VOCs throughout the treatment window.

All drying and deodorization work follows IICRC S500 and S520 standards. The Restoration Group holds IICRC Certified Firm status (#210213), which means the process is documented, not improvised.

## Local Note: Finished Basements and the Odor-Layering Problem

One pattern that comes up repeatedly in East Brunswick's older residential tracts: finished basements that have been re-drywalled, re-carpeted, and re-painted multiple times over five decades. Each layer traps odors from the previous occupants' era — cigarette smoke from the 1970s, pet odors from the 1990s, mildew from a 2011 flood. When a new water loss or fire event hits, all of those dormant compounds can reactivate simultaneously. Thermal fogging followed by hydroxyl treatment is often the right combination here because the fog penetrates the historical layers while the hydroxyl generator addresses the fresh odor compounds in the air. A single-method approach frequently leaves one generation of odor behind.
