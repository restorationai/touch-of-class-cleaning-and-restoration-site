## Why Somerville Properties See Basement Flooding So Often

Somerville's geography puts it in a natural pinch point. The Raritan River runs along the borough's northern edge, and Peters Brook cuts through the lower-lying sections toward the West End and Fairview. During significant rain events — the kind that hit the region during Ida in 2021 and before that during Floyd and Irene — stormwater overwhelms the combined drainage infrastructure and backs up into basements faster than a sump pump can keep pace.

The housing stock compounds the problem. Homes along Gaston Avenue and the streets feeding off East Main were largely built in the early twentieth century with rubble-stone or poured-concrete foundations that were never designed to be watertight under hydrostatic pressure. Many of those basements were finished decades later — drywall over furring strips, carpet over concrete — which means water wicks into wall cavities and sits under flooring long after the visible flood recedes. Aging galvanized supply lines and cast-iron drain stacks, common throughout the 08876 ZIP code, are also prone to sudden failures that have nothing to do with weather: a corroded elbow lets go overnight and a finished basement takes on hundreds of gallons before anyone notices.

Downtown's newer mid-rise apartment buildings bring a different pattern: supply-line failures in upper-unit kitchens or bathrooms that migrate down through floor assemblies and collect in below-grade parking or storage levels. These losses tend to be larger in square footage but caught earlier because building management is on-site.

## Our Basement Flood Cleanup Process in Somerville

Every job starts with a moisture assessment, not just a visual inspection. Water behind plaster can read dry at the surface while the lath beneath stays saturated — a common finding in the Victorian-era homes near the Wallace House historic district. We use thermal imaging and penetrating moisture meters to map exactly where water has traveled before any drying equipment is placed.

From there, the process moves in a defined sequence:

- **Water extraction** — truck-mounted and portable extractors pull standing water from concrete floors, carpet, and subfloor assemblies.
- **Controlled demolition** — saturated drywall, baseboards, and flooring that cannot be dried in place are removed to the flood cut line, exposing cavities for airflow. We document everything photographically before removal for your insurance file.
- **Structural drying** — commercial-grade desiccant and refrigerant dehumidifiers, paired with high-velocity air movers, dry the structure to IICRC S500 standard. Monitoring logs are recorded daily.
- **Antimicrobial treatment** — concrete and framing surfaces are treated to inhibit secondary growth during the drying period.
- **Clearance and documentation** — final moisture readings confirm the structure has returned to acceptable levels before any rebuild begins.

As an IICRC Certified Firm (#210213), our drying protocols follow the S500 standard for water damage restoration. We are also a licensed NJ Home Improvement Contractor through the Division of Consumer Affairs, which matters when the scope of work crosses into structural repairs.

Most standard homeowners policies cover sudden and accidental water losses — a burst pipe, a failed water heater, an appliance supply line.
