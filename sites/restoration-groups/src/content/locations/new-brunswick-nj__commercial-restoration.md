## Why New Brunswick Commercial Properties Face Elevated Restoration Risk

New Brunswick's building stock is unusually layered. The blocks closest to Rutgers University — including Rutgers Village and the corridors feeding into campus — are dense with early-1900s multifamily and mixed-use structures whose galvanized and cast-iron supply lines have long exceeded their service life. When one of those lines fails inside a commercial tenant space, water doesn't travel predictably: it follows old framing cavities, drops through plaster-and-lath assemblies, and pools in basement mechanical rooms before anyone notices.

The downtown high-rise corridor adds a different exposure. Sprinkler systems in newer commercial towers are reliable until they're not — a single head activated by a kitchen exhaust event or a mechanical room heat spike can release hundreds of gallons before the panel trips. Restaurants and offices along the George Street business district have seen exactly this pattern, and the losses compound quickly when the affected floor is above occupied retail.

Then there's the Raritan River. Boyd Park sits at the southern edge of the city, and the low-lying riverfront blocks between downtown and the water have flooded repeatedly over the decades. Tropical Storm Ida in 2021 pushed water into ground-floor commercial spaces in that corridor — a reminder that New Brunswick's flood exposure isn't theoretical. Businesses in those zones need a restoration contractor who already understands the city's drainage patterns, not one reading a map for the first time.

## Our Commercial Restoration Process in New Brunswick

Every commercial loss is different, but the sequence that limits business interruption is consistent. When we arrive on-site, the first priority is stopping active damage — isolating the water source, boarding compromised openings after a fire, or containing a sewage intrusion before it migrates into adjacent tenant spaces. In older New Brunswick mixed-use buildings, that containment step is more involved than it sounds: shared wall assemblies and open floor systems mean a loss on one floor can affect two or three others.

Once the source is controlled, we document thoroughly — room-by-room moisture mapping, thermal imaging of wall cavities, and photo documentation calibrated to what commercial insurance carriers need for a clean claim. Drying equipment is staged to match the building's layout and occupancy requirements; a restaurant that needs to reopen its kitchen in 48 hours gets a different equipment plan than a vacant office floor being rebuilt over two weeks.

For fire and smoke losses, the odor work in commercial spaces is more demanding than residential. Smoke penetrates HVAC ductwork, settles into upholstered seating, and embeds in porous ceiling tiles in ways that require systematic source removal rather than masking. We work through the scope methodically so the space is genuinely clean when employees return, not just visually presentable.
