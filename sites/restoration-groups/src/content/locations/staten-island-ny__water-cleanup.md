## Our Water Cleanup Process in Staten Island

Every job starts with a thorough inspection using thermal imaging and calibrated moisture meters — not just a visual walk-through. In Staten Island homes, that step matters more than it might elsewhere, because water in a plaster-and-lath wall behaves differently than water in modern drywall. Plaster can read dry at the surface while the lath behind it stays saturated; skip that step and you're sealing in the moisture that feeds mold.

From there, the process follows the IICRC S500 standard for water damage restoration:

- **Standing water extraction** using truck-mounted and portable units, including submersible pumps for basement flooding
- **Structural drying** with industrial air movers and refrigerant dehumidifiers positioned to create directed airflow through wall cavities and under flooring
- **Daily moisture monitoring** with documented readings so drying progress is trackable and defensible for insurance purposes
- **Controlled demolition** when wet materials — saturated subfloor, swollen baseboard, soaked insulation — cannot be dried in place without creating a secondary mold risk
- **Antimicrobial treatment** of affected surfaces before any reconstruction begins

For homes in flood-prone ZIP codes like 10306 (South Beach, New Dorp) or 10304 (St. George, Stapleton), we also assess whether floodwater carried contaminants that require Category 3 handling protocols — a distinction that changes both the safety procedures and the scope of what needs to come out.

## Local Note

In Staten Island's older frame homes — particularly the early-1900s stock in St. George and Port Richmond — plaster walls are applied over wood lath rather than gypsum board. When water gets into those assemblies, the plaster itself may feel firm and look intact while the lath and the framing behind it are holding significant moisture. Drying times for these wall systems run longer than for drywall, and the airflow strategy has to account for the fact that the plaster acts as a partial vapor barrier. Crews that aren't familiar with this construction type sometimes declare a job dry too early; we set expectations accordingly and extend monitoring until readings are stable across the full assembly depth.
