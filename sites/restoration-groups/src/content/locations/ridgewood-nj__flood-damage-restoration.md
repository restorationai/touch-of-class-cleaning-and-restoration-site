## Why Ridgewood Properties Are Especially Vulnerable to Flood Damage

Ridgewood's architectural character is part of its appeal — the grand Victorians along the Heights, the stately Tudors in Old Country Club, the colonials tucked behind mature oaks on the West Side. But those same homes, most of them built between 1900 and 1950, carry aging galvanized or early copper supply lines that can fail without warning. Finished basements are nearly universal in this housing stock, which means a two-inch flood event that might be a nuisance in a newer home becomes a full gut-and-dry-out project here.

The Ho-Ho-Kus Brook and the Saddle River are the other variable. Both waterways have narrow channels relative to the volume they carry after a sustained nor'easter or a fast-moving summer storm. When they rise, water doesn't just enter through basement windows — it saturates the ground table, overwhelms French drains, and wicks up through slab cracks. Homes within a few blocks of either brook can see hydrostatic intrusion even when the storm appears to have passed.

Slate and flat roofs on Ridgewood's historic homes add a third exposure: ice dams in January and February trap meltwater that finds its way through fascia and into wall cavities, producing hidden moisture damage that doesn't announce itself until mold is already colonizing the framing — typically within 48 to 72 hours of the initial saturation.

## Our Flood Damage Restoration Process in Ridgewood

Every flood loss is different, but the sequence matters and the timeline is non-negotiable. Here is how a typical residential flood job unfolds:

**Step 1 — Emergency extraction.** Truck-mounted extractors remove standing water from finished basements and main-floor areas. In older Ridgewood homes with hardwood subfloors over concrete, we probe for moisture trapped beneath the wood before assuming the surface is dry.

**Step 2 — Moisture mapping.** Thermal imaging cameras and calibrated moisture meters document every affected wall cavity, floor assembly, and ceiling plane. This documentation is also what your insurance adjuster needs to approve the full scope of work.

**Step 3 — Structural drying.** Commercial-grade desiccant and refrigerant dehumidifiers, paired with high-velocity air movers, are positioned to dry the structure from the inside out. In Ridgewood's plaster-wall homes, this phase runs longer than it would in modern drywall construction — plaster holds moisture deep in its matrix and releases it slowly, so we monitor daily readings rather than pulling equipment on a fixed schedule.

**Step 4 — Affected material removal.** Saturated insulation, wicking baseboards, and compromised drywall or plaster are removed to the flood cut line. We document everything removed for your insurance claim.

**Step 5 — Antimicrobial treatment and clearance.** Affected framing and concrete surfaces are treated before reconstruction begins. We do not close walls until moisture readings confirm the structure is within acceptable drying standards per the IICRC S500.

## Ridgewood Insurance Coordination

Flood losses in Ridgewood frequently involve two separate policies: a standard homeowner's policy (which typically covers sudden and accidental discharge, like a burst pipe) and a separate NFIP or private flood policy (which covers rising water from the Ho-Ho-Kus Brook or Saddle River). We document the loss origin carefully because the coverage path depends on whether water entered from above, from a failed internal system, or from ground-level flooding — and adjusters will ask. In Ridgewood's older finished basements — particularly in the Heights and Willard neighborhoods where homes sit on full stone-foundation footings — we routinely find that the original floor slab was poured directly over fill without a vapor barrier.
