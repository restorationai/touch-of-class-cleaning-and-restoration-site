## Why Somerville Properties Face Distinct Reconstruction Challenges

Somerville's housing stock tells its age. The residential blocks radiating out from Downtown — East Main, West End, Fairview — are dominated by early-1900s two-family frames and Victorians built with balloon framing, plaster-and-lath walls, and galvanized supply lines that are well past their service life. When those pipes finally fail, water doesn't stay in one room. It travels inside wall cavities from the second floor to the finished basement before anyone notices the ceiling stain. By the time reconstruction begins, the scope often includes structural members that absorbed moisture for days, not hours.

The commercial corridor adds a different set of problems. Storefronts along the Main Street business district frequently have older flat or low-slope roofs where a single overnight failure can saturate ceiling tile, subfloor, and inventory simultaneously. Sprinkler discharge losses — triggered by freezing in unheated storage areas during a hard January — are a recurring pattern in those buildings. Reconstruction in that context means coordinating with the building's sprinkler contractor, the borough's construction office for permits, and the tenant's schedule, all at once.

Then there's flood exposure. The Raritan River and Peters Brook flank the borough on two sides, and low-lying areas in the 08876 ZIP code saw serious inundation during Tropical Storm Ida in 2021 and again during Floyd and Irene before that. Flood-affected reconstruction in Somerville isn't just about replacing drywall — it often involves elevating mechanical systems, installing flood vents in foundation walls, and documenting substantial improvement calculations to satisfy FEMA floodplain requirements before the borough will issue a permit.

## Our Reconstruction Process in Somerville
