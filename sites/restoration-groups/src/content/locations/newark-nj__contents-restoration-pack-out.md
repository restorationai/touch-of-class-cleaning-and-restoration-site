When a basement floods in the Ironbound or a kitchen fire chars the top two floors of a Forest Hill colonial, the damage you see on the walls is rarely the whole story. Furniture, clothing, documents, electronics, and irreplaceable keepsakes absorb smoke, soot, and water in ways that aren't obvious until the odor returns weeks later or a photograph buckles inside a frame. Contents restoration and pack-out is the discipline of treating those objects — not just the structure — and in a city built on dense pre-war housing stock, getting it right requires a process tuned to Newark's specific conditions.

## Why Newark Properties Face Distinct Contents Challenges

Newark's housing is overwhelmingly pre-1960 construction: brick multifamily buildings, wood-frame two- and three-families, and the grander colonials that line the streets of Forest Hill. In that kind of stock, a single riser failure can push water through three floors before anyone notices. Combined sewer systems — common across much of the city — back up into ground-floor units during heavy rainfall, leaving furniture and stored belongings exposed to Category 3 contaminated water. Hurricane Ida demonstrated how hard the Ironbound's low-lying blocks along the Passaic River can flood; basements that took on sewage-laden water require a different decontamination protocol than a clean-water pipe burst.

Smoke behaves differently in dense urban construction as well. When fire travels between closely set buildings, smoke and soot infiltrate neighboring units through shared wall cavities, attic spaces, and HVAC chases — meaning contents in units that never caught fire still carry embedded odor and fine particulate that standard cleaning won't resolve.

## Our Pack-Out and Restoration Process

The first step on any Newark job is a room-by-room contents inventory before anything is moved. Every item is photographed, catalogued, and assigned a condition code — this documentation is what your insurance adjuster needs to process a contents claim accurately, and it protects you if questions arise later.

Items that can be safely restored are packed using materials rated for the specific contaminant type — dry-cleaning bags for soot-affected textiles, sealed plastic bins for water-damaged goods — and transported to a climate-controlled facility where the actual restoration work happens off-site. This matters in Newark's dense neighborhoods, where on-site drying and cleaning space is often limited to a single room or a narrow hallway. Working off-site also keeps the structure accessible for simultaneous structural drying or remediation.

At the facility, contents go through ultrasonic cleaning, ozone or hydroxyl treatment for odor, freeze-drying for documents and photographs, and HEPA-filtered dry-cleaning for textiles. Items that cannot be restored are documented with replacement cost values so your adjuster can process them without dispute. Once the structure is cleared, contents are returned, unwrapped, and placed according to the original inventory.

Contents claims in New Jersey tend to generate more line-item disputes than structural claims, partly because adjusters apply depreciation schedules differently to personal property.
