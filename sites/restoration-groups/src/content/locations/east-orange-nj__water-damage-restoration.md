## Why East Orange Properties See Repeated Water Losses

East Orange's housing stock is one of the densest concentrations of early-20th-century multifamily construction in Essex County. The brick apartment buildings and two-families that line the streets around Ampere and Elmwood were built when galvanized steel pipe was standard — and that pipe is now 80 to 100 years old. Galvanized pipe corrodes from the inside out, narrowing over decades until it finally splits under winter pressure spikes or a sudden temperature drop. When it goes, the failure rarely stays contained to one unit.

Flat and low-slope roofs compound the problem. Ponding water after a heavy rain finds every seam and penetration, and by the time a ceiling stain appears on the third floor, water has often been sitting inside the roof assembly for days. Basement mechanical rooms — common in the postwar infill buildings near Presidential Estates — flood during heavy rain events because the combined sewer system in low-lying areas backs up before street drainage can keep pace. East Orange saw that pattern play out dramatically during Hurricane Ida in 2021, when Second River tributaries pushed water into ground-floor and below-grade spaces across the city's lower elevations.

Sprinkler activations are another recurring source of loss along the Central Avenue and Main Street commercial corridors and near the East Orange General Hospital campus. A single head discharge in a commercial corridor building can release 15 to 25 gallons per minute — enough to saturate multiple floors before the system is shut off.

## Our Water Damage Restoration Process in East Orange

Every job starts with a moisture map, not a guess. When we arrive, we use thermal imaging cameras and calibrated moisture meters to trace exactly where water has traveled — through plaster, into subfloor assemblies, behind tile, and along the inside faces of brick walls. In East Orange's older buildings, water routinely moves further than it looks because original plaster is highly absorbent and brick cavity walls act as hidden channels.

Once the scope is confirmed, water removal begins with truck-mounted extraction equipment capable of pulling standing water from hardwood floors, carpet, and concrete slab areas simultaneously. Structural drying follows using commercial-grade desiccant and refrigerant dehumidifiers positioned to create a controlled drying chamber — airflow is calculated per the IICRC S500 standard, not estimated. We set and monitor daily drying logs, adjusting equipment placement as materials release moisture over the drying cycle.

For multi-unit buildings — which make up a large share of East Orange's residential stock — we coordinate access with building superintendents and property managers to inspect adjacent units above, below, and beside the primary loss area. Water that has migrated into a neighboring unit and gone unaddressed is one of the most common reasons a restoration job has to be reopened weeks later.

## Local Note: Plaster Walls and Hidden Moisture in East Orange's Older Buildings

One thing that surprises property owners in East Orange's prewar buildings is how long structural drying actually takes compared to a newer home. Modern drywall releases moisture relatively quickly once dehumidification is running. Original three-coat plaster over wood lath — the wall system in most of the Victorians and brick walk-ups in Doddtown and the blocks around Cicely Tyson School — holds water like a sponge and releases it slowly, sometimes over 7 to 10 days rather than the 3 to 4 days typical for drywall construction. We account for this in our drying projections upfront so that property owners and insurance adjusters aren't caught off guard when the job takes longer than a comparable loss in a newer building would.
