## Our Water Cleanup Process in Union

The first thing a crew does on arrival is classify the water source — clean supply line, gray-water appliance, or black-water intrusion from a backed drain or exterior flood. That classification drives every decision that follows, including what PPE is required, whether contents can be moved in place or must be removed, and how aggressively porous materials need to be treated.

After classification, we map moisture with thermal imaging and calibrated meters. In Union's older homes, this step matters more than it does in newer construction: plaster-and-lath walls, common in homes built before the mid-1960s in neighborhoods like Battle Hill and Union Center, absorb water differently than paper-faced drywall. The surface can read close to dry while the lath behind it stays saturated — a reading-only approach misses that, and a premature pack-out leads to mold growth inside a wall that looked fine on inspection day.

Extraction comes next, followed by the placement of drying equipment calibrated to the actual cubic footage and material types in the affected space. Drying logs are recorded daily and shared with the homeowner and, where applicable, the insurance adjuster. We don't pull equipment until moisture readings confirm the structure has returned to normal levels — not when a timeline says it should be dry.

## Local Note

One thing worth knowing about Union's postwar split-levels: the half-level design means the lower level is often only partially below grade, with exterior walls that are part concrete block and part wood-framed. When water enters through a window well or a failed sump, it hits both materials at once. Concrete block wicks moisture laterally and holds it for a long time; the wood framing above it dries on a different schedule entirely. Drying equipment placed only in the open floor area of that lower level often misses the block-wall saturation entirely. Effective drying in these homes requires equipment positioned against the block, not just in the center of the room — a detail that makes a real difference in final moisture readings and in whether the wall needs to come out.
