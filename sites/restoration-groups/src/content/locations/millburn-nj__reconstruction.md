## Why Millburn Properties Present Unique Reconstruction Challenges

Millburn's building stock is part of what makes it one of Essex County's most desirable addresses — and one of the more demanding environments for post-damage rebuilding. The large colonials and Tudor-style estates in Short Hills and Old Short Hills were built primarily between 1910 and 1950, a period when construction meant plaster-and-lath walls, old-growth lumber framing, cast-iron drain stacks, and copper supply lines that have now been in service for 70-plus years. When a pipe fails or a fire damages a structure in that housing stock, reconstruction isn't a matter of matching modern materials to modern framing. It requires understanding how those older assemblies were built, sourcing materials that meet current code while respecting original character, and pulling permits through the Millburn Township Building Department — which applies New Jersey's Uniform Construction Code with attention to detail that reflects the municipality's expectations for its residential neighborhoods.

The Millburn Avenue retail corridor and properties near The Mall at Short Hills introduce a separate set of commercial considerations. Sprinkler-discharge events in those buildings can saturate structural assemblies across multiple tenant spaces, and reconstruction timelines are compressed by lease obligations and revenue loss. Structural reconstruction in a mixed-use or retail context means coordinating phased work so portions of a building can return to operation while other sections are still being rebuilt.

## Our Reconstruction Process in Millburn

Every reconstruction engagement starts with a documented scope — not an estimate built on assumptions, but a line-by-line assessment of what structural and finish elements were damaged, what code compliance requires, and what the insurance carrier's adjuster has documented. As an IICRC Certified Firm (#210213) and NJ Licensed Home Improvement Contractor, the work we produce is permitted, inspected, and backed by documentation that holds up through the claims process.

From there, the sequence typically runs: structural stabilization and temporary weatherproofing if the building envelope is compromised; demolition of unsalvageable materials with proper disposal; framing repair or replacement; rough mechanical, electrical, and plumbing work inspected before close-in; insulation and air-barrier installation to current energy code; drywall or plaster restoration; finish carpentry, flooring, and painting. In older Millburn homes where original millwork and hardwood floors are a significant part of the property's value, we work with subcontractors who specialize in period-appropriate restoration rather than substituting modern profiles that won't match.

## Local Note

One thing that surprises property owners in the Wyoming and Glenwood sections of Millburn: the clay-heavy soil underlying much of this part of town retains moisture against foundation walls long after a flood event recedes. When reconstruction involves opening up basement or crawlspace assemblies, we consistently find that framing and sheathing in contact with or near the foundation have absorbed far more moisture than the visible damage suggests. Skipping a thorough drying phase before closing in new framing is how callbacks happen six months later. We use thermal imaging and calibrated moisture meters at every stage of close-in, not just at the beginning of the job, because in these homes the ground itself keeps pushing moisture back into the structure.
