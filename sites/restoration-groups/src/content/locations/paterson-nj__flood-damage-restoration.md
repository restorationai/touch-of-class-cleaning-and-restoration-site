## Why Paterson Properties See Repeated Flood Damage

Paterson's geography sets the stage. The city sits directly on the Passaic River, and the terrain drops sharply from the ridge neighborhoods like Hillcrest and Lakeview down to the flats near the water. That elevation change funnels runoff fast, overwhelming aging combined sewer lines that were never designed for modern storm volumes. When the system backs up, water doesn't just pool in yards — it pushes through floor drains and toilet bases into finished basements.

The housing stock compounds the problem. Much of Paterson's residential fabric dates to the late 1800s and early 1900s, built to house workers from the silk mills that once lined the river. Those 19th-century brick rowhouses and two- and three-family frame walkups — dense in areas like Sandy Hill and South Paterson — were built with cast-iron drain lines, unlined masonry foundations, and crawl spaces that were never intended to stay dry. Converted silk-era industrial buildings, now apartment complexes, add another layer: aging standpipe and sprinkler systems that can fail quietly, releasing hundreds of gallons before anyone notices.

Then there's the rental density. Paterson's overcrowded rental market means basements and lower units are frequently occupied and often unventilated — conditions where a single flood event can trigger mold colonization within 24 to 48 hours if water isn't extracted and drying equipment deployed quickly.

## Our Flood Damage Restoration Process in Paterson

Every flood job starts with a moisture assessment — not a visual walk-through, but thermal imaging and calibrated moisture meters that map saturation inside wall cavities, subfloor assemblies, and behind tile. In Paterson's older homes, that step is especially important because water travels unpredictably through rubble-stone foundations and hollow-core plaster walls.

Once we understand the full scope, we move through a structured sequence:

- **Water extraction**: truck-mounted and portable extractors pull standing water from floors, carpets, and saturated insulation. In multi-family buildings, we coordinate access across units when water has migrated between floors.
- **Controlled demolition**: saturated drywall, flooring, and insulation that cannot be dried in place are removed to the flood cut line. In Paterson's pre-1950 homes, we frequently encounter plaster-and-lath construction, which we handle carefully — plaster holds moisture longer than drywall and requires extended drying cycles.
- **Structural drying**: industrial desiccant dehumidifiers and high-velocity air movers run in a closed drying system. We set drying goals based on IICRC S500 standards and monitor daily with moisture logs that become part of your insurance documentation.
- **Antimicrobial treatment**: applied to all affected surfaces before any reconstruction begins, given the category of water typical in river-adjacent flood events.
- **Reconstruction**: once materials reach dry standard, we rebuild — flooring, drywall, insulation, paint — so you're dealing with one contractor from extraction through finished repair.

## Local Note

Paterson's converted mill buildings — particularly those repurposed as residential lofts and apartment complexes near the Great Falls National Historical Park corridor — often have wet-pipe sprinkler systems tied to aging municipal water supplies. When a head activates or a fitting fails, the discharge can run for 20 to 30 minutes before the building's shutoff is located, releasing thousands of gallons across concrete slab floors that have no drainage. Because the slabs are often polished or coated, water spreads laterally under partition walls rather than draining, saturating adjacent units on the same floor. We've learned to check neighboring units immediately on these jobs — what looks like a single-unit loss frequently isn't.
