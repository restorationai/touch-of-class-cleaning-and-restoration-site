## Why Clifton Properties See Repeated Water Damage

Clifton sits at a geographic crossroads that makes water intrusion a recurring reality rather than a once-in-a-generation event. The Passaic River and Weasel Brook form natural flood boundaries along the city's eastern and southern edges, and the low-lying neighborhoods of Lakeview and Delawanna have absorbed serious floodwater during major storm events — most memorably during Hurricanes Irene and Ida, when the Passaic overran its banks and pushed water into homes, garages, and industrial lots near Route 21. Even between named storms, heavy rainfall overwhelms the storm sewer infrastructure in older sections of the city, backing water into basements that were never designed to stay dry under those conditions.

The housing stock itself is a compounding factor. Postwar capes and colonials throughout ZIP codes 07011 and 07013 were built with materials and plumbing systems that are now at or past their service life. Cast-iron drain stacks corrode from the inside out; galvanized supply lines develop pinhole leaks inside wall cavities where no one notices until drywall is already saturated. In Botany Village, older brick multi-family buildings present a different challenge — brick and mortar wick moisture laterally, spreading a single-unit leak into adjacent units before the source is even identified. On the Route 3 commercial corridor and around Styertowne Shopping Center, overnight sprinkler discharges and roof membrane failures create large-footprint commercial losses that require industrial-scale extraction equipment and rapid documentation for property managers.

## Our Water Damage Restoration Process in Clifton

Every job starts with a thorough moisture assessment — not just the visible wet area, but the full migration path water has traveled. In a split-level home in Richfield or Athenia, that often means checking the subfloor cavity between the garage slab and the living level above, where water can pool invisibly for days. We use thermal imaging cameras and calibrated moisture meters to map the extent of saturation before a single piece of equipment is placed.

Once the scope is confirmed, water extraction begins using truck-mounted and portable extraction units capable of pulling standing water from carpet, hardwood, and concrete simultaneously. Structural drying follows the IICRC S500 standard — the industry benchmark for drying protocols — using commercial-grade desiccant and refrigerant dehumidifiers paired with high-velocity air movers positioned to create directed airflow through wall cavities and under flooring assemblies. We set daily moisture targets and return to verify readings, adjusting equipment placement until affected materials reach documented dry standard. Nothing gets closed up until the numbers confirm it.

For homes with original plaster walls — still common in older sections of Clifton — drying timelines run longer than they would in a newer build. Plaster holds moisture differently than gypsum drywall, releasing it more slowly even under aggressive airflow. We account for this in our drying projections rather than pulling equipment prematurely.

## Local Note: What Clifton's Basement Layouts Mean for Drying

One pattern we see repeatedly in Clifton's postwar split-levels and raised ranches: the finished basement shares a concrete block foundation wall with an unfinished mechanical room or crawl space section. When water enters through a window well or floor crack, it saturates the block wall, which then wicks moisture into the finished side through the framing — even after surface water is removed. If that block wall isn't addressed as part of the drying scope, the finished basement can test dry at the drywall surface while the framing behind it remains elevated for weeks. We treat the full wall assembly, not just the visible finish layer.

Most homeowner policies in New Jersey cover sudden and accidental water damage — burst pipes, appliance failures, and certain storm-related intrusion events — but flood damage from the Passaic River overflowing typically falls under separate NFIP flood coverage.
