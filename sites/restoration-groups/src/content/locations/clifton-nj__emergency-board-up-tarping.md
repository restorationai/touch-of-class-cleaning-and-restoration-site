## Why Clifton Properties Are Especially Vulnerable After a Loss

Clifton's housing tells the story of postwar New Jersey: capes, colonials, and split-levels packed onto modest lots from 07011 through 07014, most of them built when lumber was old-growth and windows were single-pane wood sash. That original construction is beautiful in a lot of ways, but it creates real problems after a fire or storm event. Wood-sash windows in older frames rarely accept a standard plywood panel cleanly — the rough openings are irregular, the sills are soft from decades of paint and moisture, and forcing a poorly fitted board can cause additional damage to the frame itself.

The Lakeview and Delawanna sections add a flood dimension. Both neighborhoods sit close to Weasel Brook and the Passaic River corridor, and anyone who lived through Irene or Ida knows how fast the low-lying streets near Route 21 can go underwater. After a flood event, ground-floor doors and windows are often compromised — swollen, warped, or simply blown out by hydrostatic pressure — and tarping a roof while standing water is still receding from the lot requires equipment staging that accounts for soft, saturated ground.

Along the Route 3 commercial corridor and around Styertowne, the loss profile shifts: overnight sprinkler discharges, roof membrane failures on flat commercial roofs, and smash-and-grab break-ins that leave storefronts open to the elements. Those jobs move fast and often involve coordinating with property managers rather than homeowners.

## Our Emergency Board-Up and Tarping Process in Clifton

Every job starts with a rapid site assessment — not a clipboard walkthrough, but a structural read of what's open, what's at risk of opening further, and what sequence of work protects the most square footage fastest. For residential fire losses, that typically means boarding compromised windows and exterior doors with 3/4-inch CDX plywood, secured with structural screws rather than nails so the panels can be removed cleanly once permanent repairs begin. On older Clifton homes with irregular openings, we cut panels to fit on-site rather than relying on stock sizes.

Roof tarping follows a similar logic. We use reinforced polyethylene tarps rated for extended outdoor exposure, weighted and fastened at the ridge and eaves rather than just draped and hoped for. A tarp that lifts in a 40 mph gust over a Richfield colonial the night after a fire does nothing. Fastening methods are calibrated to the roof deck condition — on a storm-damaged roof with compromised sheathing, we anchor into structural members, not just the decking surface.

For commercial properties, we can deploy temporary fencing and hoarding panels alongside board-up work, which matters for liability on high-foot-traffic corridors like Route 3.

## Insurance Documentation and Coordination

Board-up and tarping work is almost always covered under the emergency services provision of a standard homeowner's policy, and commercial property policies typically include similar language. We photograph every opening before and after boarding, document materials used, and provide a written scope that aligns with the format most NJ carriers expect. That documentation matters: an adjuster who arrives to find a properly secured and photographed loss settles faster than one who's working from memory and a phone photo. We work with most major carriers and can communicate directly with your adjuster if that helps move the claim.
