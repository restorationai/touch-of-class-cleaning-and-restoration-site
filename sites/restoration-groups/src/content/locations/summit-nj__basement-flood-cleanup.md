## Why Summit Basements Flood the Way They Do

The housing stock in 07901 tells the story. Most of Summit's residential blocks were built between the 1890s and the 1930s, when deep, fully excavated basements were standard — often finished with plaster walls, hardwood floors, and millwork that matches the rooms above. Those basements were built to last, but not to withstand the drainage loads that come with today's impervious surfaces and aging infrastructure.

Three failure patterns show up repeatedly in Summit:

**Stormwater intrusion through foundation walls.** Steep lots along the Brayton School area and the hillside streets above Springfield Avenue channel runoff directly against foundation walls. Original drainage systems — clay tile, hand-laid stone — were sized for a different era. When they fail or clog, hydrostatic pressure pushes water through mortar joints that look solid until they're not.

**Sewer lateral backups.** Summit's tree canopy is one of its great assets; the root systems under those trees are not. Clay sewer laterals installed before World War II are routinely compromised by root intrusion, and a single heavy rain event can overwhelm the system and send sewage-contaminated water back through floor drains. This is a Category 3 contamination situation — it requires a different response than a clean water pipe burst.

**Burst supply and radiator lines in older wings.** During hard freeze events, uninsulated plumbing in older additions and attic-fed radiator systems can fail inside walls. By the time water appears in the basement, it has often traveled through plaster and lath on multiple floors.

## Our Basement Flood Cleanup Process in Summit

The process starts with a thorough moisture assessment before any equipment is placed. In Summit's older homes, surface readings lie — a plaster wall can feel dry to the touch while the lath behind it holds significant moisture. Thermal imaging and penetrating moisture meters give a complete picture of where water has traveled, which matters enormously when you're dealing with original hardwood floors and built-in millwork that can't simply be replaced from a lumber yard.

Water source classification comes next. Clean water from a burst supply line is handled differently than a backed-up sewer lateral. Category 3 losses require EPA-compliant containment, appropriate PPE, and antimicrobial treatment protocols — steps that protect both the occupants and the crew.

Extraction, structural drying, and monitoring follow a plan calibrated to the specific materials in the space. Plaster releases moisture more slowly than modern drywall, and original hardwood flooring requires controlled drying conditions to avoid cupping and delamination. Drying equipment is positioned based on the room geometry, not a generic template, and moisture readings are logged at each visit so the drying curve is documented — something insurance adjusters and homeowners both need to see.

Final clearance is confirmed with the same instruments used at the start, not by visual inspection alone.
