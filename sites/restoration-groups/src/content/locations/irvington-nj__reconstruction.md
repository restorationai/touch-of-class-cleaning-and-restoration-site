## Why Irvington Properties See Recurring Reconstruction Needs

Irvington's housing stock is overwhelmingly early-1900s construction — two- and three-family frame homes, attached rowhouses, and prewar brick multifamilies packed tightly along streets like Grove Park and through Irvington Center. The original galvanized supply lines in many of these homes have long exceeded their useful life, and cast-iron drain stacks are corroding from the inside out. When those systems fail — and they do, often overnight — water migrates fast through shared wall cavities, saturates subfloor assemblies, and reaches finished and semi-finished basements that are common in this housing stock.

Reconstruction in that context isn't cosmetic — it's structural, and it has to be permitted and inspected accordingly.

## Our Reconstruction Process in Irvington

Every reconstruction project begins with a scope document — a line-by-line breakdown of what was damaged, what needs to be removed, what can be dried and retained, and what has to be rebuilt to meet current New Jersey residential or commercial building code. That scope drives the permit application, the material selections, and the subcontractor schedule.

For Irvington's older multifamily homes, that process typically involves a few layers of complexity that don't come up in newer construction:

- **Lead paint and plaster**: Pre-1978 homes require lead-safe work practices under EPA RRP rules. Many of Irvington's homes have original plaster walls, which behave differently from modern drywall — they're denser, they hold moisture longer, and they require careful removal to avoid disturbing underlying lead-painted surfaces.
- **Structural framing assessment**: Balloon-frame construction, common in early-1900s homes, allows fire and water to travel vertically through wall cavities with little resistance. After any significant loss, we assess whether framing members need sistering or replacement before closing walls.
- **Code-compliant upgrades**: A reconstruction permit in NJ often triggers requirements to bring affected systems up to current code — GFCI outlets in wet areas, updated egress windows in basement bedrooms, smoke and CO detector placement. We build those into the scope upfront so there are no surprises at final inspection.

Work is coordinated around tenant occupancy wherever possible. In a three-family home, keeping two units livable while rebuilding the third isn't optional — it's a financial necessity for the property owner.
