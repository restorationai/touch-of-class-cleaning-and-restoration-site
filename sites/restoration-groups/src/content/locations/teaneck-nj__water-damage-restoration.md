## Our Water Damage Restoration Process in Teaneck

Every job starts with a thorough moisture mapping of the structure — not just the visible wet area. In Teaneck's older homes, water routinely travels farther than it appears. We use thermal imaging cameras and calibrated moisture meters to trace migration through plaster, brick cavity walls, and original wood framing before a single piece of equipment is placed.

Extraction comes next. Standing water is removed with truck-mounted and portable extraction units, including specialty tools for subfloor cavities common in 1940s construction. Once bulk water is out, we establish a drying system calibrated to the actual materials present — desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers, air movers positioned to create directed airflow, and in-wall drying panels where plaster walls have absorbed significant moisture. Drying progress is logged daily against IICRC S500 standard targets so you and your insurance adjuster have a documented record of the job.

For sewer-backup events — which are disproportionately common in Teaneck's older drain systems — Category 3 (black water) protocols apply. Affected porous materials are removed, the structure is disinfected, and air quality is monitored before reconstruction begins.

## Local Note: Plaster Walls and Drying Time

Teaneck's pre-war homes are full of original plaster-on-lath walls, and they behave very differently from modern drywall when wet. Plaster absorbs moisture slowly but releases it even more slowly — a wall that reads dry on the surface can still hold significant moisture in the lath layer and the wood framing behind it. We've seen plaster walls in the Grange and West Englewood take 30 to 40 percent longer to reach drying targets than comparable drywall structures. Rushing that process and closing a job early is exactly how hidden mold colonization starts within 48 to 72 hours. Our daily moisture logs track the full drying curve so nothing gets signed off until the numbers confirm it.
