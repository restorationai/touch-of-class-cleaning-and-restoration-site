## Why Staten Island Properties See Persistent Odor Problems

The borough's housing stock tells the story. From the early-1900s frame homes in Port Richmond to the postwar split-levels clustered around New Dorp, a significant share of Staten Island's residential buildings were constructed before modern vapor barriers and drainage membranes were standard. Basements and crawlspaces in these homes absorb moisture seasonally, and aging cast-iron drain lines — common throughout the South Shore — are prone to slow leaks that go undetected for months. By the time a homeowner notices a smell, the source has often been colonizing porous materials like subfloor sheathing, floor joists, or plaster lath for weeks.

The East Shore adds another layer. Neighborhoods from South Beach through Oakwood Beach were inundated during Sandy in 2012, and the flash flooding that followed Ida in 2021 pushed water into finished basements that had been dry for years. Floodwater odor is chemically different from a simple moisture problem — it carries sewage bacteria, heavy sediment, and decomposing organic matter — and it requires a different treatment approach than a standard musty-basement job.

Commercial properties along Hylan Boulevard and Forest Avenue face their own pattern: roof-leak and sprinkler-discharge events that saturate drop ceilings and insulation, leaving behind a stale, wet-cardboard smell that lingers long after the visible water is gone.

## Our Odor Removal and Deodorization Process

Effective deodorization isn't a single step — it's a sequence calibrated to the odor source and the building materials involved. We begin with a full assessment to locate the origin: thermal imaging to find moisture still trapped in walls, moisture meters on subfloor and framing, and a room-by-room inspection to map odor intensity. Masking a smell without addressing the source just delays the problem.

Once the source is confirmed and any wet material is dried or removed, we select the treatment method based on what we're dealing with:

- **Thermal fogging** penetrates porous materials — wood framing, drywall, upholstery — by converting deodorizing solution into a fine fog that follows the same pathways smoke or sewer gas traveled. It's particularly effective in the older plaster-and-lath construction common in Port Richmond and St. George.
- **Hydroxyl deodorization** uses UV-generated hydroxyl radicals to break down odor-causing molecules at a chemical level. Unlike ozone treatment, hydroxyl generators are safe to run in occupied or semi-occupied spaces, which matters in multi-unit buildings where relocating all residents isn't practical.
- **Ozone treatment** is reserved for unoccupied spaces with severe smoke or biological odors — it's highly effective but requires the space to be vacated and properly ventilated before re-entry.
- **Encapsulants and sealers** are applied to structural wood or concrete surfaces where odor compounds have absorbed deeply and mechanical treatment alone won't fully neutralize them.

We document each phase with photos and moisture readings, which supports insurance claims and gives you a clear record of what was done.

## Local Note: What Older Staten Island Homes Do to Odor Treatments

One thing crews learn quickly on Staten Island: the plaster walls in pre-1950 homes in neighborhoods like St. George and Port Richmond absorb smoke and sewer odors differently than modern drywall. The plaster itself is dense and slow to off-gas, which means a single thermal fogging pass often isn't enough — the fog needs time to dwell, and a follow-up hydroxyl treatment over 24 to 48 hours is frequently necessary to pull the remaining odor out of the wall mass. Skipping that second step is the most common reason a homeowner calls a second company after the first one said the job was done.
