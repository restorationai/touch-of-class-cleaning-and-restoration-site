## Our Storm Damage Restoration Process in East Orange

Every job starts with a thorough damage assessment — not just the obvious breach point, but the full moisture map. In East Orange's multi-unit buildings, water from a roof failure or a downed tree can travel laterally through shared framing and masonry before it shows up as a stain on a second-floor ceiling. We use thermal imaging and calibrated moisture meters to trace the actual migration path, not just the visible damage.

Once the scope is documented, the sequence typically runs:

1. **Emergency stabilization** — tarping compromised roofs, boarding broken windows and doors, extracting standing water from affected units and basement spaces.
2. **Structural drying** — commercial-grade desiccant dehumidifiers and high-velocity air movers positioned to dry framing cavities, not just surface materials. In brick masonry buildings, drying timelines run longer than in wood-frame construction because the thermal mass of the wall assembly holds moisture.
3. **Debris and material removal** — storm-damaged roofing, saturated insulation, and compromised drywall or plaster removed and documented for the insurance claim.
4. **Repair and reconstruction** — from re-roofing and window replacement to interior finish work, coordinated under our NJ Licensed Home Improvement Contractor license so the job doesn't hand off to an unknown subcontractor mid-stream.

Throughout, the job file is built to support your insurance claim: timestamped photos, moisture logs, and itemized scope of work in the format adjusters expect.

## Local Note: Flat Roofs and Interior Drains in East Orange Walk-Ups

One thing that catches out-of-area contractors off guard in East Orange is the interior roof drain system common in the city's pre-war apartment buildings. Unlike residential sloped roofs that shed water to gutters at the eave, these flat roofs drain through cast-iron standpipes that run down through the building's interior — sometimes through closets, sometimes through finished walls. When a storm overwhelms or blocks those drains, water doesn't just leak at the roof membrane; it backs up and can overflow at every floor penetration on the way down.
