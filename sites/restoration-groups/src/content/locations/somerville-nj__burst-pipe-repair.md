## Why Somerville Properties See Burst Pipe Issues

Somerville is Somerset County's compact borough seat, and most of its residential neighborhoods were built between roughly 1890 and 1950. That era of construction means galvanized steel supply lines that corrode from the inside, reducing flow and wall thickness until a cold snap or a pressure spike causes a failure. In the West End and along Mercer Street, finished basements are common — and a finished basement is the worst place for a pipe to let go, because water wicks into framing, insulation, and drywall before it ever reaches a floor drain.

The borough's newer downtown apartment redevelopment along the Main Street corridor introduces a different failure mode: multi-unit buildings with shared riser lines, where a single supply-line break on an upper floor can affect multiple units before building management locates the isolation valve. Somerville's position between the Raritan River and Peters Brook also means that when a pipe failure coincides with saturated ground — as it did during the remnants of Ida in 2021 — drainage paths that normally carry water away from foundations are already overwhelmed. That combination turns a contained plumbing failure into a multi-source water intrusion event.

One thing that catches homeowners in Somerville's older two-family and Victorian stock off guard: the original galvanized supply lines often run through interior wall cavities that were never insulated, because when those homes were built, interior walls weren't expected to need insulation.
