## Why Staten Island's Housing Stock Shapes Biohazard Response

A significant share of Staten Island's residential properties were built between the 1940s and 1970s. That era of construction relied on materials — hardwood subfloors over open joists, cellulose insulation, cast-iron drain lines — that absorb and retain biological contamination in ways that modern engineered materials do not. When a biohazard situation occurs in a home of this vintage, the affected area rarely stops at what's visible. Contamination can migrate through flooring gaps, into wall cavities, and along drain paths before anyone realizes the scope.

The borough's basement and crawlspace prevalence adds another layer. Unlike slab-on-grade construction common in newer developments, raised foundations allow biological material and associated pathogens to reach structural members below the living space. Thorough remediation in these homes requires inspection below the subfloor, not just above it.

## Our Biohazard Cleanup Process in Staten Island

Every job begins with a contained assessment — technicians suited in appropriate PPE evaluate the full extent of affected surfaces before any materials are disturbed. This matters especially in older Staten Island homes where a single room may connect to shared wall cavities or an unfinished basement below.

## Local Note

In Staten Island's older frame homes — particularly in neighborhoods like Great Kills and New Dorp where postwar construction is dense — interior walls are frequently balloon-framed rather than platform-framed. In balloon framing, wall cavities run continuously from the basement sill plate to the roof, meaning biological contamination at one floor level can travel vertically through the wall with no fire block or horizontal barrier to stop it. Technicians working in these homes check cavity access points at multiple levels before signing off on a remediation, because what reads as a contained first-floor situation can involve the wall cavity two floors up.
