## Why Paterson Properties Face Distinct Reconstruction Challenges

Paterson's geography sets the stage for repeated structural losses. The city sits directly on the Passaic River, and low-lying blocks below the Great Falls have flooded catastrophically — during Hurricane Irene in 2011 and again during Ida in 2021, water submerged ground floors and basement units across the Riverside and downtown sections, saturating load-bearing sill plates and floor systems that had been in place for 80 or more years. After that kind of event, reconstruction isn't a cosmetic refresh; it's a structural reassessment.

Beyond flooding, Paterson's housing stock introduces complications that don't appear on a standard scope of work. Knob-and-tube wiring remnants in pre-1940 frame houses require coordination with a licensed electrician before walls can be closed. Cast-iron drain stacks in older multi-families often need replacement rather than repair once they're exposed during gut-outs. And the converted industrial buildings near the mill district — many now residential — carry sprinkler and standpipe systems that generate their own category of water loss when they fail, soaking multiple floors before the water is shut off.

## Our Reconstruction Process in Paterson

Every reconstruction project begins with a documented damage assessment — not a visual walkthrough, but a systematic evaluation of structural members, subfloor integrity, wall cavity moisture, and any hazardous materials that NJ regulations require to be addressed before rebuilding starts. In Paterson's older housing stock, that step frequently surfaces lead paint or asbestos-containing materials in floor tiles and pipe insulation, which must be properly abated before framing work proceeds.

From there, the process moves in a logical sequence:

1. **Structural stabilization** — temporary shoring where needed, removal of compromised framing, and documentation for the insurance carrier.
2. **Rough systems coordination** — scheduling licensed subcontractors for electrical, plumbing, and HVAC rough-in before walls close, which is especially critical in multi-family buildings where shared systems affect multiple units.
3. **Insulation and air sealing** — Paterson's older building envelopes are notoriously leaky; reconstruction is an opportunity to bring performance closer to current code without a full gut renovation.
4. **Finish work and final inspection** — drywall, flooring, trim, painting, and coordination with Paterson's building department for required inspections before the certificate of occupancy is restored.

As an NJ Licensed Home Improvement Contractor registered with the NJ Division of Consumer Affairs, The Restoration Group pulls the required permits and manages the inspection schedule so property owners aren't navigating that process alone.

## Local Note

In Paterson's brick rowhouse blocks — particularly in Sandy Hill and the blocks radiating out from Passaic County Community College — exterior brick wythes are often the only thing separating interior framing from the elements. When fire or flood damage requires partial wall demolition, it's common to find that the brick veneer and the structural framing have been moving independently for decades, with mortar joints that haven't been repointed since the Eisenhower administration. Closing out a reconstruction scope without addressing those joints means the rebuilt interior will face accelerated moisture intrusion within a few seasons. The Restoration Group flags these conditions during the assessment phase and includes repointing in the scope where it's structurally warranted — not as an upsell, but because skipping it means the work doesn't hold.
