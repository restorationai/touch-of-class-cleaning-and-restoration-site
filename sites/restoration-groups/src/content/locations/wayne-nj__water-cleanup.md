## Why Wayne Properties See Recurring Water Damage

Wayne's geography works against its homeowners in a few specific ways. The township sits at the confluence of two flood-prone river systems, and large portions of the 07470 zip code fall within FEMA-designated special flood hazard areas. That means water intrusion here isn't a freak event — it's a recurring seasonal risk, particularly in spring snowmelt and during late-summer tropical systems.

Beyond the rivers, the lake communities add a second layer of exposure. Homes near Pines Lake and Packanack Lake were largely built in the 1950s through early 1970s, often with block or poured-concrete foundations that weren't designed with modern waterproofing membranes. Finished lower levels — rec rooms, home offices, in-law suites — are common, and they're the first spaces to take on water when a sump fails or a window well backs up. The plumbing in these homes is aging too: galvanized supply lines and cast-iron drain stacks that corrode from the inside out, sometimes rupturing without any visible exterior warning.

Commercial properties along the Route 23 and Route 46 corridors face a different set of risks — roof-drain backups, sprinkler-head failures, and HVAC condensate line overflows that can saturate drop-ceiling tiles and subfloor assemblies across large retail footprints.

## Our Water Cleanup Process in Wayne

Water damage moves faster than most people expect. Within the first 24 hours, saturated drywall begins to soften, wood subfloor panels start to swell at the seams, and — depending on the moisture load — microbial activity can begin establishing itself in wall cavities. Speed and sequencing matter.

When a crew arrives, the first priority is stopping active intrusion if it hasn't been addressed, then assessing the full moisture boundary using thermal imaging and calibrated moisture meters. In Wayne's older housing stock, this step takes longer than it does in newer construction: plaster-and-lath walls common in pre-1970s colonials and capes read differently on a moisture meter than standard drywall, and water migrates laterally through plaster much farther from the source before it becomes visible.

Extraction comes next — truck-mounted and portable units pulling standing water from hard surfaces and subfloor assemblies. After extraction, the drying phase begins: commercial-grade desiccant and refrigerant dehumidifiers, high-velocity air movers positioned to create the directed airflow patterns called for under the IICRC S500 standard. Moisture readings are logged at each visit so drying progress is documented — a record that matters both for structural verification and for insurance claim support.

Affected materials that can't be dried in place — saturated insulation, compromised drywall sections, swollen engineered flooring — are removed and disposed of properly before reconstruction begins.

## Local Note

In Wayne's lake communities — particularly around Packanack Lake and Pines Lake — finished basements in midcentury split-levels often have a structural quirk worth knowing: the lower level sits partially below grade with a concrete slab that was poured directly on soil without a vapor barrier, a standard practice in that era. After a flood event, that slab retains moisture from below even after surface water is extracted, and a drying plan that doesn't account for ground-moisture vapor transmission will show stalled readings for days. Crews need to recognize that pattern and adjust equipment placement accordingly — positioning desiccant units to address vapor drive upward through the slab, not just lateral evaporation from walls.
