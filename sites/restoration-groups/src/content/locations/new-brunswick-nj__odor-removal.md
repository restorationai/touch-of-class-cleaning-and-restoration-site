## Why New Brunswick Properties Hold Odors Longer

The building stock here works against quick fixes. Much of the residential housing in the 08901 ZIP code — particularly the multifamily rowhouses near Rutgers Village and the older two- and three-family homes closer to the Highland Park border — was built before 1950. Those structures rely heavily on plaster-and-lath walls, old-growth wood framing, and cast-iron drain stacks. Plaster is porous in a way that modern drywall isn't: smoke particles, sewer gases, and mold-related VOCs penetrate the surface layer and get trapped in the substrate. A single cigarette fire or a slow drain leak can leave a smell that survives three rounds of surface cleaning.

The Raritan River's flood history compounds this. When stormwater or river water enters a basement — as it did in a number of riverfront and low-lying addresses during Tropical Storm Ida in 2021 — it carries organic material that feeds odor-producing bacteria long after the standing water is gone. That biological odor source requires treatment, not just masking.

High-density student rentals add another layer. Rapid tenant turnover, deferred maintenance, and the occasional unreported water event mean odor problems are often discovered weeks after they started — by which point microbial activity has had time to colonize porous materials throughout the unit.

## Our Deodorization Process, Calibrated to New Brunswick

Effective odor removal follows a sequence — and skipping steps is why DIY attempts fail. The process starts with source identification. No deodorization technology works on an odor source that's still active. We locate and remove or remediate the origin first: charred material, contaminated flooring, saturated insulation, or a drain line issue.

Once the source is addressed, we match the treatment method to the property:

- **Thermal fogging** penetrates the same microscopic pathways that smoke traveled during a fire — into wall cavities, under trim, into upholstery fibers. It's particularly effective in the older, irregular construction common in downtown New Brunswick and the George Street corridor, where gaps and voids are plentiful.
- **Hydroxyl deodorization** uses UV-generated hydroxyl radicals to break down odor-causing molecules at the chemical level. Unlike ozone, hydroxyl generators can run in occupied or partially occupied spaces, which matters in institutional settings near Robert Wood Johnson University Hospital or in commercial properties that can't afford extended downtime.
- **Ozone treatment** is reserved for unoccupied spaces with severe odor contamination — fire losses, long-term sewage events, or units with heavy biological odor. We follow IICRC guidelines on concentration and dwell time, then ventilate thoroughly before reoccupancy.
- **HEPA air scrubbing and encapsulation** rounds out the process for surfaces that have absorbed odors over years rather than a single event.

For properties with plaster walls — common throughout older New Brunswick neighborhoods — we extend treatment dwell times and may apply penetrating encapsulants to the wall surface before any repainting, because plaster will off-gas odor back into a freshly painted room if the substrate isn't treated.

## Local Note: Plaster Walls and the Odor You Can't Paint Over

One pattern that comes up repeatedly in New Brunswick's pre-war housing stock — especially in the rowhouses between the Fifth Ward and the Edgebrook area — is homeowners who repaint after a smoke or water event and find the odor returns within weeks. Plaster is hygroscopic: it absorbs and releases moisture and volatile compounds slowly over time. A fresh coat of latex paint seals the surface temporarily, but as the plaster breathes with seasonal humidity changes (and New Jersey humidity swings are significant), trapped odor molecules migrate back through. The fix isn't more paint — it's treating the plaster substrate with a penetrating deodorizing agent before any finish coat goes on. It's a step that adds time to a job, but skipping it means the odor comes back by the following summer.
