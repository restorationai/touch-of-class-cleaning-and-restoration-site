## Why Teaneck Properties See Basement Flooding Issues

Teaneck's housing stock tells the story before you even look at a weather map. The majority of homes in neighborhoods like West Englewood and The Grange were built between the 1920s and 1940s — brick Tudors, center-hall colonials, and cape-style homes with full basements that were finished long after original construction. Those older basements typically rely on cast-iron floor drains connected to combined sewer lines that carry both stormwater and sanitary waste in the same pipe. When a heavy rain event saturates the ground and the municipal system surges, water doesn't just seep through foundation walls — it actively backflows through those drains.

The low-lying terrain compounds the problem. Teaneck Creek winds through the western and central portions of the township before draining toward the Hackensack River on the eastern edge. Properties within a few blocks of the creek corridor, including areas near Votee Park and the Bryant School neighborhood, sit in natural collection zones where the water table rises quickly during extended rain. Hydrostatic pressure builds against foundation walls faster here than on higher ground, and even a hairline crack in a poured-concrete or block foundation can admit hundreds of gallons overnight.

Finished basements add a third layer of risk. What looks like drywall and carpet is often a moisture trap — materials that wick water silently for 24 to 48 hours before visible damage appears at the surface. By the time a homeowner notices the smell, microbial growth can already be colonizing the paper face of drywall behind the framing.

## Our Basement Flooding Cleanup Process in Teaneck

Every job starts with a moisture mapping inspection before any equipment is staged. We use thermal imaging and calibrated moisture meters to trace exactly how far water has traveled — under slab edges, into wall bases, and beneath any floating floor systems. In Teaneck's older homes, this step is especially important because original concrete block foundations absorb and redistribute water differently than poured concrete, and the moisture profile inside the block cavities won't show up on the surface for hours.

Once the scope is documented, truck-mounted extraction removes standing water from the floor. For sewer-backup events — which account for a significant share of Teaneck basement losses — we treat all affected surfaces as a Category 3 contaminated-water loss per the IICRC S500 standard, meaning porous materials that absorbed backflow water are removed rather than dried in place. That includes carpet, pad, and the lower sections of drywall.

Drying follows with commercial-grade desiccant and refrigerant dehumidifiers paired with high-velocity air movers positioned to create a directed airflow pattern through the space. We monitor daily with calibrated readings and adjust equipment placement until the structural materials — framing, subfloor, and remaining wall assemblies — reach target moisture levels. Typical drying cycles in Teaneck basements run three to five days depending on the volume of water and how long it was present before extraction began.
