## Why New Brunswick Properties See Water Damage Differently

New Brunswick's building stock spans almost every era of American construction, and that range creates a distinct set of water risks. The dense rowhouses and multifamily buildings that fill the blocks near Rutgers University were built when galvanized steel and cast-iron were standard — both corrode from the inside out, and both fail without much warning. A supply line that looks intact at the shutoff can be paper-thin at a joint hidden inside a wall cavity.

Downtown's high-rise development and the institutional footprint around Robert Wood Johnson University Hospital introduce a different exposure: large-volume losses from commercial sprinkler systems, chilled-water lines, and HVAC condensate. A single sprinkler head activation on an upper floor can migrate through multiple units before anyone notices. The George Street corridor's restaurants and retail spaces add overnight risk — a dishwasher line or walk-in cooler drain that fails at 2 a.m. can saturate a subfloor for hours before staff arrive.

Student rental turnover in the ZIP codes closest to campus — 08901 in particular — means deferred maintenance is common. Landlords managing high unit counts sometimes miss slow leaks until a tenant reports visible staining or a ceiling starts to sag.

## Our Water Cleanup Process in New Brunswick

The process begins with containment, not extraction. Before equipment goes in, the source has to be confirmed stopped — a detail that sounds obvious but gets skipped when crews are rushing. Once water is no longer moving, we use thermal imaging and calibrated moisture meters to map the full extent of saturation, including areas that look dry at the surface. In older New Brunswick construction, that step is especially important: plaster over wood lath holds moisture differently than modern drywall, and readings at the face of a wall can understate what's happening inside.

Extraction comes next — truck-mounted or portable units depending on access — followed by the placement of commercial-grade air movers and desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers sized to the affected area. Drying is monitored daily against the IICRC S500 standard, with moisture logs that document progress for your insurance carrier. Affected materials that cannot dry in place — saturated subfloor sections, soaked insulation, compromised drywall — are removed before they become a mold substrate.

As an IICRC Certified Firm (#210213) and NJ Licensed Home Improvement Contractor, we can carry the work from emergency extraction through structural drying and into reconstruction without handing the project off.
