## Why East Brunswick Homes Present Unique Renovation Challenges

Postwar construction in East Brunswick followed patterns common to Middlesex County's suburban expansion: concrete-block foundations, original cast-iron drain stacks, galvanized supply lines, and minimal insulation in exterior walls. That combination creates a specific set of complications when you open up a wall or pull up flooring. Galvanized pipe that looks intact on the outside can be nearly closed with mineral deposits inside — a kitchen remodel that requires moving a sink often reveals a plumbing system that needs partial replacement before any finish work begins. Cast-iron drains in finished basements are similarly unpredictable; what starts as a bathroom remodel can uncover cracked or root-infiltrated drain lines beneath the slab.

Beyond the mechanical systems, East Brunswick's low-lying sections near Lawrence Brook and the Route 18 corridor saw repeated flooding during Tropical Storm Irene and the remnants of Ida. Homes in those areas frequently have water-damaged rim joists, compromised vapor barriers, and subfloor assemblies that were never properly dried or replaced after those events. A renovation that doesn't account for latent moisture damage in the framing is a renovation that fails in five years. Our crews assess structural conditions before finalizing any scope — not after demo is already underway.

## Local Note

One thing that catches out-of-area contractors in East Brunswick: the finished basements in the older ranch and split-level tracts along Old Bridge Turnpike were often built with the original 1960s-era concrete block walls furred out with wood framing set directly against the block — no air gap, no vapor barrier. When those walls get wet, the framing can harbor moisture for months behind drywall that looks perfectly dry on the surface. We've learned to pull moisture readings at the block face, not just the drywall face, before signing off on any basement renovation scope.
