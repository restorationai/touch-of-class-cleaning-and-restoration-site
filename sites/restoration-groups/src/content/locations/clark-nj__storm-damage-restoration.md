## Our Storm Damage Restoration Process in Clark

Every storm job starts with a full scope assessment before any equipment rolls in. We photograph and document structural damage, water intrusion points, and affected materials — the kind of detailed loss documentation that carriers like Selective, NJM, and State Farm expect before approving a claim. From there, the process follows a defined sequence:

**1. Emergency stabilization.** Roof tarping, board-up, and temporary weatherproofing stop additional water entry. On split-level homes common to the Lake Avenue and Brant Lake neighborhoods, this often means working on multiple roof planes at different elevations.

**2. Water extraction and drying.** Truck-mounted extractors pull standing water from basement slabs and carpet systems. We then deploy commercial-grade desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers and high-velocity air movers calibrated to the square footage and material types — concrete, drywall, and original hardwood all dry at different rates and require different equipment placement.

**3. Structural assessment and debris removal.** Downed tree limbs, compromised rafters, and damaged sheathing are removed and documented. We hold the debris manifest for your adjuster.

**4. Reconstruction coordination.** As an NJ Licensed Home Improvement Contractor, we carry the restoration through to finished repairs — replacing roof decking, reframing damaged walls, reinstalling drywall and insulation — so you're not managing a separate general contractor mid-claim.

Because we operate 24/7, we can begin stabilization the same night a storm moves through, which matters most when rain is still in the forecast.

## Local Note

One pattern we see repeatedly in Clark's finished-basement ranches: homeowners assume a sump pump failure during a storm is a clean-water event. It usually isn't. When Robinson's Branch backs up into the municipal system, what enters your floor drain is Category 2 or Category 3 water — gray or black water carrying bacteria and sewage solids. That changes the remediation protocol entirely: porous materials like drywall, carpet padding, and wood framing that contact contaminated water generally cannot be dried in place and must be removed. Homeowners who run a shop vac and a box fan after a sewer backup are unknowingly spreading contamination. If your basement flooded during a storm and there was any sewage odor, treat it as a biohazard event until a technician can test and classify the water source.
