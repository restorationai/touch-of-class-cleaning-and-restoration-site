## Why Clark Properties See Reconstruction Challenges

Clark was largely built between 1950 and 1970, and that era of construction shows up in every reconstruction job. Ranch homes and split-levels along Lake Avenue typically have finished basements with cast-iron drain stacks that have been corroding quietly for decades. When those drains back up — as many did during Tropical Storm Ida in 2021 when Robinson's Branch pushed water into low-lying streets — the damage isn't limited to the slab. Water wicks into wood-framed partition walls, saturates fiberglass batt insulation, and travels along subfloor sheathing before anyone notices a stain on the ceiling below.

Beyond flooding, fire losses on the Westfield Avenue corridor and surrounding commercial blocks often expose a secondary issue: balloon-frame construction in older mixed-use buildings, where fire travels vertically inside wall cavities faster than in platform-framed structures. Reconstruction in those buildings requires fire-blocking installation that wasn't in the original build, which means structural work that goes beyond simple like-for-like replacement. Clark's residential zones also carry a high proportion of attached garages that share a common wall with living space — a fire that starts in the garage frequently requires reconstruction of that shared assembly to current fire-separation code, not the code in effect when the home was built.

## Our Reconstruction Process in Clark

Every project starts with a scope document, not a handshake estimate. After the mitigation phase is complete — water extraction, drying, debris removal — a project manager walks the loss with the property owner and, when applicable, the insurance adjuster. Structural members are probed and photographed, load paths are identified, and any hidden damage behind finishes is documented before a single sheet of drywall is ordered.

For Clark's finished-basement losses, that documentation step matters more than it might in newer construction. A 1,100-square-foot ranch in the 07066 ZIP code often has its HVAC air handler, electrical panel, and water heater all sharing the basement level. Reconstruction sequencing has to account for utility access and inspection windows — rough framing, then electrical rough-in, then insulation, then drywall — rather than simply replacing finishes. The Restoration Group coordinates directly with Union County building inspectors to schedule required inspections at each phase, keeping the project moving without rework.
