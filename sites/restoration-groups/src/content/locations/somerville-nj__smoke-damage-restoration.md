Smoke from a kitchen fire or an electrical fault doesn't stay in the room where it started — it travels through wall cavities, settles into plaster, and embeds itself in the wood framing that makes up most of Somerville's century-old Victorians and early colonial two-families. In a borough where homes along Gaston Avenue and the West End were built before World War II with horsehair plaster walls and uninsulated balloon framing, smoke migrates farther and faster than it would in a newer build, and the odor can persist for months if the cleanup stops at visible surfaces.

## Why Somerville Properties See Smoke Damage Issues

Somerville's housing stock is the first thing that shapes how smoke damage unfolds here. The borough's compact footprint — roughly two square miles between the Raritan River to the south and Peters Brook to the north — is dense with attached and semi-detached frame structures. When one unit in a two-family on East Main or Mercer Street has a fire, smoke pressure differentials push residue through shared wall assemblies into the neighboring unit. Tenants in the unaffected unit often report soot on interior trim and a persistent acrid smell before anyone realizes the contamination has spread.

The Main Street business district adds a different exposure pattern. Overnight kitchen fires in restaurants, sprinkler discharges that follow a small grease event, and electrical fires in older commercial spaces with knob-and-tube remnants all generate heavy protein smoke — the kind that leaves an invisible, sticky film on every surface and smells worse when surfaces are heated. Protein smoke is among the hardest residue types to neutralize because it bonds chemically to paint, tile grout, and HVAC components.

Somerset County's humid summers accelerate the problem. High relative humidity causes smoke particulates already absorbed into wood and plaster to off-gas more aggressively, which is why a home that seemed "fine" in the days after a fire can smell noticeably worse by July.

## Our Smoke Damage Restoration Process in Somerville

The first step on every job is a structured assessment — not a visual walkthrough, but a systematic room-by-room inspection that maps where smoke traveled, what surfaces it contacted, and what materials are salvageable. In Somerville's older homes, that means probing behind plaster, checking attic spaces where balloon framing acts as a chimney channel, and testing HVAC returns that may have distributed fine particulates throughout the structure.

From there, the process follows the IICRC S700 standard for smoke and fire restoration:

- **Containment and ventilation** — negative air pressure keeps cross-contamination from spreading to unaffected rooms while industrial air scrubbers with HEPA and activated-carbon filtration run continuously.
- **Dry residue removal** — dry chemical sponges lift loose soot from walls and ceilings before any wet cleaning begins. Applying liquid cleaners to unsealed soot drives it deeper into porous surfaces.
- **Surface cleaning and neutralization** — alkaline smoke residue requires pH-matched cleaning agents. Protein smoke from cooking fires requires enzymatic treatments. The chemistry is matched to the fire type, not applied generically.
- **Thermal fogging and hydroxyl treatment** — deodorization goes beyond masking. Thermal fogging replicates how smoke penetrated the structure, reaching the same voids and cavities. Hydroxyl generators run afterward to break down odor molecules at the molecular level without requiring occupants to vacate.
- **Structural drying and content restoration** — if suppression water is also present, drying is integrated into the smoke scope rather than treated as a separate job.

In Somerville's plaster-walled homes specifically, we allow additional dwell time for deodorization treatments. Plaster is more porous than drywall and holds smoke compounds deeper in its matrix.

## Local Note

Somerville's pre-war two-families and Victorians were commonly built with balloon framing — a construction method where wall studs run continuously from the foundation sill to the roof rafters without the fire-blocking horizontal members that platform framing uses. In a fire event, this framing acts like a flue: smoke and heat travel vertically through the wall cavity from basement to attic in minutes.
