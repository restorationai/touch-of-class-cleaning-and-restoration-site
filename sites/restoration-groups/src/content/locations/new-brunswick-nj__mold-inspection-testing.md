New Brunswick's dense stock of early-1900s rowhouses and multifamily buildings — especially the blocks radiating out from Rutgers Village and the Fifth Ward — creates conditions where mold can establish itself quietly behind plaster walls, inside balloon-frame cavities, and under original hardwood floors long before a tenant or owner notices a musty odor. ## Why New Brunswick Properties Are Prone to Hidden Mold

The same building density that makes New Brunswick's neighborhoods walkable and vibrant also concentrates moisture problems. Pre-1950 construction along the George Street corridor and in Robeson Village typically features brick-and-mortar exterior walls with minimal vapor barriers — when those walls absorb ground moisture or a slow roof leak goes unaddressed for a season, interior cavities stay damp long enough for mold spores to colonize within 24 to 72 hours. Student rentals near Rutgers University compound the risk: high occupancy, deferred maintenance reports, and frequent turnover mean water intrusion events often go unreported until visible growth appears on a ceiling or a landlord schedules a turnover inspection.

The Raritan River's flood history adds another layer. When Ida pushed water into low-lying riverfront areas near Boyd Park in 2021, dozens of ground-floor units and finished basements took on water. Even properties that dried out visually may still carry elevated spore counts inside wall assemblies and under subfloors — conditions that standard visual inspection alone won't catch.

Downtown New Brunswick's commercial corridor presents a different exposure profile: restaurant kitchens, office HVAC systems, and sprinkler-related losses in high-rise buildings can seed mold growth inside ductwork and above drop ceilings, where it circulates through the air supply before anyone sees a stain.

## Our Mold Inspection and Testing Process in New Brunswick

Every inspection begins with a structured visual assessment of the building envelope, plumbing chases, HVAC components, and any areas with a documented or suspected moisture history. For New Brunswick properties, that typically means paying close attention to basement slab edges (common entry points in the city's older rowhouses), bathroom and kitchen wet walls shared between units, and any areas that show evidence of past flood exposure.

Where visual indicators suggest active or past growth, we collect air samples and surface samples using calibrated equipment. Air samples establish a baseline spore count for each interior zone and compare it against an outdoor control sample — a meaningful benchmark given that New Brunswick's urban tree canopy and proximity to the Raritan River corridor can elevate ambient outdoor spore levels seasonally. Surface samples (tape lifts or swabs) identify the specific mold genus and species present, which matters for remediation planning and for any health-related disclosures required in a real estate transaction or insurance claim.

All samples are sent to an accredited third-party laboratory. The written report we deliver identifies affected areas, documents moisture readings, maps probable moisture pathways, and outlines remediation scope — a format that works directly with insurance adjusters and, when required, with NJ Department of Community Affairs inspection records for rental properties.

The Restoration Group holds IICRC Firm Certification (#210213) and is licensed as a NJ Home Improvement Contractor through the NJ Division of Consumer Affairs, so the inspection documentation we produce carries the credentials that lenders, insurers, and municipal inspectors expect.

## Local Note

One pattern that comes up repeatedly in New Brunswick's older multifamily stock: buildings that were converted from single-family rowhouses to two- or three-unit rentals often have sealed-off original chimneys that now function as unventilated vertical shafts connecting every floor. When a ground-floor unit has a moisture event, humid air migrates upward through these chases and condenses inside upper-floor walls — producing mold growth two stories above the original water source. Inspectors who don't probe these pathways will miss the secondary colonies entirely. It's a construction quirk specific to this era and housing type, and it's one reason we treat New Brunswick multifamily inspections as multi-zone investigations rather than single-unit assessments.
