When a bathroom floods and the drywall has to come out, or a kitchen fire takes out the cabinets and ceiling, the hole in the wall doesn't close itself. Someone has to frame it back, run new electrical and plumbing, and finish it to match the rest of the house before the room is livable again. That's the gap between restoration and renovation, and it's where general contracting work actually happens: scope of work, permits, framing, rough-in, and finish carpentry, whether the project started with a water loss or started with a homeowner who's tired of a 1998 kitchen.

## What renovations, remodels and general contracting actually involves

General contracting covers everything from a single-room post-damage rebuild to a full kitchen or bathroom remodel to a whole-home renovation. The work spans structural framing, electrical and plumbing rough-in, drywall and insulation, cabinetry, flooring, and finish trim, coordinated under one scope so a homeowner isn't managing five separate trades. Timelines vary widely: a drywall patch and repaint after a small water loss might run a few days, while a kitchen remodel with new cabinets, counters, and flooring typically runs four to eight weeks depending on material lead times and whether walls are moving.

## Our process

1. **Scope of work and design consultation.** We walk the space, document existing conditions, and put the project (materials, layout changes, fixtures) in writing before any demo starts.
2. **Permits and code review.** Structural changes, electrical, and plumbing work require permits through the local building department. We handle the submittal and inspection scheduling so the work is signed off, not just finished.
3. **Framing and rough-in.** Walls go up or come down, electrical and plumbing lines get run or relocated, and HVAC is adjusted before anything gets closed up behind drywall.
4. **Finishes.** Drywall, paint, flooring, cabinetry, and trim go in. This is the stage where craftsmanship either shows or it doesn't.
5. **Final walkthrough.** We go through the space with the homeowner against the original scope, punch-list any remaining items, and close out permits.

## What separates a good general contracting response from a bad one

The most common failure point on a remodel or rebuild isn't the visible finish work, it's what happens behind the walls. A contractor in a hurry will rough-in electrical or plumbing without pulling a permit, which creates problems at resale or during a future insurance claim when an adjuster asks for inspection records that don't exist. On post-damage rebuilds specifically, a rushed job will close up drywall over framing that wasn't fully dried or over wiring that wasn't actually replaced, just patched. A careful contractor documents moisture readings before closing walls, matches the rebuild scope to what the insurance carrier actually approved, and keeps photos and permit records so there's a paper trail if a question comes up later. Our IICRC-certified team and Utah contractor license (#RC-25-0737) are part of how that documentation gets handled correctly the first time.

Costs vary enormously based on square footage, material selections, and whether walls or systems are moving, so the ranges below are typical industry figures, not a quote. ## Seasonal & regional considerations

Much of the housing stock in Saratoga Springs and the surrounding Utah County communities was built from the early 2000s onward, so remodel work here more often deals with builder-grade finishes than with aging knob-and-tube wiring or cast iron plumbing. Winters along the Wasatch Front bring sustained freezing temperatures, which affects exterior work like additions or window replacement more than interior remodeling, where framing and finish work continue year-round. Rapid growth across the area has also meant permit review times can shift depending on the season and the building department's current volume, which is one more reason to get permits submitted early in the project timeline rather than after demo has already started.
