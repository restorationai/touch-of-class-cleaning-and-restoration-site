When floodwater enters a home, whether from spring runoff along the Jordan River corridor, a failed sump pump during a cloudburst, or overland flow across unfinished lots common in newer developments, the water carries silt, lawn chemicals, and sometimes sewage back into living spaces. ## What flood damage restoration actually involves

Flood damage restoration deals with Category 3 water, the industry term for water that has picked up contaminants from soil, storm drains, or sewage backflow before entering the structure. Unlike a broken supply line, floodwater has usually traveled across yards, streets, or crawlspaces, so it arrives carrying grit, bacteria, and sometimes fuel or chemical residue from garages and sheds. Materials that soak in contaminated water, drywall, carpet pad, baseboards, and often the carpet itself, generally can't be dried and saved the way they could with clean water. The work involves controlled demolition of porous materials below the flood line, extraction of standing water, antimicrobial treatment of framing and subfloor, and a structural drying phase that's monitored daily until moisture readings in studs and subfloor return to normal. On a typical single-story home with a few inches of flooding, that drying window runs several days to a week depending on how much material had to come out and how the HVAC and ductwork held up.

## Our process

1. **Water source and contamination assessment.** Before any equipment goes in, we identify where the water came from and classify it, since sewage-adjacent or chemical-contaminated flooding requires different PPE and disposal than storm runoff.
2. **Extraction and controlled demolition.** Standing water is pumped and extracted first, then saturated drywall, baseboards, and flooring that can't be sanitized are removed to the height the water reached, often called the flood cut.
3. **Cleaning and antimicrobial application.** Exposed studs, subfloor, and slab are cleaned and treated to address the bacteria and organic matter floodwater leaves behind, not just the moisture.
4. **Structural drying with daily monitoring.** Air movers and dehumidifiers run until moisture meters confirm framing and subfloor are back to a dry standard, with readings logged for the insurance file.
5. **Documentation for the claim.** Photos, moisture logs, and a written scope are compiled throughout, since flood claims tend to draw more scrutiny from adjusters than a simple appliance leak.

## What separates a good flood damage response from a bad one

The most common mistake is treating floodwater like clean water, drying materials that should have been removed because they can't be sanitized, only to see odor or mold surface weeks later. A careful crew pulls baseboards and checks behind them, pulls back carpet rather than drying over it, and inspects wall cavities for wicking even when the surface drywall looks fine. Adjusters reviewing flood claims typically look for documented moisture readings at multiple points, photos of the flood line before demolition, and a clear line between what was removed and why. Skipping the antimicrobial step, or applying it without removing enough contaminated material first, is another shortcut that shows up later as persistent odor or a callback. We carry IICRC certification and hold Utah contractor license RC-25-0737, and every flood job gets a written scope before demolition starts so there's a clear record of what came out and why.

## What does flood damage restoration cost?

Costs vary with how much water entered, how long it sat before cleanup started, and how much demolition is needed to remove contaminated materials. A key point many homeowners don't expect: standard homeowners insurance typically does not cover flood damage from rising water or storm runoff, that usually requires a separate flood policy through the National Flood Insurance Program, while damage from an internal failure like a sump pump backup may fall under a standard policy depending on its wording. Every loss is different, which is why we provide a written scope of work and cost estimate before any work begins.
