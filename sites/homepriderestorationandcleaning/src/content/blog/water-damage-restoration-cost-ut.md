## What Factors Move the Price Up or Down?

The category and class of water loss are the two biggest cost drivers in any restoration estimate. Understanding them helps you read your written scope and ask the right questions.

**Water category** (per IICRC S500):
- **Category 1 (clean water):** Comes from a sanitary source, like a supply line or a clean appliance. Lowest remediation cost.
- **Category 2 (gray water):** Contains contaminants. Washing machine overflow, dishwasher discharge, toilet overflow without solids. Requires more aggressive drying protocols and antimicrobial treatment.
- **Category 3 (black water):** Sewage, floodwater from outside, or water that has been sitting long enough to become grossly contaminated. Requires full PPE, disposal of porous materials, and sanitization. Most expensive category by a significant margin.

**Water class** describes how much evaporation load is present:
- Class 1: Minimal absorption, small area.
- Class 2: Significant absorption, wall cavities affected.
- Class 3: Ceilings, walls, insulation, and subfloor all saturated.
- Class 4: Specialty drying required (hardwood, concrete, plaster).

A Class 1 / Category 1 loss in a bathroom is a very different job from a Class 3 / Category 2 loss in a finished basement. The crew's equipment deployment, drying time, and material removal decisions all change.

**Other factors that affect cost:**
- **Response time:** Water that sits 48-72 hours begins promoting mold growth (the [EPA recommends](https://www.epa.gov/mold) drying within 24-48 hours). Delayed response moves a Category 1 loss toward Category 3 conditions and adds mold remediation to the scope.
- **Square footage affected:** Crews calculate equipment placement by square footage and cubic volume of the space.
- **Materials involved:** Hardwood floors require specialty drying systems (Dri-Eaz mats or similar). Concrete slabs need extended drying time. Spray foam insulation is nearly impossible to dry and usually requires removal.
- **Accessibility:** Crawl spaces, tight attic spaces, and multi-story homes add labor hours.
- **Code upgrades:** If structural materials are opened, Utah code may require updates to insulation R-value or vapor barriers before close-up.

## Will Homeowners Insurance Cover Water Damage Restoration in Utah?

Most standard homeowners policies cover sudden and accidental water damage but exclude gradual leaks and flooding from outside the home. In Utah, that distinction matters a great deal: a burst pipe in a Saratoga Springs home during a cold snap is almost always covered. A slow leak behind a cabinet that went unnoticed for six months is almost always denied.

**What is typically covered:**
- Burst pipes (including [frozen pipe damage](/blog/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-frozen-pipes-park-city/))
- Appliance failures (washing machine hose, dishwasher supply line)
- Sudden roof leaks from storm damage
- Accidental overflow from a tub or sink

**What is typically excluded:**
- Gradual seepage or slow leaks
- Groundwater intrusion and surface flooding (requires separate NFIP flood insurance)
- Sewer backup (often excluded unless you have a sewer backup rider)
- Damage caused by deferred maintenance

For a full breakdown of what policies typically pay, see [Does Homeowners Insurance Cover Water Damage?](/blog/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-water-damage/)

**Protecting your claim:** The documentation you provide in the first 48 hours determines how smoothly your claim processes. Take photos and video before anything is moved. Get a written moisture map from your restoration company showing moisture readings at each affected surface. Request a written scope of work before extraction begins. Adjusters look for evidence that the damage was sudden, not gradual, and that remediation followed the IICRC S500 standard.

**Deductible reality:** Most Utah homeowners carry a $1,000 to $2,500 deductible. On a $4,000 water loss, your out-of-pocket is real. On a $15,000 sewage backup, the policy coverage is the difference between a manageable recovery and a financial crisis.

## What Does the Water Damage Restoration Process Actually Look Like?

Certified restoration follows a defined sequence. Knowing the steps helps you understand what you're paying for and why skipping steps creates bigger problems later.

1. **Emergency extraction.** Truck-mounted or portable extractors remove standing water. This is the fastest-moving phase and often happens within hours of the call.
2. **Moisture mapping.** Technicians use non-penetrating and penetrating moisture meters (Tramex, Delmhorst, or similar) to map every affected surface. This establishes the baseline your insurance adjuster needs.
3. **Controlled demolition (if needed).** Wet drywall below the moisture line is cut and removed. Wet insulation is pulled. Saturated flooring may be lifted. This is not destruction, it is the only way to dry wall cavities and subfloors.
4. **Drying system deployment.** LGR (low-grain refrigerant) dehumidifiers and high-velocity air movers are placed per IICRC S500 psychrometric calculations, not guesswork. The number of machines, their placement, and the airflow pattern are documented.
5. **Daily monitoring.** Technicians return each day to record moisture readings and adjust equipment. Drying typically takes 3 to 5 days for a Class 2 loss, longer for Class 3 or 4.
6. **Antimicrobial treatment.** Applied to affected structural surfaces after drying to inhibit mold growth.
7. **Final clearance readings.** Moisture levels must reach the IICRC standard for the material type before equipment is removed. This protects you from a mold problem six weeks later.
8. **Reconstruction scope.** Once the structure is dry and cleared, a separate scope covers drywall replacement, flooring, paint, and any code-required upgrades.
