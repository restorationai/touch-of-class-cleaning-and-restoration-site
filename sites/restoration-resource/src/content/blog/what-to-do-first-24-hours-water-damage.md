If you've got standing water in your home right now, the first priority is stopping the source and getting the power and water shut off before you do anything else. Locate your main water shutoff valve and turn it, flip the breaker to any affected rooms if outlets or fixtures are submerged, and start moving valuables and furniture away from the wet area. The first 24 hours determine a lot about how the rest of this goes: how much material you save, whether mold gets a foothold, and how smoothly your insurance claim moves. Here's what that window actually looks like and what to do with it.

## What's Happening in Those First 24 Hours

Water doesn't sit still, and it doesn't stay where it started. Within the first few hours, it wicks into drywall, baseboards, and subflooring through capillary action, moving well past the visible wet line. Carpet padding holds water like a sponge and keeps feeding moisture back up into the carpet even after the surface looks dry. Behind walls, water tracks downward and sideways along studs and insulation, often showing up as a stain or bubbling paint several feet from the actual leak.

Temperature and humidity speed this along. In a Pasco summer, a closed-up house with the AC running can actually slow surface evaporation while trapping humidity inside wall cavities, and in winter, a burst pipe from a hard freeze often means water has been running longer than you realize by the time you notice it, since many homes here sit on slab foundations where a slow leak under the slab can go unnoticed until flooring starts to lift or a damp patch appears on carpet.

## Immediate Steps to Take

1. **Stop the water source.** Shut off the main valve for a supply line break, or the fixture's local shutoff if it's isolated to one appliance or toilet.
2. **Cut power to wet areas.** If water has reached outlets, switches, or anything electrical, shut off the breaker for that zone before you go back in.
3. **Document before you touch anything.** Take photos and short videos of the water level, affected materials, and any visible damage. Insurance adjusters rely heavily on this early documentation.
4. **Remove what you can safely move.** Furniture, rugs, electronics, and boxes sitting in water should come out first. Use blocks or foil squares under furniture legs you can't move to keep them from staining or wicking.
5. **Start pulling up standing water.** A wet/dry shop vac handles small amounts. For anything beyond a few inches or a flooded room, this is past what a shop vac can keep up with.
6. **Increase airflow.** Open interior doors, run fans, and if the outdoor humidity is low (common in the high desert climate here), open windows to help surface moisture evaporate while you arrange further drying.
7. **Call your insurance company.** Most carriers want to know about water losses within 24 to 48 hours, and an early call starts your claim moving while damage is still fresh and documented.

## What NOT to Do

- **Don't use a regular household vacuum** on standing water. It's not built for it and you'll ruin it or risk a shock.
- **Don't run the HVAC system** if ductwork is wet. It can spread moisture and contamination through the whole house.
- **Don't wait to see if it dries on its own.** Drywall and subflooring that read as damp today can support mold growth within 24 to 48 hours in the right conditions, and by day three or four the window for simple drying instead of material replacement starts closing.
- **Don't walk through water** if you suspect it's contaminated, such as backup from a toilet or sewer line, or if it's near electrical panels or outlets.
- **Don't toss damaged items before photographing them.** Even water-ruined drywall or carpet should be documented for the claim before it leaves the house.

## When to Call a Professional

A few buckets of water from a supply line you caught quickly is often manageable with fans, a dehumidifier, and some patience. Call in a restoration professional when the situation moves past that: water has been sitting for more than a few hours, it's covered more than a small area, it's come from a source other than clean supply water, or it's reached wall cavities, subflooring, or a finished basement area.

## The Longer Recovery Process

Once the immediate water is gone, the real work is monitored drying, and it typically runs several days, not hours. Industrial air movers and dehumidifiers get positioned based on moisture readings, and a technician checks those readings daily to confirm materials are actually drying rather than just feeling dry to the touch. Drywall, flooring, or cabinetry that stayed wet too long or absorbed contaminated water may need to come out rather than dry in place, a decision based on moisture content and material type rather than how it looks.

Throughout this phase, keep your documentation going: photos of equipment placement, moisture readings if your technician provides them, and any invoices or estimates. Once drying is confirmed complete, the next step is usually reconstruction, replacing drywall, flooring, trim, or cabinetry that didn't survive. That part moves at a normal contractor's pace, but the drying phase before it is what determines whether that reconstruction list is short or long.
