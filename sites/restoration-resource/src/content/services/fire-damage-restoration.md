## What Fire Damage Restoration actually involves

Structural fire damage is really three separate problems layered together: the burn itself, the soot and smoke residue that travels well beyond the fire's origin, and the water used to extinguish it. A kitchen fire that burned for ten minutes can leave smoke damage throughout an entire HVAC system within an hour, because the system keeps circulating air during the event. Our technicians, trained to IICRC FSRT standards for fire and smoke restoration, start by identifying what kind of combustion occurred, because protein soot from a grease fire behaves nothing like synthetic soot from burning plastics or foam furniture, and using the wrong cleaning method on either one can set the residue instead of lifting it. The work typically spans HEPA vacuuming of every affected surface, thermal fogging or ozone/hydroxyl treatment to neutralize odor at the molecular level, and in some cases full contents pack-out so items can be cleaned off-site while structural work proceeds.

## Our process

1. **Safety assessment and site stabilization.** We evaluate structural integrity, electrical hazards, and air quality before any cleaning begins. If roofing or window openings are compromised, that gets addressed first.
2. **Soot and residue characterization.** We identify whether residue is protein-based, synthetic, or a mix, which determines the cleaning agents and sequence used on every surface.
3. **Contents inventory and pack-out.** Salvageable items are documented, photographed, and moved to a controlled cleaning environment, separate from items deemed unrecoverable.
4. **Deep cleaning and deodorization.** HEPA vacuuming removes loose residue first, followed by wet or dry cleaning methods matched to the soot type, then thermal fogging or ozone/hydroxyl treatment to address odor trapped in porous materials.
5. **Structural cleaning and odor sealing.** Walls, subfloors, and framing that absorbed smoke get sealed with odor-blocking primer before any reconstruction or repainting begins.

## What separates a good fire damage response from a bad one

The most common mistake is treating fire cleanup as a surface job. Wiping down visible soot without addressing what's inside wall cavities or ductwork means the odor comes back within weeks, often worse, because residual soot continues reacting with humidity. Another frequent miss: painting over smoke-damaged drywall without sealing it first, which traps the odor under the new paint instead of removing it. Adjusters reviewing a fire claim typically want to see documented pretesting of soot type, a room-by-room scope before cleaning starts, and photos showing hidden damage in cavities and HVAC runs, not just the obviously burned areas. A thorough fire and smoke restoration also checks for secondary water damage from firefighting efforts, since standing water left unaddressed for more than 24 to 48 hours starts its own mold problem layered on top of the fire claim.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Eastern Washington's dry summers bring wildfire smoke infiltration that settles into homes even without a direct structure fire, leaving a fine ash residue on surfaces and HVAC filters that homeowners often mistake for dust. Winter months bring a different risk profile, with space heater fires and chimney-related incidents more common across the Tri-Cities as households supplement heating during cold snaps. Either way, the sooner soot is characterized and cleaned, the less time it has to etch into finishes and set into fabrics.
