The drywall has been cut back, the mold is gone, and the insurance claim letter says the loss is approved, but the kitchen still has exposed subfloor, no cabinets, and a bathroom that's down to the studs. That gap between remediation finishing and a livable space again is where renovation and general contracting work picks up. It also covers remodels that have nothing to do with a prior loss: a kitchen that's outdated, a bathroom that needs a full gut, a layout that no longer works for the household.

## What renovations, remodels and general contracting actually involves

This work ranges from a straightforward drywall and paint rebuild after a small water loss to a full kitchen or bathroom remodel with new plumbing rough-in, electrical, cabinetry, and flooring. On post-damage jobs, the starting point is usually whatever remediation left behind: open stud bays, subfloor that's been drying, maybe a section of roof decking replaced after storm or fire damage. On remodels unrelated to a loss, the starting point is an existing, functional space the homeowner wants reconfigured or updated.

Either way, the job involves the same core disciplines: framing and structural repair where walls or floor joists were compromised, rough-in coordination with plumbing and electrical trades, drywall and finish carpentry, and final selections like cabinetry, countertops, tile, and fixtures. Timelines vary a lot by scope. A single bathroom remodel might run three to five weeks from demo to final walkthrough. A kitchen gut, or a multi-room rebuild after fire or water damage, often runs eight to twelve weeks once permits, material lead times, and inspections are factored in.

## Our process

1. **Scope of work and estimate.** We document existing conditions, take measurements, and put together a written scope that spells out materials, sequence, and price before any demo or framing starts.
2. **Permits and code review.** Anything touching structure, plumbing rough-in, or electrical gets pulled through the applicable permitting authority before work proceeds, not after.
3. **Framing and rough-in.** Structural repairs, wall framing, and plumbing/electrical rough-in happen first, and this is the stage where inspectors sign off before walls close up.
4. **Finishing.** Drywall, texture, paint, flooring, cabinetry, countertops, and fixtures go in, usually in that order so finished surfaces aren't damaged by later trades.
5. **Final walkthrough and punch list.** We walk the space with the homeowner, note anything that needs adjustment, and close out permits with final inspection.

## What separates a good renovation response from a bad one

The most common problem on post-damage rebuilds is starting finish work before the structure underneath is actually ready. If framing goes in over lumber that hasn't been confirmed dry, or drywall closes up a wall cavity before an inspector has signed off on rough plumbing, the homeowner ends up with a wall that has to be opened back up. A second common issue is scope creep that isn't documented: verbal changes during the job that never make it into a written change order, which becomes a dispute later when the final invoice doesn't match what the homeowner remembers agreeing to.

Adjusters reviewing a rebuild estimate are generally looking for a scope that lines up with the approved claim, itemized by material and labor, with permit numbers attached where applicable. A contractor who can produce that paperwork cleanly speeds up supplement requests. One who can't creates delays that land back on the homeowner.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Pasco and the surrounding Tri-Cities sit in a high-desert climate with hot, dry summers and cold winters that bring freeze-thaw cycles. That matters for rebuilds involving exterior walls, roofing, or concrete work, since certain materials and coatings cure and bond differently in extreme heat or near-freezing temperatures, and scheduling exterior finish work around those windows avoids callbacks. Much of the regional housing stock includes post-war and mid-century homes alongside newer slab-on-grade construction, so framing dimensions, electrical panel capacity, and plumbing materials can vary a lot from one house to the next, which is part of why an accurate scope of work starts with an in-person walkthrough rather than a phone estimate.
