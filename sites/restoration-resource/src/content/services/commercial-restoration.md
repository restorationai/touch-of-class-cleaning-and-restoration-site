## What commercial restoration actually involves

Commercial restoration covers the mitigation and recovery work needed after water, fire, or smoke damage affects an office, retail space, warehouse, or multi-tenant building. The scope is different from a residential job in almost every dimension: larger square footage, commercial-grade mechanical systems, inventory and electronics that need protecting, and a business that may need to stay partially operational during the work. A single-tenant office suite might need extraction and structural drying over a few days. A multi-tenant building with a shared mechanical room or HVAC run can take longer, because smoke or moisture doesn't respect a suite's lease line. Equipment scales accordingly: commercial dehumidification and air mover counts are set by cubic footage and material type, not a flat per-room estimate.

## Our process

1. **Site assessment and business impact triage.** We walk the affected space with whoever is managing the loss, whether that's an owner, a property manager, or a facilities lead, and identify which areas are safe to occupy, which need to be isolated, and whether utilities or HVAC need to be shut down to stop the damage from spreading.
2. **Contents, inventory, and equipment protection.** Electronics, point-of-sale systems, paper records, and merchandise get moved, covered, or packed out before structural work starts. This step gets skipped by less experienced crews and is usually where the real losses happen.
3. **Structural mitigation.** Depending on the loss, this means water extraction and structural drying monitored with moisture meters, or soot and smoke removal using HEPA filtration and thermal fogging. Equipment placement is scaled to the commercial footprint, not borrowed from a residential setup.
4. **Phased work and partial reopening.** Where the building allows it, we section off the affected area so unaffected parts of the business can keep operating while drying or cleaning continues in the rest of the space.
5. **Final documentation and walkthrough.** Moisture readings, photos, and a written scope go to the property manager and the insurance carrier, and we do a final walkthrough before handing the space back.

## What separates a good commercial response from a bad one

The most common mistake is treating a commercial loss like an oversized residential one. Undersized drying equipment, missed moisture in wall cavities behind commercial-grade finishes, and failure to isolate shared HVAC runs are the three issues that show up most often when a commercial job gets reworked. Adjusters reviewing commercial claims also look for business-interruption documentation: dated photos, moisture logs, and a clear record of when the space became safe to reoccupy, not just a final invoice. In multi-tenant buildings, a good operator identifies early whether the loss originated in one suite or in shared infrastructure, because that changes who's responsible for what and how the claim gets filed. Electrical hazards in commercial panels and exposed wiring after a water loss also get missed more often than they should; a shutoff and inspection before drying equipment runs is not optional.

## What does commercial restoration cost?

Commercial restoration costs vary more than residential work because the variables, square footage, building systems, inventory loss, and how much of the space needs to stay operational, all move independently. ## Seasonal & regional considerations

Pasco's winter cold snaps are hard on commercial buildings with exposed plumbing runs in loading docks, mechanical rooms, and unheated storage areas, and a frozen line that fails over a weekend can flood a retail floor before anyone's on-site to catch it. Summer brings wildfire smoke that gets pulled into commercial HVAC systems through standard intake vents, leaving a smoke odor in office and retail spaces that didn't experience an actual fire. Buildings near the Columbia River basin should also plan for seasonal water table shifts that affect below-grade storage and mechanical spaces.
