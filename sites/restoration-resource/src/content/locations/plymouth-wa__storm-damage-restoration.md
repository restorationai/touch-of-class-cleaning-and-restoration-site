## Why Plymouth Properties See Storm Damage

Plymouth sits in the open Columbia Basin, where wind funneling through the river corridor can hit speeds that pull shingles, siding, and loose outbuilding roofing straight off a structure. Thunderstorm cells that build over the basin in late spring and summer frequently carry hail large enough to crack skylights and dent metal roofing, and the same storms can drop enough rain in a short window to overwhelm gutters and grading that were designed for a drier climate. A lot of the housing and ag-support buildings in this area are manufactured homes, pole barns, and single-story construction with lighter-gauge roofing, which takes wind uplift differently than heavier stick-built homes, often losing larger roof sections rather than a few shingles at a time. Irrigation infrastructure and orchard windbreaks common around Plymouth also mean fallen trees and limbs are a recurring part of storm cleanup, not an occasional one.

## Our Storm Damage Restoration Process in Plymouth

The first step on most storm calls here is stopping active water intrusion, which usually means emergency tarping or board-up before any interior work starts. Once the structure is secured, our crews extract standing water, pull wet insulation and drywall that won't dry in place, and set up drying equipment calibrated to the basin's dry air, which actually works in our favor for evaporation but means we're watching trapped moisture behind siding and sheathing more carefully than a crew would in a humid climate. Tree and debris removal is coordinated alongside the structural work rather than treated as a separate job, since a downed limb through a roofline usually means both problems need addressing before the tarp goes up for good. Drying is documented and verified against the IICRC S500 standard so the moisture readings hold up if an adjuster reviews the file later.

## Local note

One thing that catches people off guard around Plymouth: hail and high-wind events here often arrive with very little rain, so a homeowner will see a cracked skylight or dented roof vent and assume there's no interior damage because the attic looks dry. In the basin's low humidity, wind-driven dust and grit can work into roof penetrations during the same storm that caused the hail damage, and that debris holds moisture against decking long after the storm passes, which is part of why we check attic spaces and roof penetrations even on calls that read as "wind only" at first glance.
