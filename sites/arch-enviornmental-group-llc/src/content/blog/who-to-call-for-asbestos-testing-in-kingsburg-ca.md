## Who do you call first, a tester or a remediation contractor?

Call an independent tester first, always, before any remediation contractor touches the suspect material. An independent lab result tells you whether there's actually a problem before anyone starts quoting removal work, which protects you from paying to "abate" material that was never asbestos-containing in the first place.

This matters most in Kingsburg's older housing stock. Homes built before the late 1970s along streets like Sierra Street and Conejo Avenue often have original vermiculite insulation, troweled ceiling texture, vinyl floor tile, or transite siding, all materials that can contain asbestos depending on the manufacturer and installation year. A visual guess isn't a diagnosis. Only polarized light microscopy (PLM) analysis at an accredited lab confirms it.

An independent tester has no stake in the outcome. We don't perform remediation ourselves, so a positive or negative result doesn't change our bottom line. That's the entire point of calling a testing company before a remediation company: the data comes first, clean of any motive to oversell the fix.

## When should you call a remediation contractor instead?

Call a remediation contractor only after testing confirms asbestos-containing material and you have a defined scope of work. Before that point, a remediation crew has nothing to scope, and disturbing suspect material without confirmation first (cutting, sanding, dry-sweeping) is how fibers end up airborne.

Once lab results come back positive, your next call should be to a licensed abatement contractor who is unaffiliated with the testing company that found the problem. That separation keeps the process honest on both ends: the tester has no reason to inflate findings, and the contractor isn't grading its own homework later. After the abatement crew finishes, call an independent tester back for clearance air sampling to confirm the space is safe to reoccupy before anyone moves furniture back in.

## When should you call a plumber, contractor, or your insurance company?

Call your general contractor or your insurer when the issue is structural or financial, not when you need an unbiased answer about fiber content. If a renovation project has stalled because a demo crew found old floor tile or pipe wrap insulation, your contractor is right to pause, but they're not the one who should be telling you whether it's asbestos. That's a lab question, not a construction question.

Same logic applies to insurance claims. If a water loss or fire has exposed old building materials and you're filing a claim, documentation from an independent testing company carries more weight with an adjuster than an in-house assessment from the company that's also bidding the repair work.

## What happens when you call Arch Enviornmental Group LLC?

When you call, the first conversation covers what prompted the concern (a renovation, a listing inspection, a damaged material) and the age and construction type of the property, which helps the inspector plan the sampling approach before arriving. Have the property's approximate build year ready if you know it, along with which materials you're worried about.

On site, a certified inspector in a half-face respirator and nitrile gloves takes small, controlled samples of the suspect material, wetting it first to limit any fiber release, then seals each sample in a labeled bag with chain-of-custody documentation. This is a few square inches of material, not demolition. Samples go to a third-party accredited laboratory for PLM analysis; typical turnaround on standard analysis runs in the range most labs quote industry-wide, with rush options available for time-sensitive transactions.
