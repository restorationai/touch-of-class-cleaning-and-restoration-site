Sanger sits in the heart of the San Joaquin Valley, where decades of intensive agriculture, orchard operations, and packing-house activity have left a layered environmental history beneath properties that now attract commercial buyers, developers, and lenders. If you are acquiring or financing land in Sanger, an Environmental Site Assessment is not a formality, it is the document that tells you what that history actually means for the parcel you are about to own.

## Why Sanger Properties Carry Distinct Environmental Due Diligence Considerations

Fresno County's agricultural corridor has a long record of pesticide and fumigant applications, petroleum storage for farm equipment, and small-scale industrial uses that predate modern environmental recordkeeping. In and around Sanger, properties that appear straightforward on a deed search can carry recognized environmental conditions tied to former orchards, roadside fuel stops, or packing and cold-storage operations that operated under far looser standards than today's regulations require.

The valley's clay-heavy soils compound the picture. Clay layers slow vertical migration of contaminants, which can concentrate pollutants near the surface longer than sandy soils would, a factor that matters when a Phase I flags a historical release and a Phase II is needed to determine actual soil or groundwater impact. The shallow depth to groundwater in parts of the valley also means that what sits in the soil is never entirely isolated from what flows beneath it.

Sanger's position along State Route 180 has also made it a corridor for light industrial and commercial development over the years. Properties along those commercial strips often have prior uses, auto repair, dry cleaning, fuel distribution, that generate the kinds of recognized environmental conditions a Phase I is specifically designed to surface.

## Our Environmental Site Assessment Process in Sanger

A Phase I Environmental Site Assessment follows the ASTM E1527-21 standard and involves four core components: records review, site reconnaissance, interviews, and a written report. For Sanger properties, the records review pays particular attention to Fresno County agricultural permit histories, State Water Resources Control Board underground storage tank records, and Geotracker database entries that document known releases in the area.

Site reconnaissance means walking the property, not reviewing satellite imagery from a desk. We look at current conditions, drainage patterns, staining, stressed vegetation, and any structures or equipment that suggest prior uses inconsistent with what the records show. In the San Joaquin Valley, that sometimes means identifying former irrigation sumps, buried fuel tanks associated with orchard operations, or the remnants of pesticide mixing areas that were routine practice for decades.

When a Phase I identifies a recognized environmental condition that warrants further investigation, a Phase II assessment moves from records and observation into the ground itself, soil borings, groundwater sampling, and laboratory analysis calibrated to the specific contaminants of concern the Phase I identified. The Phase II report gives buyers, lenders, and regulators a defensible, data-backed picture of actual conditions rather than historical inference.

Arch Environmental Group holds certifications as a Certified Asbestos Consultant, Lead Inspector Assessor, and NIOSH 582 Microscopist, which means that when a site assessment surfaces building materials of concern on structures within the assessment boundary, we can address those findings within the same engagement rather than routing you to a separate firm.

## Local Note

One pattern worth knowing if you are assessing older commercial or mixed-use properties in Sanger: the valley's agricultural economy meant that many parcels served dual purposes for extended periods, a building might have been a packinghouse, then a warehouse, then a retail space, with each transition leaving behind equipment, tanks, or chemical residues that were never formally documented or remediated. County records from those transitional decades can be sparse. A thorough Phase I in this area leans harder on aerial photograph review and historical Sanborn map research than it might in a city with more complete municipal permitting archives. That extra layer of historical reconstruction is standard in our process for San Joaquin Valley properties, not an add-on.
