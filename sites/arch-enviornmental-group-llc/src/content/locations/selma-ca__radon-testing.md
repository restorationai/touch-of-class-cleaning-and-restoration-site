## Why Selma Properties Are Worth Testing

Radon is a naturally occurring radioactive gas that forms as uranium in soil and rock breaks down. It has no smell, no color, and no taste, the only way to know it's present at elevated levels is to measure it. In the San Joaquin Valley, the geology isn't uniform. Clay-dominant soils can trap and channel soil gases differently than sandy or loamy profiles, and the way a specific parcel drains, or doesn't, affects how gases move toward a structure's foundation. Selma's older neighborhoods, many developed in the 1950s through 1970s, often feature slab-on-grade construction or shallow crawl spaces with limited vapor barriers, both of which are common entry points for radon infiltration. Newer construction in the area has generally improved on sub-slab ventilation, but age alone doesn't tell the whole story, a well-sealed newer home can accumulate radon just as readily as an older one if the pressure dynamics are right.

## Local Note: Valley Floor Pressure Dynamics in Summer

One pattern worth knowing in Selma and the surrounding valley floor: during the hottest stretches of summer, homes that run central air conditioning continuously can develop what's called a negative pressure differential, the HVAC system exhausts more air than it draws back in, and the house effectively pulls in replacement air from wherever it can find it, including soil gases through foundation penetrations. This is the same season when windows stay shut and natural ventilation is at its lowest. If your home tests higher in summer than a neighbor's tested in spring, the difference may be less about your soil and more about how your house breathes. Continuous radon monitoring captures this variation in a way a single passive test cannot.
