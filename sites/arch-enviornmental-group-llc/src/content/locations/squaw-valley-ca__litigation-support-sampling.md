## Why Squaw Valley Properties Generate Environmental Disputes

The Sierra Nevada climate is not gentle on buildings. Squaw Valley properties experience prolonged snow loads, ice damming at roof edges, and rapid spring melt that drives moisture into wall cavities and crawl spaces before anyone notices a stain on the ceiling. Many structures in this area were built as seasonal cabins and later converted to year-round use without the insulation, vapor barriers, or mechanical ventilation that a continuously occupied home requires. That combination of deferred maintenance, seasonal occupancy gaps, and high-humidity winters creates conditions where mold colonization can advance substantially before a tenant or buyer discovers it.

Disputes in this environment tend to cluster around a few recurring patterns: landlord-tenant disagreements over who caused a moisture intrusion, buyer-seller conflicts after a real estate transaction, and insurance coverage fights where the carrier argues that damage is long-term and excluded rather than sudden and covered. Each of those scenarios requires sampling data gathered under a documented protocol, not a quick visual inspection.

## Our Forensic Sampling and Litigation Support Process

Forensic environmental work differs from a standard pre-remediation assessment in one critical respect: every decision made in the field must anticipate cross-examination. Arch Environmental Group approaches each litigation support engagement with that standard in mind from the first site visit.

## Local Note: Seasonal Vacancy and Evidence Preservation in Mountain Properties

One factor that comes up repeatedly in Squaw Valley disputes is the gap between when damage occurs and when it is discovered. A property that sits unoccupied from November through April can develop significant mold growth behind paneling or under flooring before the owner returns in spring. By that point, secondary parties may have already entered the property, moved materials, or attempted informal cleanup. For litigation purposes, this means the chain of evidence is already complicated before sampling begins. When we are retained on a Squaw Valley property with a seasonal occupancy history, we document the physical state of the space in detail before any materials are disturbed, photograph all relevant surfaces, and note any signs of prior disturbance that could affect the interpretation of sample results. That documentation discipline is not optional in a contested case.
