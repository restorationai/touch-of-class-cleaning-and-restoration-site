## Why Hanford Properties Warrant a Closer Look at Radon

California as a whole is sometimes assumed to be low-risk for radon, and in many coastal areas that's reasonable. The Central Valley is a different story. The geology beneath Kings County, a mix of alluvial deposits, silts, and clay layers laid down over millennia by the Kings River and its tributaries, can concentrate naturally occurring uranium and radium in the soil. As those materials decay, radon gas migrates upward. Flat terrain means there's less topographic variation to disperse the gas before it reaches a foundation, and the region's hot, dry summers encourage homeowners to seal up their houses tightly for air conditioning efficiency, which can trap radon indoors rather than allowing it to dilute.

Postwar construction in Hanford frequently used pier-and-beam or raised-foundation designs, both of which can allow soil gas to accumulate in the crawl space before seeping through floor penetrations, plumbing chases, and gaps around utility entries. Older homes with original concrete block or unreinforced masonry foundations are particularly porous to gas migration.

## Our Radon Testing Process in Hanford

A reliable radon test requires more than dropping a passive charcoal canister and mailing it off. For Hanford properties, the approach depends on foundation type, home age, and whether the test is for a real estate transaction or ongoing monitoring.

For most residential tests, continuous electronic radon monitors are deployed in the lowest livable level of the home, which in many Hanford properties means the first floor above a crawl space or a slab-level room. Continuous monitors log readings at regular intervals over a minimum 48-hour closed-house period, capturing fluctuations that a single passive test can miss. The data is then analyzed against EPA action level thresholds and provided in a written report suitable for real estate disclosure, lender review, or personal health decision-making.

For homes with crawl spaces, the space itself is assessed for ventilation, vapor barrier condition, and any visible signs of soil disturbance that could affect readings. These details matter when interpreting results and, if mitigation is needed, when designing a system that actually addresses the source.
