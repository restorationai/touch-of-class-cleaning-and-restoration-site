Hanford sits in the heart of Kings County, a region shaped by decades of intensive agriculture, military-adjacent land use near the former Lemoore Naval Air Station corridor, and the kind of clay-heavy San Joaquin Valley soils that hold contaminants close to the surface long after the original source is gone. When a commercial property changes hands here, or a developer eyes a site along 11th Avenue or near the old downtown rail corridor, an environmental site assessment is not a formality. It is the document that tells you what the ground has been holding onto.

## Why Hanford Properties Carry Distinct Environmental Due Diligence Risk

Kings County's agricultural legacy means that parcels on or adjacent to former orchards, dairies, or packing operations frequently carry residual pesticide, nitrate, or petroleum hydrocarbon concerns that do not show up in a title search. Hanford's older commercial core, developed through the mid-twentieth century, includes properties that housed dry cleaners, auto repair shops, fuel distributors, and light manufacturing. Each of those land uses is a recognized environmental condition (REC) under ASTM E1527-21 standards, and each one can surface during a Phase 1 ESA records review before a single soil sample is collected.

The clay-dominant soils common across the valley floor beneath Hanford also matter for Phase 2 work. Clay retards vertical migration of contaminants, which sounds protective until you realize it also means contamination can spread laterally and persist at shallow depths for years. A Phase 2 environmental site assessment in this area often requires a different sampling grid than you would design for sandier coastal soils.

## Our Environmental Site Assessments Process in Hanford

A Phase 1 ESA begins with records research: federal and state regulatory databases, historical aerial photographs, Sanborn fire insurance maps where available, and county assessor records that trace ownership and land use back as far as documentation allows. For Hanford properties, that research frequently surfaces agricultural chemical storage, underground storage tanks associated with farm equipment fueling, or prior industrial tenants in buildings that now look entirely unremarkable.

The site reconnaissance follows. A qualified professional walks the property, photographs current conditions, interviews available occupants, and notes any staining, stressed vegetation, vent pipes, fill areas, or other visual indicators. The final ESA report documents all findings, assigns REC classifications, and either closes the file or recommends a Phase 2 investigation.

When a Phase 2 is warranted, the work moves to soil borings, groundwater sampling if the water table is within reach, and laboratory analysis. Results are compared against California Department of Toxic Substances Control (DTSC) and Regional Water Quality Control Board screening levels. The written report gives buyers, lenders, and developers the data they need to negotiate, remediate, or walk away with clear eyes.

## Local Note: What the Valley Floor Tells You Before You Sample

One thing experienced assessors notice in the Hanford area: properties near historic irrigation canal alignments sometimes show anomalous soil discoloration or subsidence that has nothing to do with chemical contamination and everything to do with historic canal seepage and subsequent soil consolidation. Flagging that distinction early in the Phase 1 reconnaissance prevents unnecessary Phase 2 costs. It also keeps the due diligence timeline tight, which matters when a purchase agreement has a fixed inspection period. Knowing the difference between a legacy irrigation artifact and a genuine REC comes from working in San Joaquin Valley soils specifically, not just from knowing the ASTM standard.
