## Why Squaw Valley Properties See Elevated Off-Gassing Concerns

Altitude and temperature swings matter more than most people realize when it comes to indoor air chemistry. At Squaw Valley's elevation, homes are insulated and weatherized aggressively to handle winter conditions. That tight building envelope is exactly what allows VOCs from composite wood products, adhesives, paints, and spray polyurethane foam to accumulate rather than dissipate. Formaldehyde, which off-gasses from pressed-wood furniture, laminate flooring, and certain insulation materials, reaches higher concentrations in enclosed spaces with limited air exchange.

The seasonal pattern here adds another layer. Many properties in the Squaw Valley area are used as vacation or part-time residences. A home that sits closed for weeks, then is opened for a ski season rental or a summer stay, can have dramatically elevated VOC levels after that period of stagnation. New construction and renovation projects in the area, where fresh materials are sealed inside before occupancy, are among the most common scenarios where testing reveals concentrations worth addressing.

## Our VOC and Formaldehyde Testing Process in Squaw Valley

Testing is methodical, not guesswork. When Arch Environmental Group LLC arrives at a Squaw Valley property, the process begins with a walkthrough to identify likely source materials: flooring installed within the last several years, cabinetry with particleboard cores, recent spray foam work, or any renovation that introduced new synthetic materials into the space.

Air samples are collected using calibrated equipment designed to capture a broad spectrum of volatile organic compounds, including formaldehyde specifically, at concentrations relevant to indoor health guidelines. Samples are analyzed by an accredited laboratory, and results are returned in a written report that identifies which compounds are present, at what concentrations, and how those figures compare to established reference levels from agencies such as the EPA and NIOSH. The report is written to be useful to a homeowner, not just to a scientist. If the results indicate a concern, the report includes context for next steps.
