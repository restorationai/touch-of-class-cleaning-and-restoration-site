Fresno's Central Valley climate is one of the most demanding environments for HVAC systems in California. Triple-digit summer heat pushes air conditioners to run almost continuously from June through September, and that relentless cycling draws dust, agricultural particulates, and outdoor allergens deep into ductwork. When a system that works this hard also develops a moisture problem, from a slow condensate drain leak, a poorly insulated supply line sweating in the attic, or even residual humidity from a past water loss, the interior of the duct system can become a hidden reservoir for mold, bacteria, and accumulated debris that circulates through every room in the house.

## Why Fresno Properties See HVAC and Duct Contamination Issues

Fresno sits in one of the most agriculturally active air basins in the country. Fine particulate matter from field operations, orchard dust, and seasonal harvests loads air filters faster than in coastal cities, and when filters aren't changed on schedule, that burden migrates past the filter media and coats duct interiors. The San Joaquin Valley's temperature swings, cold, tule-fog winters followed by scorching summers, also create condensation cycles inside poorly insulated ductwork, especially in older homes where flex duct was installed in unconditioned attic spaces.

Fresno's residential building stock spans several distinct eras. Tract homes built during the postwar expansion of the 1950s and 1960s often have original galvanized steel duct runs that have corroded at seams, allowing both air leakage and contaminant intrusion. Homes from the 1970s and 1980s frequently used fiberglass duct board, which can harbor mold growth when surface moisture is present and is difficult to clean without damaging the liner. Even newer construction in the city's expanding southeast corridor can develop duct contamination issues if the system was run during construction before final filtration was in place.

## Our HVAC and Duct System Assessment Process in Fresno

An assessment is not a cleaning estimate, it is a diagnostic. The goal is to determine what is actually present inside the system before any remediation decision is made.

The process begins with a visual inspection of accessible duct sections, air handler components, evaporator coils, and drain pans. In Fresno's climate, evaporator coils and drain pans are among the most common sites for microbial growth because condensation accumulates there every time the system runs during cooling season. We photograph conditions at each access point and note construction materials, visible debris loading, and any signs of moisture intrusion or biological growth.

Where visual inspection is insufficient, we collect samples, swab samples from duct surfaces, bulk material samples if suspect growth is present, or spore trap air samples drawn from supply registers and return air plenums. Samples are submitted to an accredited third-party laboratory. Our team holds NIOSH 582 microscopist credentials, which means we can evaluate fungal spore content analytically and interpret results in the context of what is actually normal for an inland California air environment rather than applying a one-size-fits-all threshold.

The written report identifies affected zones, characterizes contaminant type and distribution, and provides scope guidance for any remediation that may be warranted, or a clear statement that the system is within acceptable parameters and no remediation is needed.

## Equipment and Methods We Use for HVAC Assessment

Duct system assessments in a hot, dusty climate like Fresno's require equipment calibrated for the conditions. We use calibrated air sampling pumps with timed flow rates to collect spore trap and cassette samples that meet laboratory chain-of-custody requirements. Borescope cameras allow inspection of duct interiors beyond the first accessible section, critical in longer trunk-and-branch layouts common in single-story ranch homes throughout the city. Moisture meters and thermal imaging help identify areas where condensation or water intrusion may have created conditions favorable to microbial growth, even when the duct surface appears dry at the time of inspection.

## Local Note

In Fresno's older residential neighborhoods, many homes were built with attic duct systems installed before energy codes required insulation wrapping on flex duct. During tule fog season, when exterior temperatures drop sharply overnight while interior temperatures stay warmer, uninsulated or under-insulated supply ducts in unconditioned attics can sweat on the exterior surface, and that moisture can wick into the duct liner from the outside in. Homeowners often assume duct contamination comes only from indoor sources, but in Fresno, the thermal cycling between cold foggy winters and hot summers is a genuine external driver of moisture intrusion that any competent assessment should account for.
