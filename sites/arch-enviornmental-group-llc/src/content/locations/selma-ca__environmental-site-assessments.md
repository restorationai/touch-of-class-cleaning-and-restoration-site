Selma sits in the heart of the San Joaquin Valley, where decades of intensive agriculture, packing-house operations, and rail-corridor industrial activity have left a complicated environmental footprint beneath some of the area's most actively traded commercial parcels. If you're acquiring, financing, or developing property in Selma, an Environmental Site Assessment isn't a formality, it's the document that tells you what the land has actually been used for and whether that history creates liability you'd be inheriting along with the deed.

## Why Selma's Land History Makes Due Diligence Complicated

Fresno County's agricultural economy shaped Selma's development in ways that aren't always obvious from a title search. Pesticide and fumigant storage, underground fuel tanks serving farm equipment, and fruit-processing facilities that operated before modern environmental regulations were common throughout this corridor. Properties along the Highway 99 frontage and the Union Pacific rail line have seen a particularly wide range of industrial tenants over the decades, uses that may not appear in current records but can still show up in soil and groundwater.

The valley's clay-heavy soils compound the issue. Low permeability means that petroleum hydrocarbons and chlorinated solvents released decades ago may still be present at depth, moving slowly and unpredictably. A Phase I ESA surfaces the records; a Phase II tells you what the soil and groundwater actually contain.

## Our Environmental Site Assessment Process in Selma

A Phase I Environmental Site Assessment follows the ASTM E1527-21 standard and produces a report that satisfies lender, buyer, and regulatory requirements. The process involves a records review, federal and state databases, historical aerial photographs, Sanborn fire insurance maps, and county records, combined with a site reconnaissance and interviews with current and past owners or occupants.

For Selma properties, the records review often surfaces prior agricultural chemical storage, former dry-cleaning operations, or fuel dispensing equipment that predates current ownership. When the Phase I identifies a Recognized Environmental Condition (REC), a Phase II assessment moves the investigation from paper to the ground: soil borings, groundwater sampling, and laboratory analysis confirm whether contamination is present and at what concentrations.

## Local Note

One pattern worth knowing in Selma: the city's older commercial blocks were developed during an era when above-ground and underground storage tanks were installed with minimal documentation. When those businesses changed hands, sometimes multiple times, tank removals weren't always permitted or recorded consistently at the county level. A Phase I that relies only on state database searches can miss tanks that were removed informally before electronic records existed. Experienced site assessors supplement database pulls with historical aerial photo review and direct observation of surface indicators like stained concrete, vent pipes, or fill ports that were capped rather than properly closed. That ground-level reconnaissance step is where a thorough Phase I earns its cost.

## What an ESA Report Covers, and What Comes Next

The deliverable is a written report that documents all findings, identifies RECs, and includes the environmental professional's opinion on whether further investigation is warranted. For transactions with lender involvement, the report typically needs to meet All Appropriate Inquiries (AAI) requirements under CERCLA, the standard that preserves innocent landowner liability protections.

If the Phase I is clean, you proceed with confidence. If RECs are identified, the Phase II data either confirms the property is within acceptable risk thresholds or quantifies what remediation would require, information that changes negotiating positions, deal structures, and sometimes the decision to proceed at all. Either outcome is more useful than closing without knowing.
