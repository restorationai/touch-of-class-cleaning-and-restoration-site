## Why Selma's Building Stock Makes Asbestos Testing Worth Taking Seriously

The San Joaquin Valley's postwar population boom pushed Selma's residential neighborhoods outward quickly through the 1950s, 1960s, and into the 1970s. Builders during those decades routinely used asbestos in joint compound, vinyl floor tiles, acoustic spray-on ceilings, duct insulation, and roofing shingles, not out of negligence, but because it was the industry standard at the time. Many of those materials are still in place today, hidden behind fresh paint or covered by newer flooring.

The Valley's climate adds another layer of concern. Extreme summer heat causes building materials to expand and contract repeatedly over decades, which can degrade the binders holding asbestos fibers in place. A popcorn ceiling that has been through forty San Joaquin summers may be more friable, meaning more likely to release fibers when disturbed, than the same material in a more temperate region. That's not a reason to panic, but it is a reason to test before you sand, scrape, or demo anything.

Selma's agricultural economy also means a significant share of properties include older outbuildings, packing sheds, and equipment storage structures that predate modern building codes. These structures are often overlooked during renovation planning, yet they frequently contain asbestos-cement board siding and roofing that requires professional assessment before any work touches them.

## Our Asbestos Testing and Inspection Process in Selma

Every inspection begins with a thorough visual walkthrough of the property. A Certified Asbestos Consultant identifies suspect materials, anything from ceiling texture and floor tile mastic to pipe wrap and attic insulation, and documents their location, condition, and approximate quantity. Friable materials (those that crumble easily) are prioritized because they pose the greatest potential for fiber release during disturbance.

Bulk samples are collected following established protocols: a small amount of the suspect material is carefully removed, sealed, and labeled for chain-of-custody tracking. Samples are then submitted to an accredited laboratory for polarized light microscopy (PLM) analysis, which identifies asbestos fiber type and concentration. Our NIOSH 582 Microscopist credential means we understand exactly what the lab is looking for and how to interpret results in the context of your specific project.

Once results are back, you receive a written report that identifies which materials contain asbestos, at what percentage, and what regulatory thresholds apply to your planned scope of work. That report is what your contractor, your local building department, and, if applicable, your insurance carrier will need before any regulated work proceeds.

## Local Note: What Selma Renovators Often Miss

One pattern that comes up repeatedly in San Joaquin Valley cities like Selma is the layered renovation problem. A home built in the 1950s may have had its original linoleum covered by a second layer of vinyl tile in the 1970s, then covered again by hardwood or laminate in the 2000s. Each of those underlayers can contain asbestos-containing adhesive or tile material, and when a contractor goes to pull the top layer, they're unknowingly disturbing the ones beneath. A proper asbestos survey samples each distinct layer separately, not just the visible surface, so that nothing gets missed before demolition begins.
