Parlier sits in the heart of the San Joaquin Valley, where decades of intensive agricultural activity, agrochemical application, and industrial-adjacent land use have left a complex environmental footprint beneath properties that today look perfectly ordinary from the street. Whether you are acquiring a commercial parcel along Manning Avenue, refinancing a mixed-use lot, or repositioning agricultural land for a new use, an Environmental Site Assessment is the due-diligence step that protects your investment before the transaction closes.

## Why Parlier Properties Carry Distinct Environmental Risk

The San Joaquin Valley's agricultural economy is its strength, but that history creates specific subsurface concerns that a standard desktop review will not catch without local context. Parlier and the surrounding Fresno County corridor saw heavy use of organochlorine pesticides, fumigants, and petroleum-based fertilizer equipment through the mid-to-late twentieth century. Fuel storage for farm equipment, on-site pesticide mixing stations, and aging underground storage tanks associated with packing sheds and co-ops are common findings on parcels that have changed hands multiple times since the 1950s.

The valley's clay-heavy soils, common throughout this part of Fresno County, tend to retain contaminants rather than flush them. Shallow groundwater in low-lying areas can migrate pollutants laterally across property lines, meaning a recognized environmental condition on an adjacent parcel can become your liability if it is not identified and documented before you close. The dry summers and wet winters also create seasonal cracking in clay soils that can channel surface spills downward faster than most property owners expect.

## Our Environmental Site Assessment Process in Parlier

A Phase I Environmental Site Assessment begins with a thorough review of historical records: aerial photographs, Sanborn fire insurance maps where available, regulatory agency databases, and chain-of-title documents. For Parlier properties, that research often extends back to packing house operations, dairy use, and orchard-era chemical storage, all of which leave a paper trail that trained reviewers know how to find.

The site reconnaissance visit is where desktop research meets ground truth. We walk the property systematically, noting staining patterns, stressed vegetation, unusual odors, vent pipes, fill areas, and the condition of any above-ground storage tanks or hydraulic equipment. We document neighboring land use as well, because a Phase I is not just about the subject parcel.

If the Phase I identifies a Recognized Environmental Condition (REC), a Phase II Assessment moves from observation to sampling. Soil borings, groundwater monitoring wells, and laboratory analysis confirm or rule out contamination at specific locations and depths. Phase II scope is always calibrated to what the Phase I actually found, not a generic sampling grid. For Parlier parcels with agricultural history, that often means targeted sampling around former equipment yards, irrigation pump stations, and areas where aerial photograph review suggests historical structures have been removed.

All work is conducted and reviewed in accordance with ASTM E1527-21 for Phase I and ASTM E1903 for Phase II, the standards lenders, attorneys, and California regulators expect to see in a final ESA report.

One pattern worth knowing if you are evaluating agricultural or transitional land in this part of Fresno County: older packing sheds and cold-storage facilities in the region frequently used ammonia refrigeration systems, and the brine pits or evaporation areas associated with those systems are not always visible on the surface today. Vegetation die-off in an otherwise irrigated area, or an unexplained depression in a yard or equipment pad, can be a surface indicator worth flagging before you proceed.
