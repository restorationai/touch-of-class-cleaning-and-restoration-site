## Why Bakersfield Properties Face Distinct Water Quality Concerns

The San Joaquin Valley's geology is not incidental to water quality. Decades of intensive agriculture across Kern County have left nitrate concentrations elevated in portions of the local groundwater basin, a pattern documented by state regulators for years. Arsenic, which occurs naturally in the valley's sedimentary soils, is another contaminant that shows up in well water at levels worth monitoring, particularly for properties on the eastern and western fringes of the city where municipal water service gives way to private wells.

Inside older Bakersfield homes, the concern shifts. Properties built before 1986 may still have lead solder at pipe joints or, in the oldest housing stock, lead service lines connecting the street main to the meter. Lead does not change the color or taste of water, which is exactly what makes it dangerous. A certified lead-in-water test is the only reliable way to know whether older plumbing is contributing to exposure.

Bakersfield's heat also plays a role. Summer temperatures that routinely exceed 100 degrees Fahrenheit accelerate bacterial growth in water that sits in storage tanks, rooftop units, or long runs of pipe that are not regularly flushed. Coliform bacteria testing is a sensible precaution for any property with supplemental storage or after a period of low water use.

## Our Water Quality Testing Process in Bakersfield

Every engagement starts with a site assessment. Before collecting samples, a technician reviews the property's water source, plumbing age, and any recent changes to service, such as pipe repairs, water heater replacements, or a shift in the municipal supply zone. That context shapes which tests are ordered, because a blanket panel applied without local knowledge can miss the contaminants most relevant to a specific address.

Samples are collected following chain-of-custody protocols and submitted to an accredited laboratory. Depending on the panel selected, results typically cover bacteria (total coliform and E. coli), nitrates, heavy metals including lead and arsenic, pH, hardness, and any additional parameters flagged during the site review. When results arrive, a written report translates the laboratory data into plain language, noting which values exceed EPA maximum contaminant levels and what remediation options exist if action is needed.

Because Arch Environmental Group holds certifications as a Lead Inspector and Assessor, lead-in-water testing on this project is conducted under the same credentialed framework used for lead paint and dust assessments, not as an add-on handled by a generalist.

## Local Note: Valley Fever Country and Your Water

Kern County sits in one of the highest-incidence regions in the state for Valley Fever, caused by a fungus in the soil. While Valley Fever is an airborne concern rather than a waterborne one, the same dry, dusty conditions that stir up soil also affect how surface water and shallow groundwater interact with contaminants. During the dry season, when dust events are common and the water table drops, contaminants that were diluted by a higher water table can become more concentrated in private wells. Homeowners on well water in and around Bakersfield are well-served by testing in late summer or early fall, after the longest dry stretch of the year, rather than waiting until a problem is visible.

If your property has a water softener or filtration system already in place, testing the water at the tap after treatment, not just at the source, confirms whether the system is actually performing as intended. Filters and softeners degrade over time, and Bakersfield's hard water accelerates that wear.
