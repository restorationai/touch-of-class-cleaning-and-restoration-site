## Why Clovis Commercial Properties Warrant Close Environmental Scrutiny

The San Joaquin Valley's agricultural legacy runs deep beneath Clovis's newer commercial footprint. Parcels that were orchards or dairies within living memory may carry residual pesticide compounds, petroleum hydrocarbons from on-site fueling operations, or nitrate loading from historical fertilizer use. The region's clay-heavy soils, common in the valley floor geology underlying much of Fresno County, can trap contaminants near the surface rather than allowing them to migrate and dilute, which means a spill from decades ago may still be detectable at concentrations that matter to a lender or a future tenant.

Clovis has also grown quickly. Strip commercial centers, auto-related businesses, and light industrial uses that were built along major corridors in the 1970s through 1990s are now being repositioned as the city's development pressure pushes eastward. Older dry cleaners, gas stations, and machine shops, the classic recognized environmental conditions that Phase I assessors look for, are woven into that commercial fabric. A thorough records review, site reconnaissance, and interviews with knowledgeable parties are not formalities here; they are the work.

## The Phase I and Phase II ESA Process, Calibrated to Clovis Conditions

A Phase I Environmental Site Assessment follows ASTM Standard E1527-21 and is the starting point for virtually every commercial real estate transaction requiring lender sign-off. The scope includes a regulatory database search, a review of historical aerial photographs and Sanborn fire insurance maps where available, a physical site visit, and interviews, all synthesized into a written report that identifies recognized environmental conditions (RECs), controlled RECs, and historical RECs.

In Clovis and the surrounding Fresno County area, the regulatory database search commonly surfaces entries from the State Water Resources Control Board's GeoTracker system, CalEPA's EnviroStar database, and the Department of Toxic Substances Control's Cortese List. Agricultural parcels may also require a review of Department of Pesticide Regulation records. These are not databases that every assessor outside the Central Valley routinely navigates, familiarity with how California's overlapping regulatory frameworks apply to this specific geography matters.

When a Phase I identifies conditions that cannot be resolved through records alone, a Phase II ESA moves the work into the field: soil borings, groundwater sampling, soil vapor surveys, or laboratory analysis of collected samples. The goal is to characterize what is actually present so that a buyer, seller, or lender can make an informed decision rather than guessing at remediation liability.
