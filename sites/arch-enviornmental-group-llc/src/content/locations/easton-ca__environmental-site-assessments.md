Whether you are acquiring a former farmstead on the valley floor, converting agricultural land to commercial use, or financing a warehouse near a rural highway corridor, an Environmental Site Assessment is not a formality here, it is a genuine due-diligence tool shaped by the land's history. ## Why Easton-Area Properties Carry Distinct Environmental Risk

The San Joaquin Valley's agricultural legacy is the defining factor in Easton's environmental profile. Pesticide and herbicide applications accumulated over generations can leave residual organochlorine compounds and heavy metals in the upper soil horizons, findings that are common enough in this corridor that a Phase 1 ESA here routinely looks harder at recognized environmental conditions tied to farming operations than it might in a purely urban setting.

Irrigation infrastructure adds another layer. Properties near lateral canals or historic drainage ditches may have experienced petroleum releases from pump engines or fuel storage tanks that were never formally decommissioned. The region's clay-dominant soils, while slow to drain, can also trap contaminants near the surface rather than allowing them to migrate quickly, which means a release that looks minor can have a larger footprint than the same release in sandier ground. Any commercial property environmental assessment in this area needs to account for that soil behavior when evaluating the likelihood and extent of subsurface impact.

## Our Environmental Site Assessment Process in Easton

A Phase 1 environmental site assessment follows the ASTM E1527-21 standard regardless of where the property sits, but the research emphasis shifts based on local conditions. For Easton-area engagements, the historical records review pays particular attention to aerial photography sequences that document land use changes from row-crop agriculture through any interim uses, as well as Fresno County records for underground storage tank registrations and agricultural well permits.

Site reconnaissance focuses on surface staining, stressed vegetation, the presence of abandoned equipment or chemical storage areas, and any structures that may contain asbestos-containing materials or lead-based paint, especially relevant in older ranch buildings and packing sheds that predate modern hazardous materials regulations. Our team holds certifications as Certified Asbestos Consultant and Lead Inspector Assessor, so observations made during a Phase 1 walkthrough that raise building-material concerns can be addressed within the same engagement rather than requiring a separate mobilization.

When Phase 1 findings identify a recognized environmental condition that warrants further investigation, a Phase 2 ESA moves the work into soil sampling, groundwater screening, or soil vapor assessment. Sampling locations are selected based on the specific RECs identified, the site's drainage patterns, and the clay-layer depth, factors that vary meaningfully across even a short distance in the valley floor geology.

One pattern that comes up repeatedly on valley floor parcels in this part of Fresno County: historic aerial photography from the 1940s through the 1970s often shows small fuel dispensing areas adjacent to equipment barns that no longer exist as structures.
