## Why Centerville Properties Warrant Close Environmental Scrutiny

The San Joaquin Valley's agricultural legacy is inseparable from its environmental risk profile. Centerville and the surrounding Fresno County corridor have historically supported orchards, dairies, fuel storage operations, and small-scale industrial uses, often on the same parcels that are now being repositioned for commercial or mixed-use development. Pesticide and herbicide migration into shallow soils is a documented concern across the Valley, and properties that once hosted above-ground or underground storage tanks for irrigation equipment, farm vehicles, or fuel distribution can carry petroleum hydrocarbon impacts that are not visible at the surface.

The region's clay-heavy soils, common throughout the Valley floor, tend to hold contaminants rather than flush them, which means impacts from historical uses can persist long after those uses ended. Shallow groundwater depth in parts of Fresno County adds another layer of complexity: a release that might be confined to the vadose zone in a drier, sandier setting can reach groundwater more readily here. These are not hypothetical concerns, they are the practical realities that shape what a Phase 1 ESA needs to examine in this geography.

## Our Environmental Site Assessment Process in Centerville

A Phase 1 Environmental Site Assessment follows the ASTM E1527-21 standard and is designed to identify Recognized Environmental Conditions (RECs) without subsurface sampling. For Centerville properties, that means a records review that goes beyond the standard federal and state database search. Agricultural use history, Fresno County permit records, historical aerial photography, and Sanborn fire insurance maps (where available for older commercial corridors) all contribute to a complete picture of what a parcel has been used for and by whom.

The site reconnaissance component is where local knowledge matters. Arch Environmental Group's consultants look for the physical signatures of historical agricultural and industrial use: stained soil, stressed vegetation patterns, abandoned piping, vent pipes, or fill areas that suggest buried infrastructure. Interviews with current and former owners or operators, when accessible, round out the record.

When a Phase 1 identifies RECs that warrant further investigation, a Phase 2 ESA moves into subsurface sampling, soil borings, groundwater monitoring, and laboratory analysis calibrated to the contaminants of concern identified in Phase 1. In the Centerville area, that frequently means screening for organochlorine pesticides, petroleum hydrocarbons, and nitrates, given the region's land use history. The resulting ESA report documents findings, data gaps, and recommended next steps in a format recognized by lenders, buyers, and California regulatory agencies.
