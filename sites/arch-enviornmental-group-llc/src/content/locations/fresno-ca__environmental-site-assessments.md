Fresno's position at the heart of the San Joaquin Valley means commercial real estate here carries environmental histories that don't always show up in a title search. Decades of intensive agriculture, fuel storage at packing houses and equipment yards, and dry-cleaning operations scattered through older commercial corridors have left a legacy of soil and groundwater concerns that any serious buyer, lender, or developer needs to understand before closing. An Environmental Site Assessment is the instrument that surfaces those concerns, and in Fresno's regulatory and geological context, getting it right matters more than getting it fast.

## Why Fresno Properties Carry Distinct Environmental Risk

The San Joaquin Valley's geology works against easy cleanup. The clay-rich soils common across much of Fresno County slow vertical drainage, which means petroleum hydrocarbons and chlorinated solvents released decades ago can still be sitting in the shallow subsurface today rather than having migrated to depth. That same clay layer also complicates Phase II sampling: a boring that looks clean at two feet can intercept a contamination plume at eight.

Fresno's commercial history adds another layer. The city grew rapidly through the mid-twentieth century as an agricultural processing hub, and many of the light-industrial and mixed-use parcels along its older commercial corridors, particularly properties that housed fuel dealers, chemical applicators, or dry-cleaning operations, changed hands multiple times without formal environmental review. Properties that were rezoned from agricultural or industrial use to commercial or multi-family residential are especially worth scrutinizing, because prior land use rarely appears on the face of a deed.

California's regulatory environment adds cost and timeline pressure that buyers and lenders need to anticipate. The State Water Resources Control Board's GeoTracker database is the first stop for known release sites, and the Department of Toxic Substances Control maintains its own Envirostor records. A thorough Phase I review cross-references both, along with Fresno County records and historical aerial photography, to build an honest picture of what a parcel has been used for.

## Our Environmental Site Assessments Process in Fresno

A Phase I Environmental Site Assessment follows ASTM Standard E1527-21, the current benchmark recognized by lenders and the EPA. The work begins with records research, federal and state regulatory databases, historical Sanborn fire insurance maps, aerial photographs, and city directory records that trace occupancy decade by decade. That desk research is followed by a site reconnaissance: a physical walkthrough to observe current conditions, note any recognized environmental conditions (RECs), and document observations with photographs.

If the Phase I identifies RECs that require confirmation, a Phase II assessment moves from observation to sampling. Soil borings, groundwater monitoring, or soil-gas surveys are selected based on the contaminants of concern and the site's geology. In Fresno's clay-heavy soils, boring locations and depths are chosen carefully to avoid missing a plume that has spread laterally rather than vertically. Laboratory analysis follows, with results interpreted against California's applicable screening levels and, where relevant, the specific exposure assumptions tied to the proposed use of the property.

The final ESA report documents findings, conclusions, and, where contamination is confirmed, recommendations for further action or remediation. That report is written to satisfy lender requirements, support CEQA review where applicable, and give buyers a clear basis for negotiating or walking away.

## Local Note

One pattern worth knowing: properties along Fresno's older industrial corridors that were once served by underground fuel storage tanks, common at equipment dealers and packing operations active through the 1970s and 1980s, frequently have tank records that were filed with the local fire authority rather than with the state UST program. Those records don't always surface in a standard GeoTracker search. A thorough Phase I for those parcels includes a direct records request to the Fresno County Environmental Health Division, which maintains its own historical UST files. Skipping that step is a common gap in assessments prepared by firms unfamiliar with how Fresno County organized its pre-digital records.
