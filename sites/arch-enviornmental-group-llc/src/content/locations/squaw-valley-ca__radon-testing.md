Squaw Valley sits in the Sierra Nevada foothills at elevations where granite bedrock and decomposed granitic soils are the norm, and that geology matters when it comes to radon. Uranium-bearing granite releases radon gas as it decays, and homes built directly over or into this kind of terrain can accumulate radon in crawl spaces, basements, and slab-on-grade foundations at levels that deserve serious attention. If you are buying, selling, or simply living in a home in this part of Fresno County, a professional radon test is one of the most straightforward steps you can take to understand what the air in your home actually contains.

## Why Squaw Valley Properties See Elevated Radon Risk

The Sierra Nevada foothills are geologically distinct from California's Central Valley floor. The granitic and metamorphic rock formations common to this elevation band are naturally higher in uranium and radium than the sedimentary soils found further west. As those minerals decay, radon gas migrates upward through soil and rock fissures. Homes in Squaw Valley and the surrounding Fresno County foothill communities tend to sit closer to that source material, and many were built with crawl spaces or partial basements that give the gas a direct pathway into living areas.

Cooler nighttime temperatures at this elevation also create negative pressure inside homes as warm indoor air rises and escapes, drawing soil gases inward through foundation cracks, utility penetrations, and gaps around pipe sleeves. That stack effect is more pronounced in mountain and foothill homes than in valley construction, and it means radon concentrations can be meaningfully higher in winter months when windows stay closed and ventilation is reduced.

## Our Radon Testing Process in Squaw Valley

Arch Environmental Group uses calibrated continuous electronic radon monitors rather than passive charcoal canisters for most residential tests. Continuous monitors record radon levels hour by hour across the full testing period, which is typically a minimum of 48 hours for a short-term test conducted under closed-building conditions. That hourly data is useful in foothill homes because it can reveal the stack-effect pattern described above: radon levels that climb overnight and ease during the warmer afternoon hours. A single averaged number from a charcoal canister would obscure that pattern entirely.

Before placing monitors, we identify the lowest livable level of the home, since radon concentrates at its highest in the space closest to the soil. We also document foundation type, visible penetrations, and any existing sub-slab or crawl-space ventilation, because those details inform both the test placement and any mitigation recommendations that follow. The EPA action level is 4 picocuries per liter; we report results against that benchmark and explain what the numbers mean in plain language.
