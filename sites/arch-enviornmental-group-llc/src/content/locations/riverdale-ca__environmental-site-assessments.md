Riverdale sits in the heart of the San Joaquin Valley, where decades of intensive agriculture, irrigation canal infrastructure, and proximity to Highway 99 corridor industrial activity have left a layered environmental history beneath many commercial and agricultural parcels. If you are buying, selling, or financing property in this part of Fresno County, an Environmental Site Assessment is not a formality, it is the document that tells you what the ground has actually absorbed over the past century of Valley farming and light industry.

## Why Riverdale Properties Carry Distinct Environmental Due Diligence Challenges

The San Joaquin Valley's agricultural legacy is the defining factor in ESA work around Riverdale. Organochlorine pesticides, fumigants, and petroleum-based soil amendments applied across decades of row-crop and orchard farming can persist in the vadose zone long after a field is converted to commercial or residential use. Shallow clay hardpan layers common to this part of Fresno County can trap contaminants near the surface rather than allowing them to migrate downward, which means a Phase 1 ESA that flags a recognized environmental condition here often leads directly to a focused Phase 2 investigation of the upper soil profile.

Irrigation infrastructure is another variable. Older lateral canals and drainage sumps associated with Fresno Irrigation District delivery systems have, in some cases, been abandoned in place beneath parcels that have since been subdivided or repurposed. A thorough records review during Phase 1 work looks specifically for that kind of buried infrastructure because it can affect both soil chemistry and subsurface stability.

Highway 99 corridor parcels also carry elevated scrutiny. Fueling stations, equipment yards, and trucking operations that operated along the corridor over the past several decades are common RECs (Recognized Environmental Conditions) in this region, and lenders financing commercial acquisitions near those corridors increasingly require ESA documentation before closing.

## Our Environmental Site Assessment Process in Riverdale

A Phase 1 ESA follows the ASTM E1527-21 standard and consists of four core components: a regulatory database review, historical records research, a site reconnaissance, and interviews with knowledgeable parties. In Fresno County, the regulatory review pulls from the State Water Board's GeoTracker and Cortese list databases, Fresno County Environmental Health records, and federal CERCLIS and RCRA databases. Historical aerial photography is particularly valuable in the Valley because it can document orchard-to-row-crop transitions, former equipment storage areas, and the footprints of structures that no longer exist.

The site reconnaissance for a Riverdale parcel pays close attention to surface staining near former equipment storage, the condition of any on-site wells or sumps, evidence of underground storage tanks, and the presence of asbestos-containing materials or lead-based paint in any structures on the property. Arch Environmental Group's certifications in asbestos consulting and lead inspection and assessment mean that when a Phase 1 reconnaissance identifies building materials that warrant closer examination, that evaluation can happen in the same site visit rather than requiring a separate mobilization.

When Phase 1 findings support it, a Phase 2 ESA moves to soil borings, soil gas sampling, or groundwater monitoring, with laboratory analysis calibrated to the specific RECs identified in Phase 1. The final ESA report documents findings, conclusions, and recommendations in a format acceptable to lenders, buyers, and California regulatory agencies.

## Local Note

One detail that catches buyers off guard on Riverdale-area agricultural conversion parcels: former orchard blocks often have buried root systems and decomposing organic matter that can create localized soil gas conditions, including elevated methane readings, years after trees are removed. This is distinct from petroleum-related soil gas and requires a different interpretive framework during Phase 2 sampling. Knowing to look for it, and knowing how to document it accurately in the ESA report, can prevent a straightforward agricultural conversion from being misread as a petroleum release site by a lender's environmental reviewer.
