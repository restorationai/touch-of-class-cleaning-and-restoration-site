Bakersfield sits at the southern end of the San Joaquin Valley on land shaped by decades of oil extraction, agricultural chemical use, and rapid commercial development along corridors like Rosedale Highway and the old Union Avenue industrial belt. That history means a commercial property transaction here carries environmental questions that a standard title search simply cannot answer. An Environmental Site Assessment connects the dots between what a parcel looks like today and what happened on or near it going back generations, giving buyers, lenders, and developers the documentation they need before a deal closes.

## Why Bakersfield Properties Carry Distinct Environmental Risk

Kern County has one of the highest concentrations of active and idle oil wells in California. Wells have been drilled, capped, re-opened, and abandoned across the valley floor for well over a century, and many of those records are incomplete or held across multiple state and county databases. A property that looks like a straightforward commercial lot on the west side of town may sit within a recognized environmental condition radius of a former tank battery or injection well. Beyond petroleum, the valley's long agricultural history means organochlorine pesticide residues and nitrate-impacted groundwater are documented concerns in many parts of Kern County. Buyers relying only on visual inspection routinely miss subsurface conditions that a Phase 1 ESA is specifically designed to surface.

The region's climate adds a practical complication. Bakersfield averages very little annual rainfall, which means soil contaminants tend to stay concentrated near the source rather than dispersing through the vadose zone the way they might in wetter climates. That can actually work against a buyer: a dry, undisturbed plume may look benign at the surface while remaining stable and concentrated at depth, exactly where a Phase 2 investigation needs to look.

## Our Environmental Site Assessments Process in Bakersfield

A Phase 1 Environmental Site Assessment follows the ASTM E1527-21 standard and involves four core components: records review, site reconnaissance, interviews, and a written report. For Bakersfield properties, the records review requires pulling from the State Water Resources Control Board's GeoTracker database, the Department of Conservation's well records, Kern County environmental health files, and federal EPA databases, because no single source captures the full picture of this region's industrial past.

Site reconnaissance in Bakersfield often means paying close attention to surface staining, stressed vegetation, and the presence of vent pipes or fill ports that can indicate underground storage tanks, particularly on older commercial parcels along truxtun Avenue and the downtown core that were developed before modern tank regulations took effect. If the Phase 1 identifies a recognized environmental condition that warrants further investigation, a Phase 2 assessment moves the work into the subsurface: soil borings, groundwater sampling, and laboratory analysis to determine whether contamination is present and at what concentrations.

Arch Environmental Group holds certifications as a Certified Asbestos Consultant, Lead Inspector Assessor, and NIOSH 582 Microscopist, which matters when a property assessment uncovers building materials that need evaluation alongside the soil and groundwater work. Older commercial buildings in Bakersfield frequently contain asbestos-containing materials in floor tile, roofing, and pipe insulation, and a team credentialed to assess both the site and the structure can consolidate that due diligence into a single engagement.

## Local Note

One thing that comes up repeatedly on Bakersfield commercial assessments is the gap between county assessor parcel maps and the actual operational footprint of former businesses. The city grew quickly during mid-century oil booms, and small industrial operations, auto repair shops, and fuel distributors often operated on parcels that were later subdivided or absorbed into larger commercial lots. A Phase 1 that only searches the current APN without tracing historical parcel configurations can miss a former dry cleaner or fuel storage area that is now technically part of an adjacent address. Thorough historical aerial photo review, going back to the earliest available imagery for the area, is a step that pays for itself on Bakersfield properties more often than in many other California markets.
