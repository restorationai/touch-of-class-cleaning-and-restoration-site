## Why Selma Properties See Elevated VOC and Formaldehyde Concerns

The San Joaquin Valley's climate is one of the most significant variables in how volatile organic compounds behave indoors. Heat is a catalyst: the warmer a space, the faster adhesives, engineered wood products, vinyl flooring, paints, and insulation shed their chemical load into the air. A home that tests within acceptable limits in February can show meaningfully higher formaldehyde concentrations by July, simply because the temperature inside has climbed.

Selma has also seen substantial residential development in recent years, bringing with it the materials most associated with off-gassing complaints, laminate flooring, composite cabinetry, spray foam insulation, and low-VOC (but not zero-VOC) paints that still emit during cure. Older homes in the area present a different profile: decades of accumulated paint layers, older adhesives, and sometimes original flooring materials that weren't formulated with today's indoor air standards in mind. Neither new nor old construction is automatically safe, they just have different chemical fingerprints.

## Our VOC and Formaldehyde Testing Process in Selma

Testing starts before any equipment is placed. A site walkthrough identifies likely emission sources, recently installed flooring, cabinetry, HVAC systems that may be redistributing contaminants, attached garages where vehicle exhaust or stored chemicals can migrate indoors. Formaldehyde testing typically uses NIOSH-validated methods, and the analysis is conducted by an accredited laboratory. ## Equipment and Methods We Use for VOC Testing

Formaldehyde is measured separately from the broader VOC panel because it requires its own sampling media and analytical pathway. The broader VOC screen uses TO-15 or TO-17 methods depending on whether the target compounds are best captured on Summa canisters or sorbent tubes. Both approaches yield laboratory-confirmed data, not field-meter estimates, which matters when results may be shared with a landlord, employer, insurance carrier, or regulatory agency.

For new construction or post-renovation testing, samples are typically collected under closed-building conditions to reflect realistic occupant exposure, not with windows open and fans running, which would artificially suppress concentrations and understate the actual risk.
