## Why Hanford Properties Carry a Higher Asbestos Risk

The San Joaquin Valley experienced rapid residential and commercial development from the 1940s through the late 1970s, and Hanford was no exception. Tract housing, civic buildings, schools, and agricultural support structures built during that window were routinely constructed with asbestos-containing materials: floor tiles, pipe insulation, roofing felts, duct wrap, textured ceiling coatings, and joint compounds. California did not phase asbestos out of most building products until the late 1970s and early 1980s, meaning any Hanford structure built or substantially renovated before roughly 1980 is a candidate for a professional inspection.

The Central Valley's climate adds another layer of concern. Extreme summer heat causes building materials to expand and contract repeatedly over decades, and that thermal cycling can degrade the binders in older floor tiles and ceiling textures. Friable asbestos, meaning material that can be crumbled by hand pressure, releases fibers more readily than intact material. In older Hanford homes where attic temperatures routinely exceed 140 degrees Fahrenheit in July and August, duct insulation and attic decking materials are worth examining closely before any HVAC work or re-roofing project begins.

## Our Asbestos Testing and Inspection Process in Hanford

A professional asbestos inspection is not a visual pass-through. Our certified asbestos consultant conducts a systematic survey of the structure, identifying suspect materials by location, condition, and accessibility. Samples are collected following established protocols: bulk samples are taken from representative areas of each suspect material, sealed, labeled, and submitted to an accredited laboratory for polarized light microscopy analysis, the method recognized by California's regulatory framework for building material testing.

For Hanford renovation projects, we pay particular attention to materials that are often overlooked: the texture on popcorn ceilings applied before 1980, the 9-inch or 12-inch floor tiles common in mid-century tract homes, the paper-faced duct insulation on older forced-air systems, and the transite panels sometimes used on exterior soffits and utility buildings. Each of these has a distinct sampling approach, and the laboratory turnaround gives you documented results you can share with your contractor, your real estate agent, or a prospective buyer.

Once results are returned, we provide a written report that identifies which materials tested positive, the percentage of asbestos by composition, the condition and friability of each material, and a recommended course of action. That documentation is what California-licensed abatement contractors need before they can legally disturb the material.
