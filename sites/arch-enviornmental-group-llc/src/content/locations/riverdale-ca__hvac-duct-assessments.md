## Why Riverdale Properties See HVAC Contamination Issues

The San Joaquin Valley's climate is not gentle on mechanical systems. During summer, HVAC equipment runs almost continuously, drawing in outdoor air that carries fine agricultural particulates, pesticide drift, and Valley Fever spores (Coccidioides) native to the region's dry soils. When that air passes through a system that has any moisture intrusion, condensation buildup around the evaporator coil, or a deteriorating duct liner, the interior surfaces become a surface where biological growth can establish.

Winter tule fog adds a different pressure. Dense ground fog persists for days at a time across the Fresno County floor, pushing ambient humidity into structures through crawl spaces, attic vents, and return-air plenums. Older homes in the Riverdale area, many built with minimal vapor barriers and single-pane windows, are particularly susceptible to moisture migration that ends up inside the duct system before anyone notices a problem at the register level.

Farm-adjacent properties face an additional layer of concern: pesticide and herbicide residues can accumulate on duct surfaces over years of operation, and standard visual inspections miss what laboratory analysis of collected samples can confirm.

## Our HVAC and Duct System Assessment Process

An assessment from Arch Environmental Group begins with a systematic visual inspection of accessible ductwork, air handling units, coil compartments, and return-air pathways. We document the condition of insulation, liner integrity, and any visible staining or biological growth using photographic records that can support insurance documentation or a property disclosure.

Where visual inspection alone is insufficient, we collect bulk samples, swab samples, or air samples from within the duct system itself. As a certified asbestos consultant and lead inspector assessor with NIOSH 582 microscopist credentials, our team is equipped to identify not just mold but also fibrous particulates, including older duct insulation materials that may contain asbestos, a real consideration in San Joaquin Valley construction from the 1950s through the early 1980s.

Samples are submitted to an accredited laboratory, and findings are compiled into a written report that identifies the nature and extent of contamination, the likely source, and a prioritized list of recommended corrective actions. We do not perform the remediation ourselves, which means our assessment findings are independent and carry weight with insurers, property managers, and prospective buyers.

## Equipment and Methods for HVAC Assessment

Duct system assessments require more than a flashlight and a swab. We use borescope cameras to inspect interior duct runs that are not accessible through standard register openings, allowing us to document conditions deep inside the system without destructive access. Air sampling pumps calibrated to NIOSH and AIHA protocols collect viable and non-viable spore samples from within the airstream, giving laboratory analysts an accurate picture of what is actually being delivered to occupied spaces.

For properties where asbestos-containing duct insulation or duct tape is a possibility, we follow California OSHA and Cal/EPA protocols for sample collection and handling, ensuring that the assessment process itself does not create a secondary exposure risk.

One pattern worth knowing in the Riverdale area: evaporative coolers, sometimes called swamp coolers, are common on older agricultural-area homes as a lower-cost alternative to central air conditioning. These units introduce significant moisture directly into the supply air, and when a property later converts to a ducted central system, the existing ductwork often retains residual mineral deposits and biological growth from years of evaporative cooling operation. If your property made that transition at any point, a duct assessment should include specific attention to those legacy conditions rather than treating the system as a clean slate.
