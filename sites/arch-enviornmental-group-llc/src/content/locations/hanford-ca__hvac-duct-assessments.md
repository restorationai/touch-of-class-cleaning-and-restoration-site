## Why Hanford Properties See Duct Contamination Problems

The San Joaquin Valley's air quality challenges are well documented, but the indoor side of that story gets less attention. Agricultural operations throughout Kings County release dust, pesticide drift, and organic particulates that infiltrate homes through attic bypasses and return-air chases. Once inside a duct system, those particles settle on liner surfaces and become a food source for microbial growth, especially during the humid stretches that follow late-summer thunderstorms or winter tule fog events.

Hanford's housing stock adds another layer of complexity. Many homes built in the 1960s through the 1980s used fiberglass duct board or flex duct installed before modern sealing standards existed. Over decades, those materials degrade: the inner liner separates, fibers become airborne, and gaps in the duct run pull unconditioned attic air directly into the supply stream. Older homes in the city's established residential corridors often have original ductwork that has never been formally assessed, meaning occupants may be breathing air that has passed through decades of accumulated debris.

## Our HVAC and Duct System Assessment Process in Hanford

An assessment begins before any panel is opened. A visual inspection of the air handler, return-air grilles, and accessible duct runs establishes baseline conditions: visible debris loading, signs of moisture intrusion, damaged insulation, and any evidence of pest activity inside the system. In Hanford's climate, attic-mounted air handlers deserve particular attention because attic temperatures can exceed 140°F in summer, accelerating the breakdown of duct adhesives and vapor barriers.

From there, the process moves to sampling. Depending on what the visual inspection reveals, that may include surface swab samples from interior duct surfaces, air samples drawn from supply registers, or bulk material samples if degraded insulation or liner material is present. All samples are analyzed by an accredited laboratory. The final report documents conditions in plain language, identifies any materials of concern, and outlines what remediation or further testing would be warranted. Because Arch Environmental Group holds certifications as a Certified Asbestos Consultant and Lead Inspector Assessor, assessments of older systems can simultaneously screen for asbestos-containing duct insulation or mastic, which is relevant in Hanford homes built before the mid-1980s.

## Equipment and Methods Used for HVAC Assessment

Accurate assessment depends on using the right collection method for each type of potential contaminant. Mold and biological growth are evaluated using spore trap cassettes or direct sampling with sterile swabs, depending on whether the concern is airborne concentration or surface colonization. Particulate loading in duct interiors can be assessed with vacuum cassette sampling, which captures a measured volume of debris for gravimetric and microscopic analysis. Where asbestos-containing materials may be present in duct wrap or mastic, bulk samples are collected per AHERA protocols and analyzed by a NIOSH 582-certified microscopist, a credential held by Arch Environmental Group's principal.

This layered approach matters in Hanford because a single duct system in an older home may present multiple concerns simultaneously: biological growth on the liner, degraded fiberglass insulation, and legacy asbestos-containing mastic at joints. Treating those as separate problems requiring separate contractors adds cost and delays resolution.

## Local Note

Homes in Hanford's older established neighborhoods frequently have original evaporative cooler penetrations that were later sealed when central air conditioning was added. During an assessment, those transition points get specific attention because they are easy to overlook and disproportionately responsible for contamination found deeper in the duct run.
