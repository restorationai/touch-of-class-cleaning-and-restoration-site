That combination of extreme heat cycling, high particulate loads, and the region's periodic tule fog in late fall and winter creates conditions inside ductwork that most homeowners never think about until an odor, an allergy flare-up, or a visible growth on a supply register forces the question. ## Why Dinuba Properties See HVAC and Duct Contamination Issues

The San Joaquin Valley's climate does something unusual to duct systems: it swings between prolonged dry heat and dense, moisture-laden fog within the same season. When a home's air conditioning runs hard through August and September, the interior of metal ductwork stays cold relative to the surrounding attic air. Once fall tule fog rolls in and attic ventilation pulls in humid outside air, that temperature differential can drive condensation onto duct surfaces, especially in older homes where duct insulation has degraded or joints have separated over time.

Dinuba's housing stock includes a significant share of homes built in the postwar decades, when duct systems were often installed with minimal insulation and sheet-metal joints sealed only with cloth-backed tape. That tape dries out and fails in the Valley's heat, leaving gaps where conditioned air escapes and unconditioned, particulate-heavy air infiltrates. Fiberglass duct liner, common in systems from the 1970s and 1980s, can also harbor biological growth once it becomes damp, and the interior surface is difficult to evaluate without a proper inspection.

Orchard and field dust is a year-round factor in this part of Tulare County. Fine particulates generated during harvest, disking, and wind events load up air filters faster than manufacturers' schedules assume, and when filters are bypassed or not changed on time, that material accumulates in the duct interior and on evaporator coil surfaces.

## Our HVAC and Duct System Assessment Process in Dinuba

An assessment begins before any equipment goes into the duct. The first step is a visual inspection of accessible components, the air handler, evaporator coil, supply and return plenums, and any flex duct connections visible in the attic or crawlspace. In Dinuba's climate, attic temperatures can exceed 150°F in summer, which accelerates the degradation of flexible duct jacketing; that's something we document as part of the structural review.

Where visual access is limited, we use video inspection equipment to examine the interior of main trunk lines and branch runs. Tape samples or swab samples are collected from areas showing discoloration, biological-looking growth, or unusual debris accumulation. Those samples are analyzed under microscopy, our team holds NIOSH 582 microscopist certification, so the report you receive identifies what's present, not just whether something looks concerning.

Air sampling at supply registers can be added to the assessment when occupant health concerns are the primary driver. Particle counts and spore trap samples provide a baseline that's useful both for understanding current conditions and for verifying clearance after any remediation work.

The written report documents findings with photographs, sample chain-of-custody records, and specific recommendations, whether that's targeted cleaning, coil treatment, duct sealing, or further investigation of a moisture source.

## Local Note

One pattern worth knowing about Dinuba specifically: many homes in the city were built with evaporative coolers as the primary cooling system and had central air conditioning added later, sometimes by different contractors over different decades. That retrofit history often means the original return-air pathway wasn't properly resized for the new system's airflow, creating negative pressure zones in certain rooms. Those pressure imbalances pull air, and whatever is in it, through wall cavities and attic bypasses rather than through the filter. If your home has gone through that kind of cooling-system transition, a duct assessment should include airflow measurement, not just a visual and sample inspection.
