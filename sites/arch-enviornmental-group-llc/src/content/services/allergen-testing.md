You've cleaned the house, replaced the air filters, and kept the windows shut, but someone in your household still wakes up congested, rubs their eyes through dinner, or reaches for an inhaler more than they should. When symptoms persist without an obvious cause, the problem is often invisible: allergen loads embedded in carpet fibers, settled into upholstered furniture, or cycling through ductwork with every HVAC cycle. Allergen testing gives you a measured answer instead of a guess, identifying exactly what is present, where it is concentrated, and at what levels.

## What allergen testing actually involves

Allergen testing is a dust-sample and surface-sample discipline. A technician collects material from the places allergens actually accumulate, settled dust on hard surfaces, reservoir dust from carpets and upholstered furniture, and sometimes HVAC return grilles, and sends those samples to an accredited laboratory for quantified analysis. The lab reports back in micrograms of allergen per gram of dust (µg/g), a unit that corresponds to thresholds established by research on sensitization and symptom onset.

Common targets include dust mite allergens (Der p 1 and Der f 1, from *Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus* and *D. farinae*), cat allergen (Fel d 1), dog allergen (Can f 1), cockroach allergen (Bla g 2), and mouse urinary protein (Mus m 1). Each has a published threshold above which sensitized individuals are likely to experience symptoms. The test tells you not just whether the allergen is present, it tells you whether it is present at a level that matters clinically.

Sampling typically takes one to two hours for a standard residential assessment. Laboratory turnaround runs several business days depending on the panel selected. The result is a written report with room-by-room allergen concentrations and a comparison to established health-based benchmarks.

## Our process

1. **Pre-sampling interview and walkthrough.** Before any samples are collected, the technician walks the property and asks about symptom patterns, which rooms, which times of day, whether symptoms improve when the occupant leaves the building. This shapes the sampling plan so the lab analysis targets the spaces most likely to explain what the household is experiencing.

2. **Targeted dust and surface collection.** Settled dust is collected from horizontal surfaces using standardized wipe or vacuum methods. Carpet and upholstered furniture samples are collected from high-contact areas using a template sampler that captures a consistent surface area, ensuring the results are comparable to published reference ranges. Samples are labeled, sealed, and logged with chain-of-custody documentation.

3. **Accredited laboratory analysis.** Samples are submitted to an accredited analytical laboratory for enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) testing, the method used in peer-reviewed allergen research and the one that produces quantified µg/g results rather than a simple positive/negative read. The panel is selected based on the walkthrough findings (pet history, pest history, building age, occupant sensitivities).

4. **Written report with benchmark comparison.** Results are delivered in a report that lists measured concentrations alongside the health-based thresholds for each allergen. Rooms or zones that exceed those thresholds are identified clearly. The report is formatted to be useful to a physician, an allergist, or a landlord, not just a technician.

5. **Findings review and remediation guidance.** A technician reviews the results with the client, explains what the numbers mean in plain language, and outlines the mitigation options appropriate to each allergen type, because the right response to a high dust mite load is different from the right response to persistent cat allergen or a cockroach indicator.

## What separates a good allergen test from a bad one

The most common failure in allergen screening is using a qualitative test, one that reports only present or absent, when a quantified result is what actually drives a decision. A positive for dust mite allergen is nearly universal in occupied homes; what matters is whether the concentration exceeds 2 µg/g (the sensitization threshold) or 10 µg/g (the threshold associated with acute asthma exacerbation). Without a number, the result is clinically meaningless.

A second common problem is sampling only the most accessible surfaces rather than the reservoir locations where allergens actually accumulate. A wipe of a countertop tells you almost nothing about dust mite burden; a vacuum sample from the carpet in the bedroom where someone sleeps eight hours a night tells you a great deal. Sampling strategy determines whether the data is useful.

Finally, chain-of-custody documentation matters when results will be used for insurance claims, legal proceedings, landlord-tenant disputes, or physician referrals. Samples without documented handling history can be challenged. Every sample collected by Arch Environmental Group is logged with collection time, location, and collector identity from the moment it leaves the building.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

The San Joaquin Valley's climate creates specific allergen conditions worth understanding. Hot, dry summers push residents to keep windows closed and run air conditioning continuously, which recirculates settled dust and can concentrate indoor allergen loads that would otherwise dissipate with ventilation. The region's agricultural activity means outdoor allergen pressure (pollens, mold spores from irrigation and harvested crops) is high for much of the year, which can make it harder to distinguish indoor allergen problems from outdoor exposure without a measured indoor sample.

Older housing stock in Kingsburg and surrounding communities, much of it built before modern vapor barriers and tight building envelopes were standard, tends to have higher settled dust accumulation and, in homes with crawl spaces, conditions that favor dust mite populations. Homes with evaporative coolers rather than refrigerated air also introduce more outdoor particulate and humidity, which affects dust mite viability and allergen load.
