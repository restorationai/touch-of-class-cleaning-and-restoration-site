When the same rooms feel stuffy no matter how long the AC runs, or when a musty smell returns within days of cleaning, the duct system is often where the investigation needs to start. Contaminated or poorly sealed ductwork can circulate particulates, mold spores, settled dust, fiberglass fragments, even rodent debris, through every room in a building without leaving an obvious trace at the register. An HVAC and duct system assessment identifies exactly what is moving through the air and why, before remediation or replacement decisions are made.

## What HVAC and duct system assessments actually involve

This is not a filter swap or a visual peek at the return. A thorough air duct inspection examines the entire air-handling pathway: the air handler cabinet, coil surfaces, drain pan, supply and return plenums, flex duct connections, and the register boots where ductwork meets finished ceilings or floors. Each of those zones can harbor different contaminants, biological growth favors the coil and drain pan where condensation collects, while settled particulates accumulate in horizontal trunk lines and low-velocity branch runs.

Duct contamination testing typically involves swab or tape-lift sampling from inside duct surfaces, spore-trap air samples taken at the register and at the air handler, and in some cases bulk material samples when visible growth or debris is present. Samples are analyzed by an accredited laboratory, and results are interpreted against EPA and NADCA guidance thresholds. The deliverable is a written assessment report, not a verbal recommendation, that documents findings, photographs, sample chain of custody, and a scope of work if remediation is warranted.

Timeline for a standard single-system residential assessment runs two to four hours on-site, with laboratory turnaround typically adding two to five business days before a final report is issued.

## Our process

1. **Pre-inspection intake and system review.** Before touching the equipment, we note the system age, filter type and change history, any prior water intrusion near the air handler, and the complaint pattern (which rooms, which seasons, what symptoms). This shapes where sampling effort is concentrated.

2. **Visual inspection of the air-handling unit and accessible ductwork.** We inspect the coil, drain pan, blower compartment, and duct interiors using inspection mirrors and, where geometry allows, a borescope camera. We document visible microbial growth, debris accumulation, liner deterioration, and unsealed joints that allow unconditioned air, or attic or crawlspace air, to enter the system.

3. **Environmental sampling.** Spore-trap air samples are collected inside the air handler, at representative supply registers, and at an outdoor background location for comparison. Surface samples (tape lifts or swabs) are taken from areas of visible concern. All samples are logged with location, time, and conditions, then submitted to an accredited laboratory under chain of custody.

4. **Laboratory analysis and data interpretation.** We review results against the outdoor background sample and against EPA and NADCA guidance. An elevated indoor-to-outdoor ratio, or the presence of water-indicator species like *Stachybotrys* or *Chaetomium*, changes the remediation scope significantly compared to a finding of ordinary settled dust.

5. **Written assessment report and scope of work.** The final report includes all sample data, photographs, laboratory certificates of analysis, and, where contamination is confirmed, a written remediation scope that a contractor can bid from or that an insurance adjuster can review. We do not sell the remediation, which keeps the assessment independent.

## What separates a good HVAC air quality assessment from a bad one

The most common failure in duct contamination testing is sampling only at the register face, the point farthest from where biological growth actually begins. Mold on a register grille is often surface dust; mold on the drain pan liner or inside the air handler cabinet is a systemic problem. An assessor who does not open the air handler and inspect the coil and pan is missing the most likely contamination site in a system that has ever had a condensate backup or a refrigerant leak.

A second common shortcut is skipping the outdoor background sample. Without it, elevated spore counts indoors have no reference point, a high raw count in a building surrounded by agricultural fields or during a windy Central Valley day may be entirely outdoor-driven. Background sampling is what makes the data defensible.

Insurance adjusters reviewing HVAC mold inspection claims look for chain-of-custody documentation, an accredited laboratory report (not an in-house read), and a clear causal link between a covered loss event (typically a water intrusion) and the contamination found. An assessment that provides all three moves through the claims process faster and with fewer disputes.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

In the San Joaquin Valley, HVAC systems run hard. Cooling season stretches from late spring through early fall, which means coils and drain pans are wet for months at a time, the exact condition that allows mold to establish on duct liner surfaces. When systems are finally switched to heat in November, that growth dries and becomes friable, releasing spores into the airstream just as windows close and ventilation drops.

Kingsburg and the surrounding communities also sit in one of the highest agricultural particulate regions in the state. Field dust, pollen, and harvest debris load filters quickly, and when filters are bypassed, even briefly, that material settles into duct interiors. Homes built in the 1970s and 1980s in this area commonly used fiberglass duct liner that degrades over time, releasing fibers that can complicate air sample interpretation without microscopist-level analysis.
