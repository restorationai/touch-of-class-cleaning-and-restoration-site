Smoke moves faster than fire. By the time flames are out, soot has already traveled through your HVAC system, settled into wall cavities, and begun etching glass, metal, and finished surfaces. The visible char is only part of the problem. The acidic residue left by combustion keeps damaging materials for days after the fire department leaves, which is why the clock on fire damage restoration starts the moment the fire is out, not when you feel ready to call.

## What Fire Damage Restoration actually involves

Fire damage is a compound problem: you are dealing with char and structural damage from the fire itself, soot and smoke residue that has migrated far beyond the burn area, water and chemical retardant left by suppression efforts, and odor molecules that have penetrated porous materials like drywall, insulation, wood framing, and upholstery.

Soot is not uniform. A kitchen grease fire produces a wet, oily protein soot that smears if wiped incorrectly and requires different chemistry than the dry, powdery soot from burning wood or the thick black smoke residue from synthetic materials like carpet, foam, and plastics. Misidentifying the soot type is one of the most common errors in fire cleanup, and it leads to surfaces that look clean but continue off-gassing odor.

Restoration equipment for a fire loss includes HEPA air scrubbers, thermal foggers, ozone generators, hydroxyl generators, and industrial-grade chemical sponges and neutralizing agents. Structural drying equipment is also staged when suppression water is present. The process is methodical and sequenced, because running ozone before soot removal, for example, bakes residue into surfaces rather than clearing it.

Timeline varies with the size of the loss. A single-room fire with limited smoke spread may take several days to a week. A whole-floor or whole-structure loss with heavy synthetic soot and suppression water can run two to four weeks before the structure is ready for any reconstruction work.

## Our process

1. **Emergency stabilization and safety assessment.** The structure is evaluated for load-bearing integrity, electrical hazards, and air quality before any crew enters for detailed work. Utilities are coordinated with the appropriate providers. Openings from fire damage are boarded and tarped to prevent weather intrusion and unauthorized entry.

2. **Soot characterization and scope documentation.** Every affected surface is catalogued by soot type, material, and degree of penetration. This documentation drives the cleaning protocol and forms the foundation of the insurance claim. Protein soot, dry soot, and synthetic soot each require different chemical agents and application methods. Skipping this step and using a single all-purpose cleaner across the structure is a shortcut that produces incomplete results.

3. **Contents pack-out and inventory.** Salvageable contents are photographed, inventoried, and removed from the structure before cleaning begins. This protects belongings from cross-contamination during the restoration process and allows the structure to be worked without obstruction. Non-salvageable items are documented for the insurance claim.

4. **Soot removal, surface cleaning, and odor neutralization.** Dry soot is removed with chemical sponges before any wet cleaning is applied. Wet or oily residues are treated with the appropriate alkaline or solvent-based cleaners. HEPA air scrubbers run continuously. Once surfaces are clean, thermal fogging or hydroxyl treatment penetrates the same porous pathways smoke used, neutralizing odor molecules rather than masking them.

5. **Post-cleaning verification and clearance.** Surfaces are inspected and, where appropriate, tested before the structure is released for reconstruction. Air quality readings confirm that particulate levels have returned to acceptable ranges. A written clearance report is provided for the insurance file.

## What separates a good fire response from a bad one

The most common failure in fire and smoke restoration is treating the visible burn area and ignoring smoke migration. Smoke follows air pressure differentials, travels through wall penetrations, and deposits in rooms that show no visible damage. A thorough response includes inspecting the HVAC system, attic space, and adjacent rooms, not just the room of origin.

A second common gap is odor. Thermal fogging and ozone treatment are not interchangeable, and neither is a substitute for complete soot removal. Odor that returns weeks after a fire almost always traces back to residue that was painted over or sealed in rather than cleaned out. Insurance adjusters increasingly require documentation that cleaning was performed to a defined standard before they approve reconstruction costs, so cutting corners on soot removal creates problems at the claim level as well.

Contents documentation is another area where less-experienced operators fall short. A detailed, photographed inventory of affected belongings is what drives contents coverage on a homeowners policy. Without it, homeowners absorb losses that should have been covered.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Pittsburgh's cold winters create a specific complication after a fire loss: suppression water freezes in wall cavities and under flooring before it can be extracted, extending drying time and increasing the risk of secondary structural damage. Fires that occur between November and March typically require faster mobilization to prevent freeze-related complications on top of the fire damage itself. Older Pittsburgh housing stock, particularly the brick rowhouses and balloon-frame construction common in neighborhoods like Lawrenceville, Polish Hill, and the South Side, presents additional challenges because smoke travels more freely through balloon-frame wall cavities than through platform-frame construction, and brick facades can trap soot in mortar joints.
