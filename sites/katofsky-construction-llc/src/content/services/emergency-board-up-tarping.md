A broken window at 2 a.m., a roof torn open by a storm, a door frame blown out by a fire department's forced entry, every hour that opening stays exposed, the damage compounds. Rain soaks into subfloor and drywall. Looters read an unboarded window as an invitation. Insurers look at the claim timeline and ask whether the property was secured promptly. Emergency board-up and tarping is the work that stops the clock on secondary damage, and it has to happen before anything else.

## What emergency board-up and tarping actually involves

Board-up and tarping is structural security and weather protection, installed fast. On the board-up side, that means cutting plywood to fit window and door openings, anchoring it to the framing (not just the sill), and sealing gaps that would let wind-driven rain through. On the roof side, it means laying heavy-duty poly tarps over damaged or missing sections, weighting or fastening the edges so the tarp stays put through wind, and routing runoff away from any open penetrations.

The work requires more than a tarp from a hardware store. Crews assess the structural condition of the opening before climbing onto a compromised roof. They identify which sections of sheathing are intact enough to anchor to, and they use mechanical fasteners rather than relying on weight alone. For fire-damaged structures, they also assess whether the wall framing near an opening can bear the load of a plywood panel before attaching it.

A properly executed board-up is weather-tight and tamper-resistant. It also photographs well, which matters when an adjuster reviews the claim.

## Our process

1. **Damage assessment on arrival.** Before any material goes up, the crew walks the perimeter and identifies every opening, every compromised roof section, and any structural hazards that affect how the work gets done safely. A fire-damaged wall stud that looks solid can be charred through at the core.

2. **Roof tarping first when weather is the active threat.** If rain is falling or forecast, the roof gets covered before windows are boarded. Tarp edges are secured with battens or weighted with sandbags, and the crew checks that water drains away from the opening rather than pooling under the tarp.

3. **Plywood cutting and fitting.** Panels are cut to the rough opening, not just leaned against the frame. Fasteners go into structural framing members. On fire-damaged openings, the crew avoids attaching to framing that shows deep char without first checking load capacity.

4. **Perimeter seal and secondary openings.** Gaps around HVAC penetrations, attic vents, and crawl space accesses that were exposed by the loss event get addressed. A boarded front window does not help if a soffit vent is open to the interior.

5. **Documentation for your claim.** The crew photographs every opening before and after work, notes the materials used, and provides a written scope. That documentation goes directly to your insurance carrier.

## What separates a good board-up response from a bad one

The most common failure is speed without thoroughness. A crew that nails a single sheet of plywood over the most visible opening and leaves has not secured the property. Adjusters routinely find secondary water intrusion through gaps that were never addressed, and that additional damage can complicate coverage if the carrier argues it was preventable.

A second common problem is improper tarp installation on roofs. A tarp that is not fastened to solid decking, or one that routes water toward an open penetration rather than away from it, can cause more water intrusion than no tarp at all. On steep-pitch roofs or fire-weakened decking, this is also a safety issue for the crew.

Insurance adjusters look at three things in the documentation: whether the property was secured promptly after the loss event, whether all openings were addressed, and whether the materials used were appropriate for the conditions. Photographs taken before and after, with timestamps, answer all three.
