If your home feels dustier than it should, or you notice a stale, musty odor every time the furnace kicks on, the problem is often not the air itself but what has accumulated inside the ductwork carrying it. Over years of normal use, Pittsburgh homes collect layers of dust, pet dander, pollen, mold spores, and construction debris inside their HVAC systems. That buildup circulates through every room every time the system runs, and no amount of filter changes clears what is already coating the duct walls.

## What air duct cleaning actually involves

Air duct cleaning is a mechanical process, not a chemical spray or a quick vacuum pass. A trained technician connects a high-powered negative-pressure vacuum unit to the main trunk line, creating suction throughout the entire duct system. Rotating agitation brushes and compressed-air whips then dislodge debris from supply and return runs, register boots, and the air handler cabinet itself. Everything loosened is captured by the vacuum before it can re-enter the living space.

For homes where mold growth or heavy biological contamination is confirmed, an EPA-registered sanitizing agent is applied to interior duct surfaces after the mechanical cleaning is complete. Dryer vent cleaning follows a similar process but uses specialized flexible rods to clear lint accumulation from the full length of the exhaust run, including bends and transitions, where blockages most commonly form.

A thorough residential cleaning of a typical Pittsburgh home typically runs two to four hours depending on system size, number of supply registers, and how long the ducts have gone without service.

## Our process

1. **System inspection and pre-cleaning documentation.** A technician walks the home, locates every supply and return register, inspects accessible duct sections with a camera or mirror, and photographs conditions before work begins. This step identifies any collapsed flex duct, disconnected joints, or active mold growth that changes the scope of work.

2. **Negative-pressure containment setup.** The truck-mounted or portable vacuum unit is connected to the main trunk line and all registers are sealed. The system is brought to negative pressure so that loosened debris travels toward the vacuum rather than into the room.

3. **Agitation and debris removal.** Rotary brushes and compressed-air tools work through each branch run, supply boot, and return plenum. The technician moves register by register, opening each one in sequence as the vacuum maintains suction throughout the system.

4. **Air handler and coil inspection.** The air handler cabinet, blower wheel, and evaporator coil are inspected and cleaned. These components are often skipped by less thorough operators, but a dirty blower wheel re-contaminates clean ducts within one heating season.

5. **Sanitization and final verification.** Where biological contamination is present, an EPA-registered sanitizer is applied and allowed to dwell. Registers are reinstalled, the system is cycled, and airflow from each register is verified before the technician leaves.

## What separates a good duct cleaning from a bad one

The single most common shortcut is skipping the air handler. A technician who cleans branch ducts but leaves a debris-coated blower wheel has done half a job. Within weeks, that wheel redistributes what was left behind.

A second failure point is inadequate vacuum capacity. Portable shop-vac style equipment cannot generate enough suction to capture debris from long duct runs. Truck-mounted or high-CFM portable units are the standard for whole-system cleaning.

Dryer vents are frequently overlooked entirely. Lint accumulation beyond the first few feet of duct is invisible from the exterior vent cap but is a documented fire risk. A complete cleaning includes the full run from the dryer connection to the exterior termination.

Finally, mold inside ductwork is not resolved by cleaning alone. If the underlying moisture source is not corrected, mold returns. A responsible operator identifies the cause and, where remediation is needed, follows a documented protocol rather than simply applying a deodorizing spray.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Pittsburgh's climate creates a specific set of conditions that accelerate duct contamination. Humid summers push moisture into return air, and older homes throughout the South Hills, North Side, and river valleys often have ductwork that runs through unconditioned crawl spaces or unfinished basements where humidity is higher still. That moisture, combined with dust and organic debris, is exactly what mold needs to colonize duct surfaces.

Renovation work is another common trigger. Allegheny County's housing stock is heavily weighted toward pre-1960 construction, and any plaster or drywall disturbance during a remodel sends fine particulate into open duct systems. Cleaning after any significant renovation is worth scheduling before the system runs again.

Fall is the most common time homeowners notice duct problems, because the first furnace cycles of the season push settled dust and odors through the home all at once.
