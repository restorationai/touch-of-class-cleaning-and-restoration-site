## Why Mount Lebanon Properties Present Specific Biohazard Challenges

Mount Lebanon's residential character is one of its defining qualities, and so is its housing stock. Much of the borough was developed between the 1920s and the 1960s, meaning a significant share of homes have original hardwood subfloors, plaster walls, and basement spaces with older concrete that is more porous than modern poured foundations. Porous materials absorb biological contaminants in ways that surface cleaning cannot address. A situation that appears contained to one room may have wicked into subfloor gaps, wall cavities, or grout lines that aren't visible from the surface.

The borough's hilly terrain also shapes how properties drain and how crawl spaces and basements behave after any kind of liquid event. Homes on steeper lots can experience pooling in lower-level rooms, which matters when biohazardous material needs to be fully traced and removed rather than assumed to be confined to a single area.

## Our Biohazard Cleanup Process in Mount Lebanon

Every job begins with a thorough assessment before any removal starts. The affected area is isolated to prevent cross-contamination of adjacent spaces, and the crew works in appropriate personal protective equipment throughout. Contaminated materials, including porous items that cannot be decontaminated to a safe standard, are packaged and disposed of in compliance with Pennsylvania Department of Environmental Protection requirements for regulated medical and biological waste.

After physical removal, the space is treated with hospital-grade disinfectants rated for the specific biological hazard present. Surfaces are tested rather than assumed clean. The goal is a space that meets measurable decontamination standards, not just one that looks clean. Documentation of the process is provided, which matters both for insurance purposes and for the peace of mind of anyone returning to the property.

Because many Mount Lebanon homes have older HVAC systems with ductwork that runs through affected areas, the team evaluates whether air handling systems require attention as part of the scope. Overlooking ductwork in a 1940s colonial is one of the more common ways a biohazard situation gets incompletely resolved.
