## Why Jeannette Properties See Roofing Problems Early

The homes throughout Jeannette were largely built between the 1910s and the 1950s, which means many roofs sit on original or once-replaced decking that has absorbed decades of moisture. Pennsylvania's climate delivers roughly 40 inches of precipitation annually, and the Laurel Highlands terrain to the east funnels cold air into the Westmoreland County valleys in ways that accelerate ice dam formation along eaves. When ice dams form, meltwater backs up under shingles and into the decking before it ever reaches a gutter. By the time a stain appears on a ceiling, the sheathing underneath has often been wet for weeks.

The steeper roof pitches common on the Victorian and Craftsman-era homes throughout Jeannette can actually shed snow well, but they concentrate runoff at the valleys and around dormers, which are the first places to fail on an aging system. Flat or low-slope additions added to those same homes in later decades create a second vulnerability, since they require entirely different waterproofing strategies than the main pitched roof above them.

## Our Roofing Installation and Replacement Process in Jeannette

Every project begins with a physical inspection, not a drive-by estimate. We document the condition of the existing shingles, the decking beneath them, the flashing at chimneys and walls, and the ventilation system in the attic. Inadequate attic ventilation is one of the most common reasons roofs in this region fail ahead of schedule: heat and moisture trapped in the attic degrade shingles from the underside while the weather works on them from above.

Once we have a clear picture of the scope, we walk you through material options suited to Jeannette's climate. Architectural asphalt shingles rated for high wind and impact are the standard choice for most residential roofing here, but we also work with metal roofing systems that handle the freeze-thaw cycle particularly well on steeper pitches. Decking that shows rot or delamination is replaced before new materials go down, not covered over. Flashing is replaced as a matter of course, not reused unless it is genuinely sound.

## Local Note

Jeannette's older homes frequently have original brick chimneys that have never had a proper cricket installed behind them. A cricket is a small peaked structure that diverts water away from the uphill side of a chimney, and without one, water pools against the masonry and works its way under the flashing over time. On a re-roof, we assess every chimney for this condition. It is a small addition to the scope that prevents a disproportionate amount of future water intrusion, particularly on the steeper roofs common in Jeannette's older residential blocks.
