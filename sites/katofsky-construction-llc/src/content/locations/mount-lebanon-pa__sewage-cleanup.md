## Why Mount Lebanon Properties See Sewage Backup Issues

Mount Lebanon sits in the South Hills of Allegheny County, where the terrain is hilly and the municipal sewer infrastructure in many blocks is decades old. Clay tile lateral lines, common in homes built before the 1970s, are prone to root intrusion from the mature trees that line the borough's streets. When roots crack or collapse a lateral, sewage has nowhere to go but back into the lowest drain in the house, which is almost always a basement floor drain or laundry tub.

Heavy rain events compound the problem. The South Hills topography channels stormwater quickly, and during intense storms the combined sewer system can surcharge, pushing wastewater backward through laterals that are already partially obstructed. Basements that have never had a backup can flood in a single storm if the lateral has been quietly deteriorating. Homes with older cast-iron or clay drain stacks inside the walls face a related risk: corroded joints can fail at the stack cleanout, releasing sewage behind finished drywall before a homeowner notices any odor.

## Our Sewage Cleanup and Sanitization Process in Mount Lebanon

The first step on any sewage job is containment. Technicians establish a work perimeter, put on appropriate PPE, and assess how far contaminated water has traveled. In a typical Mount Lebanon basement, that means checking the floor drain, the base of the water heater, and the bottom course of any drywall or wood paneling, because sewage wicks upward through porous materials faster than it spreads laterally across concrete.

Once the scope is mapped, extraction begins using truck-mounted or portable wet-vacuum equipment rated for Category 3 water, which is the classification for raw sewage under the IICRC S500 standard. After bulk removal, affected materials are assessed for salvageability. Concrete block walls and poured concrete floors can typically be cleaned and treated in place. Drywall, carpet, and wood framing that have been in direct contact with sewage are generally removed rather than dried, because the pathogen load makes restoration impractical and the material cost of replacement is lower than the risk of incomplete sanitization.

After removal, every surface in the affected area is treated with an EPA-registered disinfectant and allowed appropriate dwell time before a second wipe-down. Hydroxyl or air-scrubbing equipment runs throughout to address airborne contaminants and odor. The area is then dried to IICRC-standard moisture levels before any reconstruction begins.
