## Why New Kensington Properties See Sewage Backup Problems

The housing stock in New Kensington skews heavily toward pre-1950 construction. Homes built in that era typically have clay tile or cast iron drain lines that have spent decades under pressure from tree root intrusion, ground settlement, and the freeze-thaw cycles that hit the Allegheny Valley hard every winter. Clay tile joints separate over time, and when they do, a heavy rain event or a single blockage downstream can push sewage backward through every low-lying fixture in the house.

The city's combined sewer system compounds the problem. During significant rainfall, stormwater and sanitary sewage share the same pipes. When those pipes reach capacity, the overflow has to go somewhere, and for many older homes in New Kensington, that somewhere is the basement floor drain. Homeowners sometimes mistake the first backup for an isolated clog, address the symptom, and discover weeks later that contaminated water wicked into concrete block walls and wood sill plates while they waited.

Septic systems on the outskirts of the city face a different version of the same issue. The region's clay-heavy soil drains slowly, and a saturated drain field after a wet spring can push effluent back toward the tank and, eventually, toward the home.

## Our Sewage Cleanup and Sanitization Process in New Kensington

The first priority on any sewage call is containment. Before anything is moved or cleaned, the affected area is assessed for the extent of contamination, and cross-contamination pathways to unaffected parts of the home are blocked. Category 3 water, which is what raw sewage is classified as under the IICRC standard, requires a different removal protocol than a clean water pipe break.

Solid waste and saturated materials are removed and bagged according to applicable disposal requirements. Porous materials that cannot be adequately sanitized, including certain flooring underlayments, drywall, and insulation, are documented and removed. What remains is treated with EPA-registered disinfectants formulated for microbial contamination, not general-purpose cleaners. Surfaces are then dried using commercial-grade dehumidification and airflow equipment, and moisture readings are logged until the structure returns to baseline.

In New Kensington's older homes, this process often involves working around rubble-stone or concrete block foundations that hold moisture differently than poured concrete. Block walls wick contaminated water laterally through mortar joints, so sanitization has to address the wall face, the mortar lines, and the cavity behind them where accessible.
