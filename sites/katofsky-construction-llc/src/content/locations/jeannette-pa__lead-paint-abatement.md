## Why Jeannette Properties See Lead Paint Issues

Jeannette sits in a part of southwestern Pennsylvania where the housing stock skews old. The city's industrial peak ran from roughly 1890 through the mid-20th century, and the homes built to house glass workers and their families during that era were constructed with the materials of the time: oil-based paints loaded with lead pigment for durability and color stability. Those layers did not disappear when lead paint was federally restricted in 1978. They are still under the latex coats applied in the 1980s and 1990s, and they are still under the primer someone rolled on last spring.

The regional climate adds pressure. Westmoreland County winters bring freeze-thaw cycles that expand and contract wood trim, window sashes, and porch columns repeatedly each season. That movement fractures paint films. Chalking, flaking, and peeling on exterior surfaces are common findings on pre-1950 homes here, and those conditions are exactly what EPA guidance identifies as deteriorated lead paint requiring action. Interior surfaces are not immune either: older steam-heat systems common in Jeannette's rowhouses create humidity swings that cause paint to bubble and separate from plaster substrates.

## Our Lead Paint Abatement Process in Jeannette

Every project starts with a clear picture of what is actually present. Lead paint testing uses XRF analysis or paint chip sampling to identify which surfaces contain lead above actionable thresholds. That data drives the scope: not every surface with lead paint requires full removal. Encapsulation is appropriate in some situations; enclosure or component replacement makes more sense in others. Where paint is deteriorated, accessible, or in a friction or impact zone, abatement by removal is the correct call.

Once the scope is set, the work follows EPA Renovation, Repair and Painting (RRP) protocols. That means establishing containment barriers, using HEPA-filtered equipment for sanding and scraping, wet methods to suppress dust, and careful waste handling throughout. At the end of the project, a clearance examination by a qualified inspector confirms that lead dust levels meet federal standards before occupants return. The process is not fast relative to a standard paint job, but the documentation it produces matters: for insurance purposes, for future sale disclosure, and for the health of anyone living or working in the building.

## Local Note

One pattern that comes up repeatedly in Jeannette's older housing is the presence of lead paint on interior window components, specifically the sash channels and sill aprons. These are friction surfaces: every time a double-hung window is opened or closed, the painted surfaces grind against each other and generate fine dust. In a home that has been occupied for decades, that dust has been settling into the surrounding floor and trim for years. When abatement work reaches those windows, the surrounding floor area often requires more thorough HEPA vacuuming and wet-wiping than the window itself. It is a detail that matters for clearance testing, and it is the kind of thing that shows up in Jeannette's vintage housing stock more consistently than in newer construction.
