Mount Lebanon's heating seasons run long and cold, and the older housing stock throughout this Pittsburgh suburb means many homes are still running ductwork that was installed decades ago, in some cases before modern sealing and insulation standards existed. Over years of use, those ducts accumulate dust, pet dander, pollen, and in some cases mold spores, all of which recirculate through living spaces every time the furnace or central air kicks on. If your home has started to smell musty when the heat comes up, or if certain rooms never seem to reach the right temperature, the duct system is often where the problem starts.

## Why Mount Lebanon Homes See Duct Buildup Faster Than You Might Expect

Mount Lebanon is a dense, established community with a housing stock that skews heavily toward mid-century construction. Homes built in the 1940s through the 1960s were frequently fitted with gravity-fed or early forced-air systems, and when those systems were later converted or upgraded, the existing ductwork was often left in place. Older rectangular sheet-metal runs with unsealed joints collect debris at every seam. Flex duct added during later renovations can sag and trap particulates in the low spots.

The region's climate compounds the issue. Pittsburgh-area winters are humid as well as cold, and that moisture finds its way into duct systems through return-air leaks and improperly sealed boots at floor registers. A duct interior that cycles between warm and cool air in a humid environment is exactly the kind of surface where mold and mildew can begin to colonize, particularly if there has ever been a slow leak nearby or a basement moisture problem that went unaddressed for a season.

Dryer vent lines in Mount Lebanon's attached and semi-detached homes are another common concern. When a dryer vent runs a long horizontal path through a finished basement before exiting the exterior wall, lint accumulates at every elbow. That buildup restricts airflow, extends drying times, and in the worst cases creates a fire hazard.

## Our Air Duct Cleaning Process in Mount Lebanon

The work starts with a visual inspection of every accessible register, return, and main trunk line. Before any equipment is connected, the technician checks for signs of moisture damage, pest intrusion, or disconnected sections, because cleaning a duct system that has an active moisture problem without addressing the source first only moves the contamination around.

Once the system is mapped, a high-powered negative-pressure vacuum is connected to the main trunk. Working from the farthest registers back toward the air handler, technicians use rotary brush agitation to break debris loose from duct walls while the vacuum draws it out rather than into the living space. Supply and return sides are cleaned separately so cross-contamination does not occur between the two.

For systems where inspection reveals microbial growth or persistent odor, an EPA-registered sanitizing agent can be applied to interior duct surfaces after mechanical cleaning is complete. This step is documented and explained before it is performed, not added quietly to an invoice afterward.

Dryer vent cleaning follows a similar logic: the lint trap housing is cleared, the full run is brushed from the interior, and airflow is verified at the exterior termination before the job is closed out.

## Local Note: What Older Register Placement Tells You

In many of Mount Lebanon's two-story homes built before 1960, floor registers were positioned along exterior walls beneath windows, which was the standard practice for gravity and early forced-air systems. When those homes were retrofitted with modern high-efficiency furnaces, the duct layout often stayed the same even though the airflow dynamics changed. The result is that debris tends to concentrate at those low exterior registers rather than distributing evenly through the system. A technician who knows this pattern will spend extra time at those locations rather than treating every register identically.
