Dust the color of chalk collecting on a windowsill within days of cleaning, a musty pull of air when the furnace kicks on, a dryer that takes two cycles to finish a load: these are the signals that send Forest Hills homeowners searching for air duct cleaning rather than just living with it. Forest Hills sits in the older ring of Allegheny County's eastern suburbs, and that housing stock shapes what we find inside ductwork here more than almost anything else.

## Why Forest Hills Properties See Duct Issues

A large share of homes in this part of the county were built from the 1920s through the 1950s, which means a lot of original duct runs have been patched, re-routed, or tied into retrofitted forced-air systems that were never designed for them. Sheet metal trunks from that era often have seams that have loosened over decades of expansion and contraction through Pittsburgh's humid summers and damp, freeze-thaw winters. That seam gap is exactly where attic or crawlspace dust, insulation fiber, and in older homes occasionally a trace of old fiberglass, gets pulled into the airstream every time the blower runs. Basements with stone or parged foundations, common in this area's older construction, also tend to run damp for part of the year, and duct runs that pass through them can pick up a faint mildew smell that circulates through the house well before anyone sees visible mold.

## Our Air Duct Cleaning Process in Forest Hills

We start with a camera inspection of the supply and return runs so the homeowner can see what we're seeing before any equipment goes to work, not just take our word for it. From there we seal each register, connect a negative-air vacuum collection system to the trunk line, and agitate the interior of the ducts with a rotary brush or air whip depending on the duct material, sheet metal versus the flexible duct common in additions and finished basements. For homes with older duct systems that have been patched over the years, we spend extra time checking those joints for leaks that waste conditioned air and pull in unfiltered attic or crawlspace air between cleanings. If the home has a dryer vent run longer than the typical straight shot, which is common in houses with laundry moved to a basement or interior room, we treat that as a separate job since lint buildup in a long or elbow-heavy run is a real fire consideration, not just an efficiency one.

## Equipment & Methods We Use

Source-removal duct cleaning, the method recognized by national duct cleaning standards, relies on negative pressure and mechanical agitation rather than spraying anything down the ductwork. We use HEPA-filtered vacuum collection so what gets knocked loose actually leaves the house instead of settling back into living space, and we photograph before-and-after conditions at the main trunk and a sample of registers so homeowners have a record of what changed. For air duct sanitization requests, typically after a rodent intrusion, water intrusion, or a previous owner's smoking, we use EPA-registered antimicrobial products labeled for HVAC use, applied only after the mechanical cleaning is complete.

## Local note

One thing we watch for specifically in this area: homes with additions built onto an original 1930s or 1940s structure often have two different duct systems feeding into one return, and the transition point between them is a common spot for disconnected or crushed flex duct hiding above a dropped ceiling. It's an easy thing to miss on a surface inspection and a common reason a newer addition never seems to heat or cool evenly.
