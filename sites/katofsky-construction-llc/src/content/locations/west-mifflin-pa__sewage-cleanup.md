## Why West Mifflin Properties See Sewage Backup Problems

Allegheny County's combined sewer infrastructure in communities like West Mifflin was engineered for a different era. During heavy rain events, storm runoff and sanitary sewage share the same pipes, and when that system surpasses capacity, the pressure has to go somewhere. For homes with floor drains, laundry tubs, or low-lying basement bathrooms, that somewhere is often indoors.

The housing stock throughout West Mifflin leans heavily toward mid-century construction, with many homes built between the 1940s and 1970s. Cast-iron drain lines from that period corrode from the inside out, narrowing over decades until root intrusion or grease buildup causes a full blockage. Concrete block basement walls, common in this part of Allegheny County, are porous enough that sewage water can wick laterally through the masonry even after surface water is removed, which is why thorough sanitization matters as much as extraction.

Sloped terrain throughout the area also plays a role. Properties at lower elevations along drainage corridors are more vulnerable to backflow during storm events, particularly when municipal backflow preventers are absent or aging.

## Our Sewage Cleanup and Sanitization Process in West Mifflin

Raw sewage is classified as Category 3 water, the most hazardous type in the IICRC S500 framework. That classification drives every decision from the moment the crew arrives.

The process begins with containment. Affected areas are isolated to prevent cross-contamination to unaffected parts of the home. Porous materials that have absorbed sewage, including carpet, padding, drywall, and in some cases wood subfloor, are removed rather than dried in place. Attempting to dry contaminated porous materials is not a remediation strategy; it is a way to trap pathogens inside a structure.

Once contaminated materials are bagged and removed, standing water and residual waste are extracted using truck-mounted and portable extraction equipment. Surfaces are then cleaned, disinfected with EPA-registered antimicrobials, and treated for odor. Because West Mifflin's older homes often have unfinished basement walls and exposed concrete, the sanitization step includes those masonry surfaces, not just the finished floor area.

Drying equipment, including industrial air movers and dehumidifiers, runs until moisture readings in walls and subfloor materials return to baseline. Technicians monitor readings daily and document them, which matters when an insurance claim is involved.

## Local Note

West Mifflin's older concrete block basements present a specific challenge during sewage cleanup that is easy to underestimate. Unlike poured concrete, block construction has mortar joints that act as wicking channels. Sewage water that appears contained to a small floor area may have already traveled several feet laterally through the block cores. Technicians working in this housing stock probe the block wall cavities with moisture meters before declaring the perimeter clean, because a wall that reads dry at the face can still be saturated inside. Skipping that step leads to odor and microbial growth returning weeks after the visible mess is gone.
