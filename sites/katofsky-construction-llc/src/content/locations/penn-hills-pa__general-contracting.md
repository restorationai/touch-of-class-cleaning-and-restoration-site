## Why Penn Hills Homes Present Specific Renovation Challenges

The residential fabric of Penn Hills is overwhelmingly mid-century. Ranch homes, split-levels, and brick colonials built between the late 1940s and the early 1970s dominate the borough's hillside streets. That era of construction means a few things show up consistently when walls come open or floors come up.

First, knob-and-tube or early aluminum wiring is still present in a meaningful share of older homes. Any renovation that touches walls or ceilings in these properties needs an electrical assessment before the project scope is finalized. Second, the steep topography that defines so much of Penn Hills creates drainage patterns that push water toward foundations and crawl spaces. A bathroom remodel or basement renovation in this terrain often reveals moisture intrusion that needs to be addressed before finish work begins. Third, original plaster walls, common in homes built before 1960, behave differently than modern drywall during demo and reconstruction. They add weight to the project timeline and require more careful dust containment, particularly in occupied homes.

None of these are deal-breakers. They are simply the conditions that shape how renovation work gets planned and sequenced here.

Every project starts with a thorough walkthrough. Before a single measurement is drawn up, we want to understand what the house is actually made of, not just what the listing said it was made of. ## Local Note

One pattern that comes up repeatedly in Penn Hills renovations: the original bathroom tile in homes built between 1950 and 1965 was frequently set over a thick mortar bed, not a backer board. When a homeowner wants to retile a shower or tub surround, that mortar bed adds significant weight and demo time compared to a modern installation. More importantly, it sometimes conceals water damage that has been sitting behind the tile for years without ever showing on the surface. A bathroom remodel in a Penn Hills split-level is almost always more involved than the same job in a newer home, and budgeting for that discovery phase is the difference between a project that finishes on schedule and one that stalls mid-demo.
