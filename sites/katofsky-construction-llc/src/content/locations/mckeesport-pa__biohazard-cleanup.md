## Why McKeesport's Building Stock Affects Biohazard Cleanup

A significant share of McKeesport's residential properties date to the steel-boom decades of the early and mid-twentieth century. That era of construction means porous plaster walls, original hardwood subfloors laid over board sheathing, and basement floors that are often bare concrete or deteriorating tile. Porous and aged materials absorb biological contamination differently than modern vinyl or cement board, and surface-level cleaning is rarely sufficient. Remediation in these buildings requires careful assessment of what lies beneath the visible surface before any work is signed off as complete.

Many of the city's rowhouses and duplex conversions also share wall cavities and HVAC chases between units. That matters for biohazard work because odor and contamination can migrate into adjacent spaces. Properly scoping the affected area from the start prevents a situation where a neighboring unit shows problems weeks after the primary cleanup is finished.

## Our Biohazard Cleanup Process in McKeesport

Every job begins with a contained assessment. Before any materials are disturbed, the affected area is evaluated for the full scope of contamination, including surfaces that may not be visibly affected. Personal protective equipment, containment barriers, and negative air pressure are established to prevent cross-contamination to unaffected parts of the property.

Biological materials are removed and packaged according to Pennsylvania Department of Environmental Protection guidelines for regulated medical and biohazardous waste. Transport and disposal follow a documented chain of custody. Once materials are removed, affected surfaces are treated with EPA-registered disinfectants appropriate to the pathogen risk level. A final verification step confirms that treated areas meet accepted clearance standards before containment is removed.

For properties with finished basements or older plaster construction common throughout McKeesport, we assess whether structural materials need to be removed entirely or whether in-place treatment is appropriate. That decision is made based on the material, the depth of penetration, and the condition of the substrate, not on what is fastest.
