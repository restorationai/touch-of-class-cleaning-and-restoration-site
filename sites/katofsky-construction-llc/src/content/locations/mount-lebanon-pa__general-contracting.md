Mount Lebanon's residential streets are lined with brick colonials, stone-faced Tudors, and craftsman bungalows that were built to last, but lasting a century also means kitchens with galley layouts designed before open-concept was a phrase anyone used, bathrooms tiled in colors that peaked in 1962, and structural quirks that only reveal themselves once a wall comes down. Whether you're updating a post-war colonial on the south end of the municipality or converting an underused formal dining room into a home office, renovation work here calls for a contractor who reads older construction rather than fighting it.

## Why Mount Lebanon Homes Require a Different Renovation Approach

Mount Lebanon sits in the South Hills of Allegheny County, and its housing stock reflects nearly a century of continuous residential development. The oldest blocks, many built between the 1920s and 1950s, commonly feature plaster-and-lath interior walls, knob-and-tube or early Romex wiring tucked inside those walls, and cast-iron drain stacks that have served the home for decades. When a kitchen or bathroom remodel opens those walls, the scope of work often expands: a plaster wall that looks intact can conceal wiring that needs to be brought up to current code before new fixtures go in.

The climate adds its own layer. Southwestern Pennsylvania winters bring freeze-thaw cycles that work on mortar joints, foundation parging, and any exterior penetration that wasn't properly flashed. A bathroom addition or a rear mudroom expansion on a home in this area needs flashing and waterproofing details that account for that seasonal movement, not just the minimum required to pass inspection.

Mount Lebanon also operates under Allegheny County permit requirements, and the municipality has its own building inspection process for structural work, additions, and changes of occupancy. Pulling the right permits, scheduling inspections, and keeping the project on a realistic timeline around those inspections is part of the job, not an afterthought.

## Our Renovation and General Contracting Process

Every project starts with a walkthrough that treats the existing structure as information, not just an obstacle. Before a single wall is opened, we assess what's behind it: insulation type, framing condition, plumbing and electrical routing. In Mount Lebanon's older homes, that pre-work assessment routinely changes the material list and the schedule, and it's far better to know that on day one than to discover it mid-demolition.

From there, the process follows a sequenced trade schedule: structural work and rough framing first, then mechanical trades (plumbing, HVAC, electrical), then insulation and drywall, then finishes. For post-damage rebuilds, situations where fire, water, or mold remediation has already opened the structure, we coordinate directly with the remediation documentation so the rebuild scope aligns with what the carrier has approved.

Finishes are selected to work with the home's existing character where the homeowner wants continuity, or to make a clean departure where they don't. Either direction, the goal is a result that reads as intentional rather than patched.

## Coordinating with Mount Lebanon's Permit and Inspection Process

Structural alterations, additions, and significant mechanical work in Mount Lebanon require permits through the municipality. The permit review timeline affects project scheduling, and we build that window into the project plan from the start rather than treating it as a surprise delay. For kitchen remodels that involve moving load-bearing walls, or bathroom additions that require new drain lines, the permit drawings need to be detailed enough to move through review without multiple revision cycles.

Homes in the municipality that were built before 1978 also carry lead paint considerations for any work that disturbs painted surfaces. This is a practical reality in much of Mount Lebanon's housing stock, and it affects how demolition work is staged and how debris is handled.

## Local Note

In Mount Lebanon's older neighborhoods, the original bathroom tile work is often set directly over a mortar bed rather than cement board or drywall. That mud-set installation is actually more durable than modern thin-set methods in many respects, but it adds significant weight to the floor assembly. When a bathroom remodel involves removing that tile, the subfloor and joist condition underneath is worth assessing carefully before new materials go in, the mortar bed removal itself can reveal decades of slow moisture migration that never showed up as an obvious leak but has been working on the wood framing below.

If you're planning a bathroom renovation in a Mount Lebanon home built before 1960, budget time in the project schedule for that assessment. It's the kind of detail that separates a renovation that holds up for another fifty years from one that creates problems within a decade.
