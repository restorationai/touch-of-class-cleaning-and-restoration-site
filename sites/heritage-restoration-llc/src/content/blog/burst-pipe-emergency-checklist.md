If a pipe just burst in your home, here is what to do right now: shut off the main water supply, cut power to any affected rooms at the breaker box, and get standing water moving toward a drain or out the door with a mop or wet-vac. Every minute water sits on wood subfloor, drywall, or insulation, the damage compounds. This checklist walks you through the response in order, from the first 60 seconds through the weeks of drying and repair that follow.

---

## Step 1: Stop the Water and Protect Yourself

**Find your main shutoff valve first.** In most Minnesota homes built before the 1980s, it is in the basement near the front foundation wall or where the service line enters from the street. Newer construction often puts it in a utility room or crawl space. If you have never located it, do that today, not during a flood.

1. Turn the main shutoff valve clockwise until it stops. Water flow should slow within seconds as pressure drops.
2. Open a cold-water faucet on the lowest floor to drain pressure remaining in the lines.
3. Go to your electrical panel and flip the breakers for any room where water is present or could reach. Water and live outlets are a life-safety issue, not a property issue.
4. If the burst is near a gas appliance or you smell anything unusual, leave the house and call your gas utility before re-entering.

Once the water is off and the power is isolated, you can move safely.

---

## Step 2: Document Everything Before You Touch It

Insurance adjusters work from evidence. A phone camera is your best tool right now.

- Photograph the broken pipe itself, including any visible corrosion, joint failure, or freeze damage.
- Shoot wide angles of every affected room, then close-ups of wet flooring, baseboards, drywall, and personal property.
- If water has pooled, photograph the depth against a fixed reference, a door frame, a cabinet leg.
- Note the time and date on your photos, or confirm your phone is timestamping them automatically.

Do not move or discard damaged materials until your adjuster has seen them or you have confirmed in writing that documentation is sufficient. Throwing out a waterlogged carpet before it is inventoried can complicate your claim.

---

## Step 3: Begin Water Removal, But Know the Limits

Removing standing water quickly is the single highest-impact thing you can do to limit damage. Mold can begin colonizing wet porous materials in as little as 24 to 48 hours under warm conditions, and Minnesota's older housing stock, much of it with plaster walls, wood lath, and tongue-and-groove subfloor, holds moisture in ways that surface drying masks.

**What you can do yourself:**

- Use a wet-dry vacuum to pull water from hard floors and low-pile carpet.
- Blot (do not scrub) area rugs and move them to a dry area or outside if weather permits.
- Place towels along baseboards to wick water away from the wall-floor joint.
- Run box fans across wet surfaces and open windows if outdoor humidity is lower than indoor humidity, in a Minnesota winter, outdoor air is almost always drier.
- Remove soaked throw rugs, cardboard boxes, and furniture from the wet zone.

**What household tools cannot do:**

A shop vac removes surface water. It does not extract moisture from inside wall cavities, beneath hardwood flooring, or within fiberglass batt insulation. Water travels by capillary action, it wicks upward into drywall and inward through subfloor seams long after the visible puddle is gone. If you dry the surface and leave the structure wet, you are creating the conditions for hidden mold growth.

Professional water damage restoration uses calibrated moisture meters and thermal imaging to find water that is invisible at the surface, then places industrial air movers and dehumidifiers in a calculated pattern to draw moisture out of the structure itself. That is a different process from running a fan.

## What NOT to Do After a Pipe Bursts

Some common instincts make the situation worse.

- **Do not use a standard household vacuum** on standing water. It is not designed for it and creates an electrocution risk near wet surfaces.
- **Do not turn the heat up high** thinking it will dry things faster. Elevated heat without dehumidification raises indoor humidity and can accelerate mold growth.
- **Do not use bleach on wet drywall or subfloor** as a preemptive mold treatment. Bleach is a surface disinfectant; it does not penetrate porous materials, and it does not substitute for proper drying.
- **Do not assume the damage is limited to what you can see.** Water behind a plaster wall can read dry at the surface while the lath and framing behind it stay saturated for days.
- **Do not ignore a slow drip or minor seep** that preceded the burst. Pipe failures rarely happen without warning. Corrosion, pinhole leaks, and joint failures often announce themselves weeks before a full break, and the water from that slow leak may have already reached areas you have not checked.

## When to Call a Professional

Call a water damage restoration professional if any of the following are true:

- Standing water covered more than a few square feet of floor area.
- Water reached drywall, insulation, wood framing, or a finished ceiling below the leak.
- The leak ran for more than an hour before it was stopped.
- You can smell anything musty or earthy, that is microbial activity that is already underway.
- The affected area includes a bathroom, kitchen, or laundry room where an appliance supply line or drain may also be involved. Appliance leak cleanup often uncovers secondary damage behind cabinets and under flooring that is easy to miss.
- You are filing an insurance claim. Carriers expect documentation and drying logs that meet the IICRC S500 standard; a professional restoration company produces those records as part of the job.

## The Recovery Timeline: What Comes After the Emergency

Most homeowners underestimate how long proper drying takes. A wet wall cavity or subfloor assembly typically requires three to five days of continuous drying with professional equipment before moisture readings stabilize, sometimes longer in a cold Minnesota winter when the building envelope is under thermal stress.

Here is a general sequence:

1. **Days 1–2:** Water extraction, equipment placement, initial moisture mapping.
2. **Days 2–5:** Active drying with daily moisture readings to track progress and adjust equipment placement.
3. **Day 5 (approximately):** Final moisture verification. If readings are within acceptable range, equipment is removed.
4. **After drying is confirmed:** Damaged drywall, insulation, and flooring are removed and replaced. This is the reconstruction phase, patching, priming, painting, and flooring reinstallation.
5. **Insurance close-out:** Final documentation is submitted to your carrier. Keep copies of every moisture log, photo, and invoice.

Rushing any phase, pulling equipment early, skipping final readings, patching over wet drywall, typically results in mold appearing inside the wall within weeks.
