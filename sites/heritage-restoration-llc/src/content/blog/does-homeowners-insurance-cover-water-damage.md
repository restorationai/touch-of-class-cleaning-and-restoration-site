Homeowners insurance *sometimes* covers water damage, but the answer depends almost entirely on how the water got in. Damage from a sudden, accidental event (a pipe that bursts overnight, a washing machine hose that fails) is typically covered under a standard HO-3 policy. Damage from a slow leak you didn't catch, a backed-up sewer line, or rising floodwater usually is not. That distinction, sudden versus gradual, internal versus external, is the single most important thing to understand before you file a claim or start tearing out drywall.

## What Standard Homeowners Insurance Actually Covers

Most HO-3 policies treat water damage as two separate questions: where did the water come from, and could the homeowner have reasonably prevented it?

**Events that are typically covered:**

- A supply line to a refrigerator, dishwasher, or toilet fails suddenly
- A pipe freezes and bursts during a Minnesota winter
- A water heater ruptures unexpectedly
- An accidental overflow, a tub left running, a toilet that won't stop
- Rain enters through a roof or window that was damaged by a covered peril (like hail or wind)

**Events that are typically NOT covered under a standard policy:**

- A slow drip behind a wall that has been seeping for weeks or months
- Sewer or drain backup (this requires a separate endorsement, and it's worth adding if you have a basement in central Minnesota)
- Groundwater seeping through a foundation or window well
- Flooding from a river, lake, or overland runoff, that requires a separate NFIP or private flood policy
- Damage the insurer can show you knew about and ignored

The word adjusters use for that last category is *gradual deterioration*, and it's the most common reason water damage claims are denied. If a pipe joint has been weeping long enough to stain the drywall brown, the carrier may argue the damage wasn't sudden, and they may be right.

## How to Read Your Policy Before You Call Your Agent

Before you dial anyone, pull out your declarations page and look for two sections: **Coverage A** (dwelling) and the **perils insured against** language. HO-3 policies cover the dwelling on an open-perils basis, meaning everything is covered unless specifically excluded. Water exclusions are almost always listed explicitly.

Search the document for these words:

- *Flood*, almost certainly excluded; look for the definition, because some policies define flood broadly enough to include a burst pipe that flows across the floor
- *Seepage* or *leakage*, typically excluded when gradual
- *Sewer backup* or *drain backup*, check whether you have an endorsement (sometimes called Water Backup Coverage) that adds this back in
- *Mold*, many policies cap mold remediation at a sublimit (often $5,000–$10,000) even when the underlying water damage is covered

If the language is ambiguous, your agent can clarify, but go in knowing what you're looking at. Agents are helpful, but they work for the insurer, not for you.

## The First 24–48 Hours: What to Do Right Now

If you're dealing with active water damage while reading this, the steps below matter more than any coverage question. Mitigation, stopping additional damage, is something most policies *require* you to do. Failing to act can give the carrier grounds to reduce your payout.

1. **Stop the water at the source.** For a burst pipe, that means the main shutoff valve, usually near the water meter or where the supply line enters the house. For a failed appliance, turn off the supply valve behind or beneath the unit.
2. **Cut power to affected areas.** Water and live circuits are a serious hazard. If you're not certain which breakers control the wet zone, shut off the main.
3. **Document everything before you touch it.** Walk through with your phone and record video of standing water, wet materials, and any visible damage. Shoot the source of the water if you can safely reach it. This footage is your evidence.
4. **Remove standing water if you can do it safely.** A wet-dry shop vac works for small amounts. Don't use a household vacuum.
5. **Move salvageable belongings out of the wet area.** Furniture, rugs, and boxes sitting in water will absorb moisture and become unsalvageable quickly.
6. **Open windows and run fans if the outdoor air is drier than inside.** In a Minnesota summer, that's often true. In winter, keep the house warm, cold air slows drying and risks secondary freezing.
7. **Call your insurance company to open a claim.** Most carriers have 24-hour claim lines. Opening the claim does not commit you to anything; it starts the clock on their response obligation.

## What Not to Do

- **Don't rip out wet drywall or flooring before the adjuster visits**, unless the material is a health hazard or the adjuster has given you written authorization. Removing material before documentation can complicate your claim.
- **Don't use high-heat drying methods** (space heaters pointed at wet walls, heat guns). Rapid surface drying traps moisture inside structural cavities, which is exactly how mold colonizes framing and insulation within 24–72 hours.
- **Don't assume the surface is dry because it looks dry.** Water behind plaster or under a hardwood floor can read bone-dry at the surface while the subfloor and joists below stay saturated for weeks. Moisture meters and thermal imaging cameras tell a different story than your hand.
- **Don't sign anything from a restoration contractor before you understand what you're authorizing.** Assignment of Benefits agreements, in particular, transfer your claim rights to the contractor. That arrangement works fine with reputable companies and can be a problem with others.

## When the Damage Is More Than You Can Handle Yourself

Fans and a shop vac are appropriate for a small, contained spill on a hard floor. They are not appropriate for:

- Any water intrusion that reached insulation, subfloor, or wall cavities
- Standing water that sat for more than a few hours
- Water from a sewage source (gray or black water carries pathogens)
- Any situation where you can smell a musty or earthy odor, that's microbial growth starting
- Damage that spans more than one room or level

Professional water damage restoration involves calibrated drying equipment, commercial dehumidifiers, air movers, and moisture mapping, that removes water from structural materials at a rate that consumer equipment simply can't match. The goal isn't just a dry surface; it's getting the moisture content of wood framing and subfloor back to levels where mold cannot establish.

The IICRC S500 standard, which guides how professional restorers approach structural drying, defines specific moisture thresholds and drying timelines. A restoration crew will monitor those readings daily and adjust equipment placement until materials reach target levels, typically over three to five days for a Category 1 (clean water) loss in a well-ventilated space, longer for larger or more complex losses.

## The Longer Road: Reconstruction After Water Damage

Once structural materials are confirmed dry, the rebuild begins, replacing drywall, insulation, flooring, cabinetry, or whatever was removed during mitigation. This phase is where many homeowners are surprised to learn that their policy covers the cost to restore the home to its pre-loss condition, not necessarily to upgrade it. If your 1970s vinyl flooring was damaged, the carrier will typically pay to replace it with comparable material, not hardwood.

Keep every receipt, every invoice, and every piece of written communication with your adjuster. If the claim becomes complicated, a large loss, a dispute over scope, or a mold sublimit that doesn't cover the full remediation, a public adjuster (who works for you, not the carrier) or a restoration contractor experienced in insurance billing can help you navigate the process.
