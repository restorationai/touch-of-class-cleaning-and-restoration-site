## The Short Answer: Vet Before You're Desperate

The worst time to choose a restoration company is at 11 p.m. with water soaking through your subfloor. Contractors know this, and a few take advantage of it. The good news: most of the vetting you need to do takes about 20 minutes, and you can do it right now, before anything goes wrong. If you're already mid-crisis, skip to the checklist below. If you're researching ahead of time, read the whole thing. Either way, you'll finish this page knowing exactly what separates a contractor worth hiring from one worth avoiding.

## What the Restoration Industry Actually Looks Like

Restoration contracting, water damage, fire and smoke cleanup, mold remediation, is a licensed trade in Minnesota, but the barrier to entry is lower than most homeowners assume. A company can legally operate with a general contractor's license, a truck, and a dehumidifier. That's not necessarily a red flag on its own, but it means the credential gap between a well-trained crew and an undertrained one can be enormous, and you can't see it from a Google listing.

The professional benchmark most insurers and property managers look for is IICRC certification, the Institute of Inspection, Cleaning and Restoration Certification. IICRC-certified technicians are trained to standards like the S500 for water damage and the S520 for mold. These aren't just letters on a business card; they mean the crew understands psychrometrics (how temperature and humidity interact during drying), containment protocols, and when a structure is genuinely dry versus surface-dry. If a company can't tell you which IICRC standards govern their process, that's worth noting.

In central Minnesota, the housing stock adds another layer of complexity. Many homes in and around Morrison County were built between the 1920s and 1960s, which means older plumbing, plaster-and-lath walls that hold moisture differently than modern drywall, and in some cases, materials that predate modern hazardous-material regulations. A contractor who treats every job like new construction may miss what's actually happening inside your walls.

## The Vetting Checklist: What to Ask Before You Sign Anything

Whether you're calling companies proactively or responding to a loss right now, run through these five checks before you commit.

**1. Ask for their license and insurance documentation, in writing.**
A legitimate contractor will send you a certificate of insurance without hesitation. You want general liability coverage and workers' compensation. If a worker is injured on your property and the company lacks workers' comp, you may be exposed. Don't accept "we're covered" as an answer.

**2. Ask specifically about IICRC certification.**
Which standards do they follow? Which technicians on your job are certified? A company can be IICRC-certified at the corporate level while sending uncertified crews to the field. It's a fair question and a good contractor won't be offended by it.

**3. Find out who actually does the work.**
Some restoration companies are primarily marketing operations, they take your call, sign the contract, and then subcontract the physical work to whoever is available. Subcontracting isn't automatically bad, but you deserve to know who will be in your home and whether those people are held to the same standards.

**4. Get a written scope of work before any equipment is placed.**
A reputable company will assess the damage, document it with moisture readings and photographs, and give you a written estimate before they start billing. Be cautious of any contractor who wants to start immediately and "figure out the paperwork later", especially if they're asking you to sign an Assignment of Benefits (AOB) form, which transfers your insurance claim rights to them.

**5. Check how they handle insurance billing.**
Most established restoration companies work directly with insurance carriers and use industry-standard estimating software (Xactimate is the most common). Ask whether they'll document and submit the claim on your behalf, or whether you'll be left to negotiate with your adjuster alone.

## Red Flags That Should Make You Walk Away

The restoration industry has a handful of recurring scams and shortcuts that show up after every major storm or widespread loss event. Here's what they look like on the ground.

- **Storm chasers**: Companies that appear in a market only after a weather event, often with out-of-state plates and no local history. They may do adequate work, or they may disappear when a warranty issue surfaces six months later.
- **Pressure to sign immediately**: Legitimate damage assessment takes time. If a contractor is pushing you to sign a contract within the first 30 minutes of arriving, ask yourself why the urgency benefits them.
- **Vague or verbal scopes**: "We'll dry it out and see what we find" is not a scope of work. You need line items, equipment lists, and projected timelines in writing.
- **No moisture documentation**: For water damage specifically, a professional crew will take baseline moisture readings with a calibrated meter and track them daily. If no one is measuring, no one actually knows whether your structure is drying.
- **Mold guarantees**: No ethical contractor guarantees mold will never return after remediation. Mold is a moisture problem first; if the moisture source isn't fixed, mold will come back regardless of how thorough the remediation was. A contractor who promises otherwise is either uninformed or not being straight with you.

## What Good Restoration Work Actually Looks Like

For water damage, the process should follow a predictable arc: extract standing water, establish drying equipment (air movers and dehumidifiers calibrated to the space), monitor moisture daily, and remove only the materials that can't be dried in place. Walls that read dry at the surface can still have saturated lath or insulation behind them, a contractor relying on visual inspection alone will miss this. Drying typically takes several days, not hours, and the equipment should stay until readings confirm the structure has returned to normal moisture levels for your region.

For fire and smoke damage, the scope is often wider than it looks. Smoke travels through HVAC systems, settles into porous materials, and leaves behind acidic residue that continues to corrode surfaces even after the visible soot is cleaned. Odor that seems to fade in the first week can return when temperatures rise. A thorough fire restoration addresses the structure, the contents, and the air handling system, not just what's visibly charred.

For mold remediation, containment is the first priority, preventing spores from spreading to unaffected areas during the removal process. The work should follow EPA guidelines and, in Minnesota, be paired with fixing whatever moisture source allowed the mold to establish in the first place. Remediation without source correction is a temporary fix.
