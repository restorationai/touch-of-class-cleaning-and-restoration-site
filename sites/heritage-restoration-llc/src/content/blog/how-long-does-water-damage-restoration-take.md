Most water damage restoration takes **3 to 5 days for drying alone**, but the full process, from the moment water enters your home to the day the last coat of paint dries, often runs 1 to 4 weeks depending on how much material got wet, how long it sat, and what it soaked into. There is no universal answer, and anyone who quotes you a firm number before seeing the damage is guessing. What follows is a realistic, phase-by-phase breakdown so you know what to expect and why each step takes as long as it does.

## Phase 1: Emergency Mitigation (Hours 1–24)

The clock starts the moment water enters the structure. In the first 24 hours, the goal is simple: stop the source and remove standing water before it migrates further.

If a pipe burst, find your main shutoff valve, in most older Minnesota homes it's in the basement near the utility meter, and close it before you do anything else. If the water is still coming in from a storm or a roof failure, contain what you can with towels and buckets, but do not put yourself in a situation where you're wading through water near an electrical panel.

Once the source is controlled, the priority shifts to extraction. A wet-dry vacuum can pull surface water from hard floors. Towels work on small areas. But if water has spread across more than a couple hundred square feet, or if it has reached carpet, insulation, or wall cavities, extraction equipment, truck-mounted or portable, is the only realistic option. Hand-drying a saturated carpet pad is not drying it; it's delaying the problem.

This phase should happen within the first few hours. Every hour of delay allows water to travel further along subflooring, wick up drywall, and begin the conditions that lead to mold growth.

## Phase 2: Structural Drying (Days 1–5, Sometimes Longer)

This is the phase most homeowners underestimate, and it's where restoration timelines most often go sideways.

After standing water is extracted, the materials themselves are still saturated. Drywall, wood framing, subfloor panels, and insulation all hold water at a cellular level that you cannot see or feel at the surface. A wall can read dry to the touch while the lath or framing behind it holds enough moisture to sustain mold growth for weeks.

Professional drying uses a combination of industrial air movers and dehumidifiers calibrated to the room's humidity, temperature, and the type of material being dried. The IICRC S500 standard, the industry reference for water damage drying, classifies losses by category (clean water, gray water, black water) and class (how much material is affected), and drying protocols are built around those classifications. A Class 1 loss, say, a small supply line leak caught within a few hours, might dry in 2 to 3 days. A Class 3 or 4 loss, where water has saturated walls from floor to ceiling or soaked into a concrete slab, can take 5 to 7 days or longer.

Moisture readings are taken daily with a pin or pinless moisture meter. Drying is not complete when the surface feels dry, it's complete when readings return to baseline for that material in that climate. In central Minnesota, where outdoor humidity swings significantly between seasons, those baselines can vary, and a good technician accounts for that.

**What slows drying down:**
- Water that sat for more than 24–48 hours before extraction
- Wet insulation inside wall cavities (insulation almost always has to be removed, it does not dry in place)
- Engineered wood subfloor, which absorbs water faster and releases it more slowly than solid wood
- Finished basements, where vapor barriers and wall assemblies trap moisture
- Cold ambient temperatures, which reduce the efficiency of dehumidification

## Phase 3: Mold Risk Window (Days 2–7)

Mold does not appear overnight, but the conditions that allow it to colonize develop quickly. Under the right temperature and humidity conditions, mold can begin to establish on wet organic material, drywall paper, wood framing, carpet backing, within 24 to 72 hours of initial wetting.

This is why the drying phase is not optional or something to revisit next week. If you are managing a loss yourself and drying is taking longer than expected, watch for these early signs: a musty or earthy smell that wasn't there before, visible discoloration at the base of walls or along seams, or a persistent humidity that makes the air feel heavy even with windows open.

If mold is confirmed, either visually or through air sampling, the restoration timeline extends. Mold remediation is a separate scope of work from water damage drying, and it adds time and cost. It is not something to paint over or encapsulate without addressing the underlying moisture first.

## Phase 4: Demolition and Removal (If Needed)

Not every water loss requires tearing out material. A small, clean-water leak caught quickly may allow all affected materials to dry in place. But if drywall has been wet for more than 24–48 hours, if insulation is saturated, or if flooring cannot be dried without removing it, selective demolition becomes part of the process.

This typically means cutting drywall to a height above the moisture line (often called a "flood cut"), removing wet insulation, and pulling up flooring to access the subfloor beneath. None of this is destructive for its own sake, it's the only way to get airflow and dehumidification to the materials that actually need it.

Demolition adds 1 to 3 days to the timeline, depending on scope. It also creates a documentation record that matters for insurance purposes: photographs of affected materials, moisture readings before and after, and a scope of work that your adjuster can review.

## Phase 5: Reconstruction (Days 7–30+)

Once everything is dry and any mold concerns are resolved, the rebuild begins. This is the phase that looks most like normal construction: hanging new drywall, taping and mudding, priming, painting, reinstalling flooring, replacing trim.

Timeline here depends almost entirely on scope. Replacing a section of drywall in a utility room is a one-day job. Rebuilding a finished basement with new flooring, insulation, drywall, and trim throughout can take two to four weeks, especially if materials need to be ordered or if the work involves multiple trades (plumber, electrician, drywaller, flooring installer).

If your home was built before the mid-1980s, which covers a significant portion of the housing stock in Little Falls and throughout Morrison County, there's an additional consideration: older construction may contain lead paint or asbestos-containing materials in floor tiles, joint compound, or pipe insulation. Disturbing those materials during reconstruction requires specific handling protocols. A restoration contractor working in this region should be familiar with those requirements.

## When to Call a Professional

If the affected area is small, the water is clean, and you caught it within a few hours, you may be able to manage initial drying yourself with fans and a dehumidifier. But call a restoration professional if:

- Water has spread beyond the room of origin
- The source was a toilet, sewage backup, or any water you can't confirm is clean
- The smell is already musty
- You can't verify that walls and subfloor are actually dry, not just surface-dry
- Your insurance carrier is involved and you need documented drying logs
