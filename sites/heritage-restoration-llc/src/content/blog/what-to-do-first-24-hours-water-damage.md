Stop the water first. If a pipe burst or an appliance failed, find your main shutoff valve, usually in the basement, utility room, or crawl space near where the line enters the house, and turn it off. If the source is a roof leak or window seam, move what you can and contain the spread. Every minute water sits, it travels further: under hardwood, behind baseboards, into wall cavities. The first 24 hours determine whether you're dealing with a drying job or a gut-and-rebuild.

## Step One: Stop the Source and Make It Safe

Before you touch anything else, two questions matter: Is the water still coming in? And is it safe to be in the space?

**Shut off the water supply.** If you don't know where your main shutoff is, find it now, before you need it in a panic. In older Little Falls homes with basements, it's typically on the front foundation wall near the water meter. Turn it clockwise until it stops.

**Cut power to affected rooms if there's standing water.** Water and live outlets don't mix. If you're not certain which breakers control the flooded area, shut off the main panel and call an electrician before re-entering. This is not optional.

**Identify the water category.** Not all water damage is the same:
- *Category 1*, clean water from a supply line or rain. Safest to handle yourself in the short term.
- *Category 2*, gray water from an appliance drain, dishwasher, or washing machine overflow. Contains some contaminants; limit contact.
- *Category 3*, black water from a sewage backup, toilet overflow, or floodwater that's been sitting. Treat this as a biohazard. Don't wade through it, and don't run fans that will aerosolize it.

If you're not sure what category you're dealing with, assume the worst and keep family and pets out of the space.

## Step Two: Document Everything Before You Move Anything

Your insurance claim lives and dies by documentation. Before you pull up carpet, move furniture, or start mopping, spend 10 minutes with your phone.

- Walk every affected room and shoot video, narrating as you go. Capture ceilings, walls, floors, and the source.
- Photograph serial numbers and model tags on any damaged appliances.
- Take wide shots of each room, then close-ups of the waterline on walls, buckled flooring, and soaked materials.
- Note the time and date in your first video frame, say it out loud.

Don't throw anything away yet, even if it looks ruined. Adjusters need to see damaged materials in place or at least on-site. A soaked mattress you drag to the curb before an adjuster visits is a claim you may not recover.

## Step Three: Slow the Spread, What You Can Do Right Now

You won't dry a house with a mop, but you can limit secondary damage while you wait for professional equipment.

**Remove standing water with a wet/dry vacuum** if you have one. Focus on hard floors first, water migrates under baseboards into wall cavities within hours.

**Pull up area rugs and move furniture off wet carpet.** Wet rugs left on hardwood will stain and warp the floor beneath them. Place aluminum foil or plastic wrap under furniture legs to prevent dye transfer and rust staining on carpet.

**Open windows if outdoor humidity is lower than indoor humidity.** In a Minnesota summer, that's often not the case, humid outside air introduced to a wet space can make things worse. In winter, cold dry air actually helps. Use your judgment, or check the outdoor dew point.

**Run whatever air movement you have**, box fans, ceiling fans, to keep air circulating over wet surfaces. This is not the same as professional drying, but it slows mold's clock.

**Do not run your HVAC system** if ducts pass through the affected area. You risk spreading moisture (and potential contaminants) throughout the house.

## What Not to Do in the First 24 Hours

Some instincts make things worse. Avoid these:

- **Don't use a regular household vacuum on standing water.** It's a fire and electrocution hazard, and it will destroy the motor.
- **Don't use a hair dryer or space heater to dry walls.** Concentrated heat on wet drywall can cause surface cracking while the interior stays saturated, and it's a fire risk.
- **Don't paint over water stains or seal walls** before the structure is confirmed dry. Trapping moisture behind a vapor barrier is how you grow mold inside a wall you can't see.
- **Don't wait to call your insurance company.** Most policies require prompt notice of a loss. Waiting days can complicate your claim even if the damage itself is clearly covered.
- **Don't assume it's dry because the surface feels dry.** Concrete slabs, plaster walls, and wood subfloors can read dry at the surface while holding significant moisture inside. Professionals use moisture meters and thermal imaging cameras to find what you can't feel.

## When to Call a Water Damage Professional

You can handle a small, contained spill, a dishwasher that overflowed onto a tile floor, cleaned up within the hour. But call a restoration company when:

- The affected area is larger than roughly 10 square feet of flooring or one wall section
- Water has been sitting for more than a few hours
- The source was gray or black water
- Water reached carpet, drywall, insulation, or subfloor
- You can smell a musty or sour odor, that's microbial activity beginning
- The loss involves a finished basement, crawl space, or any area with limited airflow

Mold can begin colonizing wet organic material, drywall paper, wood framing, carpet backing, within 24 to 48 hours under the right conditions. Central Minnesota's climate means that window can be even shorter in humid summer months. Waiting to see if it dries on its own is often the most expensive decision a homeowner makes.

Professional water damage restoration involves industrial air movers and dehumidifiers that pull many times more moisture per hour than consumer equipment, moisture mapping to confirm the drying goal is actually met, and documentation that supports your insurance claim from start to finish.

## The Days After: What Recovery Actually Looks Like

Even after the visible water is gone, the drying process typically takes three to five days with professional equipment, sometimes longer for thick concrete slabs or dense insulation. During that time:

- Expect loud equipment running continuously. This is normal and necessary.
- Expect a technician to check moisture readings daily and adjust equipment placement.
- Expect some materials, heavily saturated drywall, carpet pad, insulation, to be removed rather than dried in place. This isn't damage; it's the correct protocol. Drying wet insulation inside a wall cavity is rarely effective and creates long-term mold risk.
- Once the structure is confirmed dry, reconstruction can begin: new drywall, flooring, paint, and finish work.

If your loss is insurance-covered, a restoration company that handles direct billing can coordinate the documentation, drying logs, and scope of work your adjuster needs, keeping you out of the middle of a process that can otherwise feel overwhelming.
