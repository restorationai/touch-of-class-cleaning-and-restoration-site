Ice dams form along low-slope eaves, wind-driven snow works under aging shingles, and spring melt can expose damage that went undetected all season. ## Why Randall Properties Face Particular Roofing Challenges

Morrison County's climate is the primary driver of roofing wear in Randall. The region averages significant snowfall each winter, and the temperature swings between January lows and March thaw cycles create repeated freeze-thaw stress on shingles, flashing, and underlayment. Asphalt shingles that perform reasonably in milder climates can crack and curl here within a few years if they were not rated for the thermal cycling Minnesota demands.

Older homes in the Randall area, many built during mid-century rural construction booms, often have roof decks made from board sheathing rather than modern plywood or OSB. That original sheathing can hold moisture differently than sheet goods, and when it deteriorates, a simple shingle replacement turns into a deck repair or full replacement. Identifying that condition before tearing off the old roof is part of how a proper installation starts here.

Ventilation is another factor specific to this region. Tight attic spaces common in older Morrison County homes trap heat in winter, accelerating ice dam formation at the eaves. A new roof installed without correcting the ventilation profile will develop the same problems within a few seasons.

## Our Roofing Installation and Replacement Process in Randall

Every project starts with a thorough inspection of the existing roof system, including the deck, underlayment, flashing at all penetrations and valleys, and the soffit and ridge ventilation. We document what we find before any material is ordered, so there are no surprises once the tear-off begins.

Tear-off is done completely. Layering new shingles over old ones is a shortcut that masks deck problems and adds unnecessary weight. Once the deck is exposed, any soft spots, rot, or damaged sheathing are addressed before the new system goes down.

Underlayment selection matters in Minnesota. Synthetic underlayments rated for cold-weather application provide better moisture resistance and stay workable in lower temperatures than traditional felt. Ice and water shield is applied at the eaves and in valleys, where ice dam damage is most likely to occur. Shingles are then installed per manufacturer specifications, with fastener patterns and overlap dimensions that meet both the product warranty requirements and Minnesota's wind-load considerations.

Flashing at chimneys, skylights, pipe boots, and wall intersections is replaced or resealed as part of every full replacement. These are the points where most leaks originate, and new shingles around failing flashing are not a lasting repair.
