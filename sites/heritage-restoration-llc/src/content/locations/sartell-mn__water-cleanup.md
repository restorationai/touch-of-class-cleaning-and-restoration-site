## Why Sartell Properties See Water Damage Issues

Sartell's position on the Mississippi gives the city a lot of character, and a lot of water exposure. The river corridor sees significant ice movement in late winter and early spring, and when jams break upstream, water levels can rise quickly in low-lying areas near the riverbank. Away from the river, Sartell's newer residential developments, many built on former agricultural land, can sit over clay-heavy soils that drain slowly. After a heavy rain, that slow drainage means water lingers against foundation walls and finds its way through window wells, basement egress openings, and slab cracks.

The city also has a mix of construction eras. Neighborhoods developed in the 1980s and 1990s often used fiberglass batt insulation in basement rim joists, insulation that wicks moisture and holds it against the framing long after the visible water is gone. Newer construction near the north end of town tends to have finished basements with drywall and carpet, which absorb quickly and show damage slowly. Neither type is forgiving if water sits for more than 24 to 48 hours.

## Our Water Cleanup Process in Sartell

The first thing that matters when water is present is stopping it from spreading. On arrival, the crew identifies the source, whether that's a failed water heater, a backed-up floor drain, or water migrating through the foundation, and addresses it before extraction begins. Leaving the source active while cleaning up is one of the most common reasons water damage costs more than it should.

Extraction comes next. Truck-mounted and portable extractors pull standing water from carpet, hardwood, and concrete. Once the bulk water is gone, the real work starts: moisture mapping with thermal imaging and calibrated meters to find water that has migrated behind drywall, under flooring, or into wall cavities. In Sartell's colder months, that mapping step is especially important because the temperature differential between a heated interior and a cold exterior wall can mask moisture readings at the surface while the cavity stays saturated.

Drying follows a structured plan based on the IICRC S500 standard, the number and placement of air movers and dehumidifiers is calculated, not guessed. Daily monitoring tracks whether the materials are drying on schedule. If readings plateau, the crew adjusts equipment placement or opens additional wall cavities. Documentation of every reading is kept throughout, which matters when an insurance claim is involved.

## Local Note

Sartell homes built in the 1990s along the river-adjacent streets often have basement walls finished with wood-framed stud walls furred out from the poured concrete, a common detail from that era that creates a hidden cavity between the framing and the foundation. When water enters through the wall, it collects in that cavity and can travel laterally along the bottom plate for several feet before showing up as a wet carpet or a damp baseboard. Thermal imaging catches this pattern; a visual inspection alone typically misses it. If your basement finished wall feels dry to the touch but smells musty after a water event, that cavity is worth checking before the drywall is assumed to be fine.
