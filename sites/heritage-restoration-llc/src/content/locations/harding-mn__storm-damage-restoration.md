Minnesota's storm season hits Morrison County hard, and Harding is no exception. The combination of open agricultural land surrounding the community and the region's exposure to fast-moving squall lines off the prairie means wind-driven rain, hail, and downed trees can arrive with very little warning. When a storm tears through and leaves a roof open to the sky or pushes a tree through a garage wall, the window for preventing secondary damage is short, and the work that follows requires more than a tarp and a shop vac.

## Why Harding Properties See Storm Damage Issues

Morrison County sits in a climate band where late spring and early summer bring severe thunderstorm watches on a near-weekly basis. Straight-line winds are the most common culprit here, and they behave differently than a tornado's rotational path: instead of a narrow damage corridor, they can strip shingles across an entire block, push water under fascia boards along a wide stretch of homes, and leave dozens of properties with compromised envelopes at once. Harding's mix of older farmstead-style construction and more recent rural residential builds means two neighboring properties can have completely different vulnerabilities. A home with a steeper roof pitch and heavier asphalt shingles may shed wind better, while a lower-slope structure with aging three-tab shingles is far more likely to lose material in a 70-mph gust. Once the roof deck is exposed, even a brief rain event can saturate insulation, push water into wall cavities, and begin the clock on mold colonization, which can start within 24 to 48 hours in warm, humid conditions.

Hail is a separate but equally damaging problem in this part of central Minnesota. A hailstorm that leaves dents in gutters and vehicles has almost certainly also fractured the granule coating on asphalt shingles, accelerating UV degradation and reducing the roof's ability to shed water. That damage is often invisible from the ground but shows up clearly during a proper inspection.

## Our Storm Damage Restoration Process in Harding

The first priority after a storm is stopping additional water from entering the structure. That means emergency tarping of breached roof sections, boarding of broken windows or damaged siding, and a thorough interior moisture assessment to understand what has already gotten in. Heritage Restoration LLC uses thermal imaging and calibrated moisture meters to find water that has migrated behind walls or under flooring, not just what is visible at the surface.

Once the structure is stabilized, the scope of work becomes clearer. Structural drying follows the IICRC S500 and S520 standards, using commercial-grade desiccant dehumidifiers and high-velocity air movers positioned to draw moisture out of building assemblies rather than just surface materials. Moisture readings are logged daily so the drying process is documented, which matters both for the structural integrity of the repair and for the insurance claim record.

After materials are confirmed dry, reconstruction begins: roofing, siding, insulation, drywall, and any structural framing that was compromised. Because Heritage Restoration LLC holds a general contractor license (#BC807677), the restoration and rebuild happen under one roof rather than requiring a homeowner to coordinate separate trades.

Storm damage claims in Minnesota are handled by the homeowner's property insurer, and the documentation quality you submit at the start of the claim shapes how smoothly the process goes. ## Local Note

One pattern worth knowing in this part of Morrison County: older rural homes in the Harding area were often built with board-sheathed roof decks rather than plywood or OSB. When water gets under damaged shingles and into board sheathing, it tends to absorb unevenly, and individual boards can cup or split before the moisture reading at the surface suggests a problem. During inspections after a significant rain event, Heritage Restoration LLC probes the deck from the attic side as well as the exterior, because a surface reading alone can miss the early stages of sheathing failure in these older assemblies.
