## Why St. Stephen Properties See Storm Damage Differently

Central Minnesota's storm season runs longer than most homeowners expect, with significant hail and wind events possible from April through October. The flat-to-rolling terrain around St. Stephen means wind has room to accelerate before it reaches a roofline, and gusts that register as "moderate" on a weather report can still lift ridge caps, crack soffit panels, and drive rain horizontally into attic spaces.

The housing stock in this part of Stearns County skews older. Many homes were built with dimensional lumber framing and board sheathing rather than modern OSB, which means they handle incidental moisture differently. Board sheathing can cup and separate when wet, creating gaps that weren't there before the storm. Older windows with wood frames absorb water at the sill and can swell shut or, worse, allow water to wick into the rough opening and down into the wall cavity without any visible interior sign. A surface inspection after a storm can miss what's happening two feet inside the wall.

Agricultural outbuildings, detached garages, and pole barns are also common on St. Stephen properties, and they present their own complications: large uninsulated spans, metal roofing that can lift at the seam, and concrete slab floors that pool water with no drain.

## Our Storm Damage Restoration Process in St. Stephen

The first priority after any storm is stopping new water from entering. That means emergency tarping of breached roofs and boarding of broken windows or doors before any interior work begins. Once the structure is secured, the assessment shifts inward.

Moisture mapping with thermal imaging and calibrated meters identifies where water has traveled, not just where it landed. In older St. Stephen homes with plaster ceilings or tongue-and-groove interior paneling, water can migrate laterally for several feet before showing a visible stain. The IICRC structural drying standards we follow account for this, establishing drying goals based on material type and baseline readings rather than a fixed number of days.

Tree impact damage gets its own evaluation. When a large limb or full trunk strikes a roof, the structural load can compromise rafters or even the top plate of exterior walls. We document that damage photographically and in writing for the insurance claim before any debris is removed, because the adjuster needs to see the point of impact, not a cleaned-up hole.

Debris removal, structural drying, and controlled demolition of unsalvageable materials are sequenced to keep the home weather-tight throughout the process. Reconstruction coordination follows once drying is confirmed.
