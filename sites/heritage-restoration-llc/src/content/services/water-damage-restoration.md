Standing water doesn't wait. Within the first hour after a pipe bursts or a washing machine supply line fails, water is already wicking into drywall, migrating under hardwood, and saturating the subfloor beneath your feet, surfaces that can feel dry to the touch while holding enough moisture to feed mold growth within 24 to 48 hours. Water damage restoration is the race against that clock: getting the water out, drying the structure completely, and verifying with instruments, not guesswork, that the building is back to a safe moisture baseline.

## What Water Damage Restoration actually involves

Water damage restoration is not a shop-vac and a few box fans. A proper response uses truck-mounted or portable extraction units capable of pulling hundreds of gallons from carpet, pad, and subfloor assemblies. Once standing water is removed, the real work begins: placing commercial-grade refrigerant dehumidifiers and high-velocity air movers in a calculated pattern to drive moisture out of wall cavities, flooring systems, and structural framing.

Throughout drying, technicians use penetrating moisture meters and thermal imaging cameras to track what's happening inside the wall, not just at the surface. A reading at the drywall face can look acceptable while the wood stud behind it remains saturated. Drying typically takes three to five days for a Category 1 (clean water) loss in a single room, but that timeline extends when water has migrated to multiple floors, when the source was a sewage backup (Category 3), or when the structure has older plaster-and-lath walls that hold moisture differently than modern drywall assemblies.

Heritage Restoration LLC holds IICRC certifications in Water Restoration Technology (WRT), Applied Structural Drying (ASD), and is licensed in Minnesota (#BC807677), which means the work follows documented industry standards, not a technician's best guess.

## Our process

1. **Emergency water removal.** Extraction comes first. Submersible pumps handle deep standing water; truck-mounted or portable extractors follow for carpet, pad, and hard flooring. Every minute water sits, it moves further into the structure.

2. **Moisture mapping and category assessment.** Before equipment is placed, technicians map the full extent of the loss using moisture meters and thermal imaging. The water source is classified, clean supply line, gray water from an appliance, or black water from sewage, because Category 2 and 3 losses require containment protocols and affected porous materials (drywall, insulation, carpet) are typically removed rather than dried in place.

3. **Structural drying setup.** Dehumidifiers and air movers are positioned according to the IICRC S500 standard's psychrometric principles, the right equipment ratio for the square footage, ceiling height, and material types present. This is not a set-it-and-forget-it step; equipment placement is adjusted daily based on readings.

4. **Daily moisture monitoring.** A technician returns each day to record moisture readings at every monitoring point, log ambient temperature and humidity, and adjust equipment. These logs become part of the claim documentation.

5. **Final clearance and documentation.** Drying is complete when all affected materials reach acceptable moisture content relative to unaffected reference materials in the same structure, not when the floor feels dry underfoot. A written moisture log is provided for your insurance file.

## What separates a good water damage response from a bad one

The most common failure in water damage work is declaring a structure dry before it actually is. Technicians who rely on surface-contact readings alone, without penetrating meters or thermal imaging, miss moisture in wall cavities, beneath flooring systems, and inside insulated ceiling assemblies. That moisture doesn't disappear; it feeds mold.

A second common gap is under-scoping the affected area. Water follows gravity and air pressure, not floor plans. A leak on the second floor frequently appears in the ceiling of the room below, then wicks laterally into adjacent walls. Adjusters and experienced technicians know to trace the migration path, not just treat the visible wet spot.

For Category 2 and 3 losses, containment matters. Gray and black water carry contaminants that require negative air pressure, proper PPE, and disposal of affected porous materials, not just drying them out. Skipping containment on a sewage backup isn't a shortcut; it's a liability.

Insurance adjusters look for a daily moisture log, a clear scope of work tied to the IICRC S500 standard, and photo documentation of equipment placement and removal. A well-documented file moves faster through the claims process.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Little Falls and the surrounding Morrison County area see freeze-thaw cycles that stress older plumbing, particularly in homes with pipes running through exterior walls or unheated crawl spaces. A hard cold snap in January or February is the most common trigger for burst-pipe losses in this region. Spring snowmelt adds a second peak, when saturated ground pushes water through foundation cracks and window wells faster than sump pumps can keep up.

Older housing stock in the area, including homes with plaster-and-lath construction, holds moisture differently than newer drywall assemblies and often requires extended drying times and closer monitoring.
