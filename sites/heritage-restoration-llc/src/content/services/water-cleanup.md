Standing water doesn't wait. Within the first hour, it's already moving, wicking into drywall, soaking beneath hardwood, pooling inside wall cavities where no towel or shop vac can reach. By 24 hours, wet cellulose materials begin to support mold growth. By 48 to 72 hours, structural materials that looked fine on the surface can be saturated several inches deep. Water cleanup is a race against a timeline most homeowners don't know is already running.

## What water cleanup actually involves

Water cleanup is not mopping up what you can see. The visible water, on floors, in corners, against baseboards, is typically the smallest part of the problem. The larger concern is what migrated into the building materials around it.

Effective water damage cleanup requires industrial extraction equipment, not consumer wet-vacs. Truck-mounted or portable extractors pull water from carpet, pad, and subfloor at volumes that make a household unit irrelevant. After extraction, the drying phase begins, and this is where the work gets technical. Refrigerant and desiccant dehumidifiers pull moisture from the air while high-velocity air movers accelerate evaporation from wet surfaces. The goal is to create a controlled drying environment that moves moisture out of materials and into the air before the equipment captures it.

Moisture meters and thermal imaging cameras map what the eye can't see: wet insulation inside walls, saturated subfloor beneath vinyl plank, moisture trapped behind tile backer. Without that data, you're guessing, and guessing wrong means mold colonies establishing inside a wall that was declared dry.

Timeline varies by loss category and material type. A clean-water pipe burst affecting a single carpeted room may dry in three to five days under proper conditions. A Category 2 or 3 loss, sewage backup, appliance overflow with contaminated water, or flood water cleanup after a storm event, involves additional containment, antimicrobial treatment, and in many cases controlled demolition of materials that cannot be safely dried in place.

## Our process

1. **Assessment and water classification.** Before a single piece of equipment is placed, the water source is identified and the loss category is determined. Clean water from a supply line is handled differently than gray water from a dishwasher or black water from a sewage backup. Category affects every downstream decision: what materials can be dried versus removed, what PPE is required, and how the insurance documentation reads.

2. **Extraction.** High-capacity extractors remove standing water and pull moisture from carpet, pad, and porous flooring. In Category 2 and 3 losses, affected materials that cannot be safely dried, saturated drywall below the flood line, soaked insulation, are removed during this phase to prevent contamination from spreading.

3. **Drying system placement and monitoring.** Dehumidifiers and air movers are positioned based on the moisture map, not placed at random. Daily moisture readings track drying progress against target values for each material type. Equipment is adjusted as readings change, a drying system that isn't monitored is just running up your electric bill.

4. **Antimicrobial treatment.** Affected surfaces are treated with EPA-registered antimicrobials appropriate to the water category. This step is not optional on Category 2 or 3 losses and is documented for the insurance file.

5. **Final moisture verification and documentation.** Drying is not complete until readings confirm materials have reached acceptable moisture content, not when the equipment has run a set number of days. Final readings are logged, photographed, and included in the claim documentation package.

## What separates a good water cleanup response from a bad one

The most common failure in flood water cleanup is stopping at extraction. Water is pulled from the surface, the space looks dry, and equipment is removed, while moisture readings inside wall cavities and under flooring are still elevated. Mold shows up six weeks later and the homeowner has no documentation proving the original responder missed it.

A second common problem is misclassifying the water source. Gray or black water treated as clean water creates a health hazard and an insurance documentation problem. Adjusters increasingly scrutinize water category classification, and a claim that doesn't match the loss documentation can delay or reduce payment.

Insurance adjusters look for a complete moisture log, baseline readings taken before drying begins, daily progress readings, and final clearance readings tied to IICRC S500 drying standards. Without that paper trail, disputes over scope and completeness are common. The IICRC certifications our team holds, including Water Restoration Technician (WRT) and Applied Structural Drying (ASD), mean the documentation we produce is written in the same language adjusters and carriers expect.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Little Falls and the surrounding Morrison County area see conditions that make water losses more frequent and more complicated than in milder climates. Minnesota's freeze-thaw cycle, hard freezes through February, rapid thaw in March and April, is the leading driver of burst pipe losses. Supply lines in exterior walls, pipes in unheated garages, and older homes with minimal insulation in rim joist areas are the most common failure points.

Spring snowmelt combined with saturated ground creates basement seepage and sump pump overload events that often affect multiple properties in the same neighborhood simultaneously. Older housing stock in the Little Falls area, much of it built before modern vapor barriers and drainage mat systems, can absorb and hold moisture in ways that newer construction doesn't. That means longer drying times and more careful moisture mapping to avoid missing pockets of saturation in older plaster and lath walls.

Summer storms bring their own risks: heavy rainfall overwhelming window wells, storm drains backing up, and water entering through foundation cracks that were stable all winter. High summer humidity also slows the drying process, which means equipment run-time, and cost, can be longer in July than in January for a comparable loss.
