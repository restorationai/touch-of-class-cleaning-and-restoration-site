Choosing a restoration company after a disaster is one of the worst purchasing decisions you'll ever have to make — not because it's complicated, but because you're usually making it while water is still spreading across your floor or smoke smell is soaking into your walls. Contractors know this. Some of them count on it. The good news: a handful of specific checks, done in about 20 minutes, will separate the legitimate operators from the storm chasers and the unlicensed crews who disappear after the deposit clears.

## Why the Restoration Industry Attracts Bad Actors

Restoration work — whether it's [water damage](water-damage-restoration), [fire damage](fire-damage-restoration), or [mold remediation](mold-remediation) — sits at an unusual intersection: jobs are urgent, insurance is usually paying, and the work happens inside walls and under floors where it's hard to verify quality after the fact. That combination creates real opportunity for price inflation, unnecessary scope, and shoddy drying that gets covered up before the moisture readings are actually safe.

In Southern California, the problem is compounded by the sheer volume of contractors. After a significant rain event or a pipe failure in a slab-foundation home (common in older Cerritos and Long Beach housing stock), you'll get door-knocking within hours. Some of those crews are excellent. Some are not licensed in California, carry no general liability insurance, and have no formal training in drying science or containment.

The framework below helps you tell the difference quickly.

## Five Things to Verify Before You Sign Anything

Do these five checks before you authorize any work — even emergency mitigation. Most take under five minutes each.

1. **Confirm California contractor licensing.** Go to the California Contractors State License Board website (cslb.ca.gov) and search the company name or license number. You want an active license in the correct classification — typically a "B" General Building license or a "C-33" Painting and Decorating license for mold work. If a contractor can't give you a license number on the spot, that's your answer.

2. **Ask for a certificate of insurance — not a verbal assurance.** You want general liability and workers' compensation. If a worker is injured in your home and the company has no workers' comp, you can be held liable. Request the certificate be emailed to you before work starts. Legitimate companies have this document ready.

3. **Check for IICRC certification.** The Institute of Inspection, Cleaning and Restoration Certification sets the drying and remediation standards the industry actually uses. An IICRC Water Restoration Technician (WRT) or Applied Microbial Remediation Technician (AMRT) credential means the technician has been trained in the science behind what they're doing — not just the labor. You can verify credentials at iicrc.org.

4. **Read the authorization form before you sign it.** Some contractors use Assignment of Benefits (AOB) clauses that transfer your insurance rights to them, giving them direct control over your claim. California has restrictions on AOB in property insurance, but the language still appears. If you see it, ask your insurance adjuster before signing.

5. **Ask who actually does the work.** Many restoration companies are primarily sales and project management operations that subcontract the physical labor. That's not automatically bad — but you should know. If the crew showing up has never met the project manager who sold you the job, quality control gets complicated fast.

## Red Flags During the Estimate

A legitimate restoration estimate should be specific, not round-numbered. If someone quotes you "$4,000 to dry out the house" without walking every affected room with a moisture meter, that number came from somewhere other than your actual loss.

Watch for these specific warning signs:

- **Pressure to sign immediately** before your insurance adjuster has been notified. Your insurer has the right to inspect the damage before major work begins (emergency mitigation is an exception — stopping active water intrusion is always appropriate).
- **Scope that grows dramatically after demo begins.** Some expansion is normal — you can't see inside walls. But if the estimate doubles after day one without a documented moisture map showing why, ask for the readings in writing.
- **No written drying logs.** Professional water damage mitigation is a data-driven process. Technicians should be recording temperature, relative humidity, and material moisture content daily. If they can't show you logs, they're not drying to standard.
- **Mold found "everywhere" on a first visit without air sampling.** Visual mold assessment has limits. A responsible remediator will tell you what they can see and recommend third-party testing if the scope is unclear — not immediately quote you a whole-house remediation based on a smell.

## What to Do in the First Hour (Before You Call Anyone)

If you're dealing with active water damage, your first moves matter regardless of who you eventually hire.

1. Shut off the water source if you can. Your main shutoff is typically near the water meter at the street or in a utility closet.
2. Document everything with your phone before touching it. Video is better than photos for insurance purposes — walk every affected room.
3. Call your insurance company to open a claim. You don't have to have a contractor lined up first. Opening the claim starts the clock on your coverage and gets an adjuster assigned.
4. Move electronics, documents, and irreplaceable items out of wet areas if it's safe to do so.
5. Do not run fans or your HVAC system if you suspect mold is present — you can spread spores through ductwork.

For fire or smoke damage, the same documentation-first principle applies. Resist the urge to clean soot yourself; certain smoke residues (particularly from synthetic materials) require specific chemical neutralization, and scrubbing can drive particles deeper into porous surfaces.

## How to Compare Multiple Bids

Get at least two estimates for any job over a few thousand dollars. When comparing them, don't compare the bottom-line numbers — compare the scope line by line.

A lower bid that excludes cavity drying, antimicrobial treatment, or post-drying clearance testing isn't actually cheaper. You'll pay for those omissions later, either in a mold problem that develops in six months or in a reinspection that your insurance company requires before they'll close the claim.

Ask each contractor: *"What does your drying protocol look like, and how will I know when the structure is dry enough to rebuild?"* The answer should reference moisture content targets for the specific materials in your home (wood framing, drywall, concrete subfloor) — not just "when it looks dry" or "after three days."

## The Longer Recovery: What Comes After Mitigation

Mitigation — stopping the damage and drying the structure — is only the first phase. Reconstruction (replacing drywall, flooring, cabinetry) is a separate scope, and not all mitigation companies do both. Know before you start whether you're hiring one company for the full job or two separate contractors who will need to coordinate.

For mold jobs specifically, best practice is to have post-remediation clearance testing done by an independent industrial hygienist — someone who has no financial stake in whether the work passes. Some remediators offer in-house clearance, which is a conflict of interest worth noting.

The full timeline for a significant water loss — from emergency call to finished reconstruction — typically runs four to eight weeks depending on material availability and permit requirements. Anyone who tells you two weeks for a major loss is either cutting corners or hasn't seen the full scope yet.
