## Restoration emergencies common in Potomac

Most of Potomac's housing sits on large wooded lots west of the Beltway, with mature tree canopy that drops limbs on roofs during Mid-Atlantic thunderstorms and nor'easters. That same tree cover, combined with clay-heavy soil across much of Montgomery County, means storm water drains slowly and tends to pond against foundations and walk-out basements rather than running off quickly. Winter brings its own risk: a hard freeze followed by a quick thaw is a classic setup for a burst supply line or a split hose bib, especially in homes with older copper or galvanized plumbing. Between the tree exposure, the clay subsoil, and a building stock that spans several plumbing eras, basement flooding and frozen-pipe calls are the two patterns we see most.

## Services we provide in Potomac

Water damage in Potomac often starts below grade, in a finished basement or a walk-out level that backs up to a wooded slope. We extract standing water, pull saturated baseboard and drywall where needed, and set up monitored drying equipment documented to the IICRC S500 standard so the drying is defensible if a carrier questions the scope.

Fire and smoke damage calls frequently involve larger single-family homes with multiple HVAC zones, which means smoke and soot can migrate well beyond the room of origin. We handle structural cleaning, odor control, and contents work, then move into reconstruction once the affected materials are documented and approved.

Mold growth shows up most in finished basements and crawlspaces where a slow leak or grading issue went unnoticed, since colonization can begin within 24 to 48 hours of sustained moisture. We assess, contain, and remediate affected materials, then confirm the space is dry before closing out the job.

Storm damage, including wind-driven roof and siding damage from fallen limbs, gets tarped and boarded first to stop ongoing intrusion, with reconstruction scoped and scheduled once the structure is stable.

Potomac's housing stock ranges from mid-century ranch and split-level homes to larger estate-style construction built from the 1980s onward, with basements and walk-out lower levels common across both eras.
