**Smoke damage in your Baldwin home after a fire or nearby wildfire smoke event?** Call now, we answer 24/7. Smoke residue is acidic and keeps etching glass, metal, and painted surfaces the longer it sits, so the clock matters even after the flames are out.

Smoke behaves differently than fire itself, which is why a lot of homeowners underestimate the job. A small kitchen fire can send soot particles through return air vents into rooms that never saw flame. Protein residue from a smoldering fire leaves an almost invisible, greasy film that smells far worse than it looks. Synthetic soot from burning plastics and furniture leaves a thick, sticky black coating that stains porous surfaces within hours. Either way, the odor molecules work their way into drywall, subflooring, and HVAC ductwork long after the visible mess is gone, which is the part that drives repeat calls when it's handled wrong the first time.

## What smoke damage restoration actually involves

Smoke damage restoration is the cleanup and deodorization phase that happens after a fire, whether it was contained to a single appliance or involved a full room. The work covers soot and residue removal from walls, ceilings, trim, and hard surfaces, cleaning or discarding affected contents, and treating the structure itself so the smoke odor doesn't come back once the house warms up or the HVAC kicks on again. For wildfire smoke events, where no flame touched the structure but smoke infiltrated through windows, attic vents, or the HVAC system, the process is similar but usually lighter: surface cleaning, air scrubbing, and duct treatment rather than full soot removal. Equipment typically includes HEPA air scrubbers, dry chemical sponges for dry-soot surfaces, degreasing agents for protein residue, and ozone or hydroxyl generators for odor neutralization. Timeline depends heavily on how much of the structure was affected, light residential jobs can wrap in a few days, while whole-house smoke damage after a structure fire often runs one to two weeks once contents cleaning is factored in.

## Our process

1. **Soot characterization.** We identify whether residue is dry/protein-based, wet/synthetic, or a mix, because the wrong cleaning agent can smear dry soot into permanent staining or re-deposit oily residue.
2. **Source and content triage.** Affected furniture, textiles, and personal items are sorted into clean-in-place, clean off-site, or non-salvageable, documented with photos for your claim.
3. **Surface cleaning and soot removal.** Walls, ceilings, hard flooring, and trim are cleaned with the appropriate chemical or dry-sponge method for the soot type present.
4. **Thermal fogging or ozone/hydroxyl treatment.** Odor-causing particles trapped in porous materials and ductwork are neutralized at the molecular level rather than just masked.
5. **Final air quality pass and HVAC check.** Air scrubbers run until particulate and odor readings are acceptable, and we check whether ductwork needs separate cleaning before the system is used again.

## What separates a good smoke damage response from a bad one

The most common mistake is treating smoke damage like a surface-cleaning job. Spraying an odor masking agent over untreated soot buys a few days before the smell returns, usually once the weather warms or the furnace runs. Another frequent miss is skipping the HVAC system entirely, soot and odor particles that settled in the ductwork during the fire get recirculated through every vent in the house for months afterward. Using an all-purpose cleaner on protein residue instead of a degreaser is another one, it smears the film instead of lifting it, which can force a full repaint later. Insurance adjusters generally want to see documented soot type, a room-by-room scope with before/after photos, and a clear distinction between salvageable and non-salvageable contents, not just a lump-sum invoice. We put that scope in writing before work starts.
