## What water heater flood cleanup actually involves

Water heater failures fall into a few categories: a slow leak from a corroded tank seam that's been dripping for days, a sudden burst that dumps the full tank volume at once, or a supply line or T&P valve failure that keeps feeding water even after the tank itself is empty. Each behaves differently. A slow leak often means water has been sitting long enough to reach subfloor and wall cavities, while a sudden burst is usually cleaner water but a larger volume in a shorter window. Either way, the first job is confirming the water supply to the unit is actually shut off, not just the heater's power, since a continued feed turns a contained spill into an ongoing one. From there the work shifts to pulling standing water, assessing how far it traveled under flooring and behind baseboards, and setting equipment to dry the structure before moisture turns into a secondary mold problem, which can begin in as little as 24 to 48 hours in the right conditions.

## Our process

1. **Shut off and isolate the source.** Confirm the water supply valve is closed and the unit is de-energized before anything else happens, since equipment can't dry a room that's still being fed.
2. **Extract standing water.** Pumps and extraction units remove bulk water from the floor, including water that's pooled under appliances, inside cabinet toe-kicks, or down a floor drain that can't keep up with volume.
3. **Assess hidden moisture.** Moisture meters and thermal imaging check subfloor, drywall, and framing near the heater's location, since water heaters are frequently installed in closets or against walls where leaks travel sideways before they show.
4. **Set containment and drying equipment.** Air movers and dehumidifiers are placed based on the moisture map, with daily readings tracked until materials return to a dry standard, documented per the IICRC S500 structural drying guidelines.
5. **Remove and dispose of unsalvageable materials.** Saturated baseboard, swollen cabinetry, or wet insulation that can't be dried in place is removed and bagged for disposal before reconstruction begins.

## What separates a good water heater flood response from a bad one

The most common mistake is treating the visible puddle as the whole job. Water heaters are usually installed against a wall, in a closet, or on a platform, and water from a burst tank finds the path of least resistance, which often means it's traveled under the subfloor or into an adjoining room before anyone sees it on the surface. A rushed cleanup pulls the standing water, runs a fan for a day, and calls it done, missing moisture trapped under vinyl flooring or inside wall insulation that keeps the area damp for weeks.

A second issue is skipping documentation. Insurance adjusters want to see moisture readings, photos of the affected materials, and a clear record of when drying equipment was placed and removed. Without that paper trail, disputes over what was actually wet, and for how long, become harder to resolve. A thorough response also checks adjacent rooms and the level below if the heater sits on an upper floor, since gravity moves water further than the footprint of the leak itself.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Water heaters in the Baldwin area often sit in basements or garages, spaces that run colder through Maryland winters. Cold inlet water combined with an aging tank accelerates internal corrosion, which is one reason failures cluster in the winter and early spring months. Homes with heaters on an upper floor or in a closet near living space are at higher risk for damage spreading into finished rooms below, since gravity carries the water downward through ceilings and wall cavities.
