## What Storm Damage Restoration actually involves

Storm damage restoration covers the work that happens after wind, hail, heavy rain, or a fallen tree compromises a home's exterior envelope: roofing, siding, windows, or foundation drainage. It's part emergency stabilization and part water mitigation, because once the envelope is breached, rain intrusion behaves like any other water event, soaking insulation, wicking up drywall, and settling into subfloor assemblies. The work typically starts with securing the opening (tarping a roof, boarding a broken window), then shifts to extracting any standing water and drying the structure with air movers and dehumidifiers. Depending on the extent of wind or hail damage, exterior repair scoping for roofing, siding, or gutters often follows the mitigation phase, usually coordinated with your insurance adjuster.

## Our process

1. **Emergency stabilization.** Tarping exposed roof sections, boarding broken windows or doors, and securing loose siding or gutters to stop ongoing water intrusion.
2. **Debris clearance and access.** Clearing fallen branches, shingles, or storm debris that's blocking safe access to the affected area, including coordination with tree removal when a trunk or large limb is involved.
3. **Water extraction and moisture mapping.** Removing standing water from wind-driven rain intrusion and mapping moisture levels in drywall, subfloor, and framing with meters, since storm water often travels further into wall cavities than it first appears.
4. **Structural drying.** Positioning air movers and dehumidifiers based on the moisture map, with daily monitoring until readings return to dry standard, following IICRC S500 drying guidelines.
5. **Repair scope documentation.** Photographing and documenting roof, siding, window, and interior damage to support the exterior repair estimate your adjuster will review.

## What separates a good storm damage response from a bad one

The most common mistake is treating storm damage as a roofing problem only and skipping the interior moisture check. Wind-driven rain can push water several feet past the point of entry, soaking insulation and sheathing that looks fine from inside the attic but is holding moisture that will eventually show up as a stain, or worse, as rot in the framing. A rushed tarp job that doesn't account for wind uplift can also fail within a day or two, letting the next round of rain back in before the real repair starts.

Insurance adjusters reviewing storm claims look for documented timeline: when the tarp or board-up went on, when extraction started, and moisture readings showing the structure actually reached dry standard rather than just "looked dry." Photos of the original damage, debris, and standing water matter as much as the drying logs, because storm claims are frequently disputed on whether damage was pre-existing or wind-caused. A thorough written scope before any repair work begins protects both the timeline and the claim.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Central Maryland sees two distinct storm windows: summer thunderstorm season, when fast-moving cells bring high wind and hail with little warning, and late summer into fall, when the remnants of Atlantic hurricanes and tropical systems push sustained heavy rain through the region for days at a time. Winter nor'easters add a second risk, combining high wind with heavy, wet snow load that stresses older roof structures. Homes in Baldwin and the surrounding area, many with roofs and siding dating back decades, are more prone to wind uplift at aging flashing points, which is often where storm water finds its way in.
