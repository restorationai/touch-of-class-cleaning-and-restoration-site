Floodwater is not the same as a supply line leak. It carries silt, runoff, and whatever the ground has been sitting in, which means it's classified as Category 3 water from the moment it crosses your threshold. That changes almost everything about the response: what gets saved, what gets removed, and how fast the clock is running before porous materials start breaking down.

## What flood damage restoration actually involves

Flood damage restoration starts with stopping the water's path and pulling standing water out with truck-mounted or submersible extraction equipment, not shop vacuums. From there the work shifts to identifying what absorbed contaminated water and needs to come out: baseboards, lower drywall, wet insulation, carpet pad, and sometimes subfloor. Because floodwater is considered grossly contaminated, the IICRC S500 standard treats affected porous materials differently than it would a clean-water loss. Drying equipment (air movers, low-grain refrigerant dehumidifiers) goes in only after the contaminated materials are handled, and moisture readings in studs, subfloor, and slab are tracked daily until they're back to a dry standard, which typically runs three to seven days depending on how much the structure absorbed and how long the water sat before the call came in.

## Our process

1. **Water categorization and safety check.** We confirm the source (storm surge, overland flooding, sewer backup, groundwater intrusion) and treat it as Category 3 unless the source is clearly clean, since misclassifying floodwater is one of the most common mistakes in this industry.
2. **Extraction and contaminated material removal.** Standing water comes out first, followed by removal of flood-contact materials that can't be sanitized, flooring, drywall up to the water line, insulation, and anything porous that stayed wet past the point of safe drying.
3. **Antimicrobial application and containment.** Remaining structural surfaces get treated to address bacteria and contaminants left behind by the floodwater, and we contain the work area to keep that contamination from spreading to unaffected rooms.
4. **Structural drying and daily moisture monitoring.** Air movers and dehumidifiers run against documented moisture targets in framing, subfloor, and any salvageable materials, with readings logged until the structure meets a dry standard.
5. **Documentation handoff.** Photos, moisture logs, and material removal records go into a report built for your adjuster, since flood claims get scrutinized more closely than a typical water loss.

## What separates a good flood damage response from a bad one

The most common mistake is drying instead of removing. Operators trying to save on demolition will run fans over contaminated drywall or carpet pad instead of pulling it, which can mask surface moisture while bacteria and mold colonize the material underneath, usually within 24 to 48 hours in Maryland's humidity. A second mistake is skipping moisture mapping below the surface. Flooring and plaster can read dry on top while the subfloor or lath stays saturated, which shows up later as cupping, odor, or a mold callback. Adjusters reviewing flood claims look specifically for documented moisture readings over time, photos of removed materials before disposal, and a clear line between what was Category 3 contact and what wasn't, so incomplete documentation is one of the fastest ways a claim gets delayed or denied.

Most standard homeowners policies cover water damage from a burst pipe or internal source, but flooding from external sources like storm surge, rising groundwater, or overland flow is typically only covered under a separate flood insurance policy, so it's worth checking which policy applies before assuming coverage.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Baldwin and the surrounding Baltimore County area see two flood patterns worth knowing. Late winter and early spring bring snowmelt combined with rain on still-frozen ground, which overwhelms drainage and backs water into basements built into the area's clay-heavy soil. Late summer and early fall bring the remnants of tropical systems moving up from the Chesapeake, dropping heavy rain in short windows that older storm drains and grading weren't built to handle. Homes on lower lots or near creeks and tributaries in this region tend to see repeat flooding, which makes fast extraction even more important to limit cumulative structural damage.
