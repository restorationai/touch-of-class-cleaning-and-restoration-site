When a loss crosses a certain threshold, whether that's a burst main soaking three floors of an apartment building, a fire that guts a commercial kitchen and smoke-damages the units above it, or a storm that floods a warehouse full of inventory, the response can't look like a standard residential job scaled up. Large loss and catastrophic response calls for multiple crews working in phases, direct coordination with adjusters and engineers, and a scope of work that's documented from day one because the dollar amounts and the complexity demand it. RestoPros of Central Maryland handles these losses with the same WRT-certified drying protocols used on smaller jobs, applied at a scale that keeps a property, a business, or a building full of tenants moving toward reoccupancy instead of sitting idle.

## What large loss and catastrophic response actually involves

A large loss is generally defined by the restoration industry as a claim in the six-figure range or higher, or a loss affecting multiple units, floors, or structures at once. Think apartment complexes, HOAs, retail centers, warehouses, schools, and multi-tenant office buildings. The work itself isn't fundamentally different from residential mitigation, extraction, structural drying, demolition where materials can't be saved, but the logistics change everything. You're managing dozens or hundreds of moisture readings instead of a handful, coordinating equipment placement around tenants who may still be operating parts of the building, and producing documentation detailed enough to support a claim that an adjuster, a public adjuster, and possibly an engineer will all review. Timelines stretch from days into weeks or months depending on the scope, and the drying plan has to account for different materials, different categories of water, and different occupancy schedules all under one roof.

## Our process

1. **Rapid scoping and mobilization.** On arrival, the priority is understanding the full footprint of the loss, how many units or areas are affected, what's structural versus cosmetic, and what needs to happen first to stop the damage from spreading.
2. **Phased mitigation across the structure.** Crews extract standing water and begin structural drying in the worst-affected areas first, while less critical zones are monitored and brought into the drying plan on a rolling schedule.
3. **Coordination with adjusters, engineers, and property management.** Large losses almost always involve more than one decision-maker. Scope changes, material selections, and access schedules get communicated in writing so nobody is working off outdated information.
4. **Documentation built for claim review.** Moisture logs, psychrometric readings, photos, and line-item scope are recorded continuously, not reconstructed after the fact, because large claims get scrutinized more closely than a single-room loss.
5. **Transition to reconstruction or closeout.** Once drying is verified and the structure is cleared, the project moves into repair scope, punch list, and final sign-off with the parties who need to approve it.

## What separates a good large loss response from a bad one

The most common failure point on catastrophic losses is undersizing the initial response, sending a crew built for a residential job into a commercial-scale loss, which means drying equipment gets stretched too thin and hidden pockets of moisture get missed in wall cavities, subfloor assemblies, or HVAC returns. Another is poor documentation discipline: adjusters on large claims expect granular moisture logs and dated photos tied to specific rooms or units, not a single summary report at the end. Good operators also flag structural and electrical hazards early and loop in engineers or other trades before they become change orders nobody saw coming. And because large losses often involve multiple stakeholders, a response that doesn't communicate scope changes in writing tends to end up in disputes over what was authorized and what wasn't.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Central Maryland's older commercial buildings and multi-unit properties, many built with brick, block, and plaster construction before modern vapor barriers were standard, hold moisture differently than newer builds, which affects drying timelines on large losses. Winter freeze events can split pipes in unheated mechanical rooms or vacant units across an entire building at once, turning a single-point failure into a multi-floor loss overnight. Summer humidity raises the stakes on any large-scale water event because untreated moisture in commercial HVAC systems or large unoccupied floor plates can support mold growth within 24 to 48 hours, and remnants of tropical systems moving up the East Coast occasionally bring flash flooding that affects basements and below-grade commercial space across the region.
