## Why Potomac Properties See Water Damage Issues

Much of Potomac sits on sloped, heavily wooded lots carved into the hills above the Potomac River valley, and a lot of the housing stock from the 1970s through today was built with walkout or daylight basements that put at least one foundation wall below grade. That design works well for views and natural light, but it also means hydrostatic pressure from saturated clay soil can push groundwater through foundation cracks and cove joints during heavy spring rain or rapid snowmelt. Mature tree root systems, common on these larger lots, are another frequent culprit: roots work their way into aging clay or cast-iron sewer laterals and cause slow backups that homeowners often mistake for a plumbing fixture problem rather than a line failure. In parts of unincorporated Potomac still served by private wells and septic systems, a saturated drain field or a failed sump pump can turn a routine storm into standing water in a finished lower level within hours.

## Our Water Damage Restoration Process in Potomac

We start with a moisture assessment using meters and thermal imaging to map how far water has traveled behind drywall, under engineered hardwood, and into subfloor layers, because surface-dry materials in these hillside basements often hide saturation in the framing below. Extraction comes next, pulling standing water with truck-mounted or portable pumps depending on access and the depth of the loss. From there we set air movers and dehumidifiers calibrated to the room's size and material mix, and we monitor drying progress daily against the IICRC S500 structural drying standard rather than guessing when a space is dry. Our technicians carry IICRC Water Damage Restoration Technician (WRT) training, which matters most in these mixed-material Potomac homes where plaster, newer drywall, hardwood, and finished basement carpet pads all dry at different rates under the same conditions.
