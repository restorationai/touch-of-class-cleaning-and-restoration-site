## Why Dundalk Properties See Distinct Fire Damage Patterns

Much of Dundalk's housing stock dates to the early and mid-20th century, built up around the Bethlehem Steel works when the community grew fast and dense. Plaster-and-lath walls, older wiring, and rowhouse construction with shared party walls are still common here. That matters after a fire: plaster absorbs and holds soot and smoke odor differently than modern drywall, and in attached or semi-attached rowhomes, smoke can migrate into a neighboring unit through shared attic space or wall cavities even when the fire itself stayed contained next door. Baltimore County's humid summers and damp winters also mean that any water used to suppress the fire needs to come out fast, because moisture sitting in century-old plaster or subflooring invites secondary mold growth within days, not weeks.

## Our Fire Damage Restoration Process in Dundalk

The first visit is about stopping damage from spreading, not just assessing what already happened. We board up broken windows and tarp roof openings so wind and rain off the bay don't finish what the fire started, then move into extraction wherever firefighting water has pooled. Our technicians hold IICRC water restoration training, which comes into play on nearly every fire job because suppression water is almost always part of the cleanup. From there we separate what can be cleaned, deodorized, and restored from what needs to be removed: charred framing, soot-saturated insulation, and plaster that's absorbed smoke odor past the point of practical cleaning. Structural fire damage in older Dundalk homes often means opening up a wall cavity we expected to leave closed, simply because that's where the smoke settled.
