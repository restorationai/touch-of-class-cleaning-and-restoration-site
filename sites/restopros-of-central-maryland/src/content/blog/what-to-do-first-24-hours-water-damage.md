If you're standing in water right now, here's the order of operations: shut off the water source, cut power to the affected area if it's safe to reach the breaker, move anything you can lift off the floor, and start documenting with your phone before you touch anything else. Everything else, calling your insurer, deciding whether to rent fans or call a restoration crew, can wait until those four things are done. Water damage gets worse by the hour, not the day, so the choices you make in the first 24 hours matter more than almost anything that happens afterward.

## Understand What You're Dealing With

Not all water damage behaves the same way, and that affects how urgent your response needs to be. A supply line leak or burst pipe is usually clean water, at least at first, but it can soak through subfloor and into joists faster than it looks on the surface. A backed-up drain or appliance overflow carries contaminants and needs to be treated more cautiously. A sewage backup or floodwater intrusion is a different category entirely and isn't a DIY cleanup under any circumstances.

In Baldwin and the surrounding Central Maryland area, a lot of the housing stock dates back several decades, which means plaster-and-lath walls, older cast iron and galvanized plumbing, and crawl space foundations that don't always drain well. Water that gets into a crawl space here can sit against joists for days before anyone notices a smell, especially in the humid stretch of a Maryland summer when everything dries slower. If your home has a crawl space or an unfinished basement, check both, not just the room where you first noticed the problem.

## The First Steps (In Order)

1. **Stop the source.** Shut off the main water valve if you can't isolate the leak to a single fixture. For a washing machine hose or dishwasher line, the fixture's local shutoff is usually enough.
2. **Cut power to the area if there's standing water anywhere near outlets or appliances.** Don't wade into standing water to reach a breaker, call an electrician or your utility if the panel isn't safely accessible.
3. **Photograph everything before you move anything.** Wide shots of the room, close-ups of water lines on walls, soaked carpet, and any damaged belongings. This is the documentation your insurance adjuster will ask for later.
4. **Remove standing water you can safely reach** with towels, a wet/dry shop vac, or a mop. Every gallon you remove now is less moisture working its way into subfloor and framing.
5. **Move furniture, rugs, and electronics off wet flooring.** Use aluminum foil or small wood blocks under furniture legs if you can't move pieces entirely off the wet area.
6. **Start air movement.** Open windows if the humidity outside is lower than inside, run box fans, and set up a dehumidifier if you have one. Don't rely on this alone for anything beyond a very small, contained spill.
7. **Call your insurance company** to open a claim and ask what documentation they require before repairs begin.

## What Not To Do

- Don't use a household vacuum (non-wet-rated) on standing water. It's a shock and fire hazard.
- Don't run a ceiling fan if water has reached the fixture or the attic above it.
- Don't assume carpet padding is salvageable just because the carpet on top feels dry. Padding holds moisture and loses structural integrity fast.
- Don't paint, patch, or seal drywall over a wet area to "get ahead of it." Trapped moisture behind a fresh coat of paint is how hidden mold problems start.
- Don't wait to see if it dries on its own before calling your insurer. Most policies require prompt notice, and delayed reporting can complicate a claim.
- Don't enter a room with sagging ceiling drywall or a ceiling that's actively dripping. That's a collapse risk, not a cleanup job.

## When to Call a Professional

A wet spot from an overflowing sink that you caught within minutes is often something you can manage with a shop vac and some fans. A few signs tell you it's past that point:

- Water has been sitting for more than a few hours, or you don't know how long it's been there.
- The water came from a toilet, drain backup, or any source other than a clean supply line.
- Water has reached flooring, baseboards, or drywall in more than one room.
- You can hear or feel water under laminate or hardwood flooring when you walk on it.
- There's a musty smell, which can show up within a day or two if moisture has reached wall cavities or subfloor.
- The affected area includes a finished basement, where moisture often travels further than it appears.

## What Happens After the First 24 Hours

Once the water source is stopped and standing water is removed, the next phase is drying, and it's slower than most homeowners expect. Materials like drywall, subfloor, and framing lumber absorb water and release it gradually, which is why professional drying follows a monitored schedule (often referred to under the IICRC S500 standard) rather than a fixed number of days. Expect moisture readings to be taken daily until materials return to a normal range, not just until the surface feels dry to the touch.

Mold is the other clock running in the background. Under the right conditions, warm, humid, and with a food source like drywall paper or wood, mold spores can begin colonizing within 24 to 48 hours of a water event. That's a big part of why the steps above matter: every hour water sits untreated is an hour closer to a problem that goes from "wipe it down" to "mold remediation."

Once everything is confirmed dry, the rebuild phase starts, replacing drywall, flooring, baseboards, or cabinetry that didn't survive. Depending on the extent of the damage, this can range from a weekend patch job to several weeks of work.
