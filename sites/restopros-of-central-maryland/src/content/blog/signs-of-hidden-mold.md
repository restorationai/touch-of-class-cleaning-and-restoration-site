If you're smelling something musty in a room that looks perfectly clean, or you've noticed a water stain that keeps coming back no matter how many times you paint over it, there's a good chance mold is growing somewhere you can't see. The seven signs below are the most common tip-offs: a persistent musty odor, discoloration on walls or ceilings, peeling or bubbling paint, warped or soft flooring, condensation on windows or pipes, a spike in allergy symptoms at home, and a past leak or flood that was dried quickly but not thoroughly. If two or more of these sound familiar, the next step is to find the source before it spreads further, not just cover the symptom.

## The 7 Signs, and Why Each One Matters

**A musty, earthy smell that doesn't go away.** Mold produces microbial volatile organic compounds (MVOCs) as it digests organic material like drywall paper or wood. You'll often smell it before you see it, especially in closets, basements, or behind furniture pushed against an exterior wall.

**Discoloration that looks like a shadow or a water stain.** Mold colonies show up as black, green, gray, or sometimes orange speckling. On drywall, it often looks like a faint shadow at first, which is easy to dismiss as dirt.

**Paint or wallpaper that's bubbling, cracking, or peeling.** Moisture trapped behind a finished surface pushes paint away from the wall as it evaporates and condenses. If this is happening in a spot with no obvious plumbing nearby, the moisture source may be a roof leak or condensation inside the wall cavity.

**Warped, soft, or spongy flooring.** Hardwood that cups, laminate that bubbles at the seams, or carpet that stays damp longer than it should all point to moisture sitting underneath, which is exactly where mold prefers to grow, out of sight.

**Condensation that keeps forming on windows, pipes, or exterior walls.** Baldwin's humid Mid-Atlantic summers push a lot of moisture into homes, and older houses in this part of Central Maryland often have less wall insulation and ventilation than current code calls for. Chronic condensation is a slow, steady water source that mold doesn't need much time to use.

**Allergy or respiratory symptoms that ease when you leave the house.** Sneezing, congestion, itchy eyes, or a cough that improves at work or on vacation and returns at home can be your body reacting to airborne mold spores, particularly in homes with forced-air HVAC systems that circulate them room to room.

**A past leak, overflow, or flood that was mopped up but never fully dried.** Mold doesn't need a dramatic flood, it just needs sustained moisture. A slow slab leak, a washing machine overflow, or a roof leak during a storm can leave hidden dampness in subfloor or wall cavities that doesn't surface for weeks.

## Immediate Steps to Take

1. **Find and stop the moisture source.** Check under sinks, around toilets, at the base of water heaters, and along exterior walls near downspouts. If you can't find it yourself, that's often the first sign you need a moisture inspection rather than more guessing.
2. **Run a dehumidifier in the affected area** and keep the room's relative humidity below 50 percent if you can measure it with a cheap hygrometer.
3. **Photograph everything** before you touch or move anything. Stains, discoloration, warped materials, and the surrounding area all matter if you file an insurance claim later.
4. **Isolate the space** by closing doors and, if the smell is strong, covering vents in that room to limit spores moving through the HVAC system.
5. **Avoid using fans to blow air across visible mold.** Air movement helps dry a wet material, but across an active mold colony it scatters spores into the rest of the house.

## What Not To Do

- Don't paint over discoloration or stains before the underlying moisture is resolved. It will come back, usually bigger.
- Don't rip into drywall or pull up flooring yourself if the affected area is larger than roughly a few square feet. Disturbing a colony without containment can push spores into living spaces you haven't tested yet.
- Don't rely on bleach on porous materials like drywall, subfloor, or insulation. Bleach can kill surface mold on non-porous surfaces like tile or glass, but it doesn't reach the roots growing inside porous material, and it adds moisture that can make things worse.
- Don't assume a small visible patch means a small hidden problem. Surface mold is often just the part that made it to a painted or papered surface; what's inside the wall cavity can be considerably more extensive.

## When to Call a Professional

A few situations call for more than a DIY response:

- The affected area is larger than about 10 square feet.
- Mold keeps returning after you've cleaned it.
- You can smell it but can't find it.
- Anyone in the household has asthma, a compromised immune system, or chronic respiratory issues.
- The mold followed a known water event, a leak, overflow, or flooding, that wasn't fully dried out within 24 to 48 hours.
