Restoration work moves fast and homeowners are often making decisions while exhausted, displaced, or dealing with an adjuster for the first time. That combination is exactly what less scrupulous outfits count on.

## Why This Decision Gets Rushed, and Why That's the Problem

Water, fire, and mold losses don't wait for you to do research. A pipe bursts at 11 p.m., a kitchen fire fills the house with smoke, or you finally pull up the carpet and find black staining along the tack strip. In Central Maryland, heavy spring storms and the occasional ice-damming thaw mean a lot of homeowners are making this call for the first time, under pressure, often with an insurance adjuster asking for a vendor name within hours.

That urgency is also the opening that out-of-town "storm chasing" crews exploit. They show up after regional weather events, write an estimate padded with unnecessary equipment and line items, get a signature, and are gone before the homeowner realizes the invoice doesn't match the work performed. The fix isn't to slow everything down. It's to know which few questions actually separate a legitimate company from a problem before you sign anything.

## The Checklist: What to Verify Before Any Work Starts

1. **Ask for proof of insurance**, not just a license number. A reputable company will give you their general liability and workers' comp coverage without hesitation. If a worker is hurt on your property and the company isn't covered, that liability can fall back on you.
2. **Ask who will actually be on-site.** Some companies subcontract the labor to whoever is available that week. That's not automatically bad, but you should know it's happening and who is responsible for the work.
3. **Get the scope of work in writing before demolition.** For water damage restoration, that means documented moisture readings and photos of affected materials before anything gets torn out. For fire damage restoration, it means an itemized list of what's being cleaned versus replaced. Verbal promises don't help you later if the invoice looks different.
4. **Ask how they handle insurance billing.** A company experienced with claims will explain how they document the loss for your adjuster and whether they bill the carrier directly or expect you to pay and get reimbursed.
5. **Read the authorization form line by line.** Some forms are a simple agreement to perform listed work. Others are an open-ended assignment of your entire insurance benefits. Know which one you're signing.

## What Not to Do

- **Don't let anyone start tearing out materials before damage is documented.** Insurance adjusters rely on moisture maps, photos, and readings to approve the claim. Once drywall or flooring is in a dumpster, that evidence is gone.
- **Don't sign a contract with a blank dollar amount** or one that says "time and materials, no cap." Ask for a not-to-exceed figure or a clear per-day equipment rate instead.
- **Don't assume the cheapest bid is the safest one.** Underbidding is sometimes a sign a company plans to cut drying time short or skip remediation steps that don't show up until mold returns in a few weeks.
- **Don't ignore a smell that's gone but a stain that isn't**, or the reverse. With mold remediation in particular, visible staining and musty odor don't always track together, and a company that only addresses what's visible may be missing contaminated material behind a wall or under flooring.

## When to Call a Restoration Company vs. a Handyman or General Contractor

A handyman or general GC is the right call for cosmetic repairs: repainting, replacing trim, patching a small hole. Restoration work is different because it starts with diagnosis, not repair. If water got behind a wall, under a subfloor, or into insulation, the first job is figuring out how far it traveled and whether it's actually dry, not just surface-dry. That typically means moisture meters, sometimes thermal imaging, and drying equipment placed and monitored over several days, following the IICRC S500 standard for water damage.

Fire and smoke damage follows a similar logic. Soot residue behaves differently depending on what burned and how hot the fire got. Protein residue from a kitchen fire smells and cleans differently than wood smoke in a structure fire, and using the wrong cleaning agent can set a stain rather than lift it. If you're dealing with lingering odor, visible soot on surfaces beyond the room of origin, or any fire involving synthetic materials, that's a job for a company set up to test and treat before repainting over the problem.

Mold is its own category. A small, contained spot on a bathroom ceiling tile is often a cleaning job. Mold that's spread across more than a few square feet, that followed a water event, or that keeps returning after you've wiped it down is a sign the moisture source hasn't actually been addressed. That's when it's worth bringing in a mold remediation company rather than continuing to treat the surface.

## What the Recovery Process Actually Looks Like

Once a legitimate company is on-site, the sequence is fairly consistent regardless of the type of loss. First comes assessment and documentation: moisture readings, photos, and a scope of work, usually the same day. Next is mitigation, stopping the damage from spreading further: water extraction, air movers and dehumidifiers, or removing soot-contaminated materials that can't be salvaged. Drying typically runs several days with daily monitoring, not a single visit. Only after materials test dry (or soot and odor are addressed) does reconstruction begin: drywall, flooring, trim, paint.

Homeowners sometimes expect the whole process to wrap in a day or two. Mitigation can move fast. Full drying and reconstruction, especially for a multi-room loss, usually takes longer, and a company that promises an unrealistically short timeline upfront is worth a second look.
