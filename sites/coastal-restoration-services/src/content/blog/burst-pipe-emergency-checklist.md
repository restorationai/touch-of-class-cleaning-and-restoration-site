A burst pipe can dump hundreds of gallons of water into your home in under an hour. The first thing to do is **shut off the main water supply valve** — not after you've grabbed towels, not after you've called your spouse. Right now. Once the water stops flowing, you have a window to limit the damage before it soaks into subfloor, insulation, and wall cavities. This checklist walks you through every step in order, from the moment you hear the rush of water to the point where a professional takes over the drying process.

---

## Step 1 — Stop the Water Immediately

Your main shutoff valve is usually in one of three places: a utility closet near the water heater, under the kitchen sink, or on an exterior wall facing the street. In Vandenberg Village and the surrounding Santa Barbara County area, many homes have the shutoff in a ground-level box near the curb — look for a rectangular metal lid stamped "WATER." Turn the valve clockwise until it stops.

Once the main is off:

1. Open a cold-water faucet on the lowest floor of the house to drain pressure from the lines.
2. Turn off your water heater at the breaker or gas valve — running a water heater dry can damage the element.
3. If water is near any electrical panel, outlets, or appliances, **do not enter that area**. Flip the breaker for that zone at the main panel first, or stay out entirely until an electrician clears it.

If you can see the broken section of pipe and it's a simple split, you can wrap it tightly with a pipe repair clamp or even duct tape as a temporary measure — but only after the main is off. This buys time; it is not a repair.

---

## Step 2 — Document Everything Before You Clean Up

Before you move a single wet item, take photos and video. Walk every affected room. Capture:

- Standing water depth (a ruler or tape measure in the frame helps)
- Waterlines on walls and baseboards
- Wet flooring, cabinetry, and personal property
- The pipe itself and the point of failure

This documentation is the foundation of your insurance claim. Adjusters work from evidence, and photos taken after cleanup are worth far less than photos taken during the loss. Timestamp is automatic on most phones, but double-check that your camera's date and time are set correctly.

Call your homeowner's insurance carrier next — most policies have a reporting window, and some carriers want to send their own adjuster before major work begins. Get a claim number before anyone starts tearing out drywall.

---

## Step 3 — Begin Water Removal (and What NOT to Do)

Once you've documented and notified your insurer, start removing standing water. Use a wet/dry shop vac for smaller volumes. For anything more than an inch or two across a large area, a submersible pump moves water faster.

**Do not use a standard household vacuum.** It will be destroyed and creates an electrocution risk.

Move wet furniture, rugs, and belongings out of the affected space and into a dry area or the garage. Peel back area rugs — leaving them on hardwood or laminate accelerates warping. If you have hardwood floors, place aluminum foil under furniture legs to prevent tannin stains.

Open windows and interior doors to increase airflow, but be thoughtful about outdoor humidity. Santa Barbara County's coastal climate means outdoor air in the morning can run above 80% relative humidity, especially in winter. On a foggy morning, opening windows may actually slow drying. A box fan pointed outward in one window and a second window cracked open on the opposite side of the room is more effective than simply opening everything.

**What not to do:**

- Do not use a standard hair dryer or space heater aimed at wet walls — you'll dry the surface while moisture stays trapped inside the cavity, which is exactly the condition that leads to mold growth within 24–48 hours.
- Do not remove wet drywall without checking for asbestos first. Homes built before 1980 in California may have asbestos-containing joint compound or texture. Disturbing it without testing is both a health risk and a code violation.
- Do not assume the floor is dry because it feels dry to the touch. Moisture meters consistently find elevated readings in subfloor and underlayment long after the surface seems fine.

---

## Step 4 — Recognize When This Is Beyond DIY

Some burst pipe situations are manageable with a shop vac and a few fans. Others are not. Call a water damage restoration professional if any of the following are true:

- **Water has been sitting for more than a few hours.** Saturation time matters enormously. Drywall that has been wet for six hours has absorbed significantly more moisture into the paper face than drywall wet for thirty minutes. The longer the window, the more likely demolition becomes necessary.
- **The leak is behind a wall or under the floor.** If you're seeing a bubbling paint blister, a soft spot in the floor, or a ceiling stain that's growing, the water source is hidden. Thermal imaging and moisture meters locate the extent of the damage without unnecessary demolition.
- **The affected area is more than one room.** Water follows the path of least resistance — it moves under flooring, through wall plates, and down into lower floors. A burst pipe in a second-floor bathroom can show up in the first-floor ceiling, the wall cavity between floors, and the subfloor all at once.
- **You smell anything musty within 24–48 hours.** That earthy, basement-like smell is microbial growth starting. At that point, remediation — not just drying — is the appropriate response.

## Step 5 — The Recovery Process (What Comes After Drying)

Professional water damage restoration follows a defined sequence. Understanding it helps you ask the right questions and set realistic expectations.

1. **Extraction** — Industrial extractors remove water from carpet, pad, and hard flooring far more completely than any shop vac.
2. **Structural drying** — High-capacity dehumidifiers and air movers run continuously, typically for three to five days. Technicians take daily moisture readings to verify progress.
3. **Monitoring** — Readings are logged at each visit. Drying is not complete until materials reach their pre-loss moisture content, not just until they feel dry.
4. **Demolition (if needed)** — Wet drywall, insulation, and flooring that cannot be dried in place are removed. This is called a "flood cut" when it involves cutting drywall to a horizontal line above the waterline.
5. **Reconstruction** — New drywall, insulation, flooring, and paint return the space to its pre-loss condition.

The full timeline from a burst pipe to a finished, painted room typically runs two to four weeks depending on the extent of saturation and material lead times. The drying phase itself is usually complete within a week.
