## What renovations, remodels and general contracting actually involves

This is the work that happens after the demo is done and the design is settled, or after a restoration crew has dried out, cleaned, or stripped a space back to studs and subfloor. It covers kitchen and bathroom remodels, room additions, interior reconfiguration, and full post-damage rebuilds where fire, water, or mold remediation left a home needing new drywall, flooring, cabinetry, and trim. The scope ranges from a single-room refresh to coordinating every trade on a house that lost a roof or a kitchen wall. Timelines vary with permit review, material lead times, and how many trades have to sequence through the space: a bathroom remodel might run three to five weeks, while a post-fire rebuild involving structural framing, electrical, and finishes can take two to four months.

## Our process

1. **Scope of work.** We walk the space, document existing conditions, and put the project in writing: what's being removed, what's being built, what finishes and fixtures are included. For post-damage rebuilds, this scope is reconciled against the restoration report and, where applicable, the insurance estimate.
2. **Permits and plan review.** Anything touching structural framing, electrical panels, plumbing relocation, or load-bearing walls goes through the city or county building department before work starts. We handle the submittal and inspection scheduling so it isn't sitting on the homeowner's desk.
3. **Framing and rough-in.** Studs, headers, and subfloor go in first, followed by electrical, plumbing, and HVAC rough-in behind the walls. This is also where any remaining moisture or smoke-affected framing from a prior loss gets addressed before it's closed in.
4. **Finishes.** Drywall, flooring, cabinetry, tile, paint, and fixtures go in roughly in that order, with each trade signing off before the next starts.
5. **Final walkthrough and punch list.** We go through the finished space together, note anything that needs adjustment, and close out permits with final inspection.

## What separates a good renovation response from a bad one

The most common failure point isn't the visible finish work, it's what happens before the walls close. A contractor in a hurry will drywall over framing that's still reading elevated moisture content, or skip the inspection that's supposed to happen before insulation goes in. On a post-fire or post-water rebuild, adjusters are specifically looking for documentation that framing was dried or replaced to spec, not just painted over to hide soot or water staining. Mismatched load paths on a reframed wall, missing permits that surface at resale, and subcontractors who aren't sequenced correctly (electrical rough-in happening after drywall, for example) are the issues that turn a six-week remodel into a four-month one. A properly run job documents moisture readings, permit sign-offs, and material specs at every stage, not just at the end.

## What does renovations, remodels and general contracting cost?

Every renovation or rebuild is scoped individually based on square footage, finish level, and whether structural or mechanical work is involved, and Coastal Restoration Services Inc provides a written scope of work before any work begins. Homeowners insurance typically covers the rebuild portion of a loss (framing, drywall, flooring that matches pre-loss condition) but not upgrades or discretionary finish changes beyond that scope, so renovation work layered on top of a covered rebuild is usually billed separately. ## Seasonal & regional considerations

Santa Maria's building stock runs from older downtown homes with original wood framing to mid-century ranch houses and newer stucco tract construction in Orcutt and the surrounding valley, each with different framing and moisture behavior once a wall is opened up. Winter atmospheric river storms are the most common trigger for rebuild work tied to roof or window intrusion, and scheduling exterior-facing work (roofing, siding, stucco patching) around that wet season matters for both timeline and drying time of new materials. Coastal fog and humidity year-round also mean framing lumber and drywall should sit and acclimate before finishes go on, rather than being closed in too fast.
