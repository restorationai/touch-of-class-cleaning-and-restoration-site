## What commercial restoration actually involves

Commercial restoration covers water damage, fire and smoke damage, and the reconstruction that follows, but the scale and constraints are different from a residential job. A single-family water loss might mean drying one or two rooms. A commercial loss in an office suite, retail storefront, or multi-tenant building often means coordinating around business hours, tenant leases, shared HVAC systems, and code requirements that don't apply to a house. Equipment is sized up: commercial dehumidifiers and air movers run in larger counts over longer stretches because commercial buildings have more square footage, higher ceilings, and often slab-on-grade construction that holds moisture differently than a residential crawlspace or basement. Fire and smoke jobs in commercial spaces also have to account for what's inside: inventory, point-of-sale equipment, server racks, paper records, none of which respond to cleaning the way household contents do.

## Our process

1. **Loss assessment and scope documentation.** We walk the affected space with moisture meters, thermal imaging, and photo documentation to establish what's wet, what's charred, and what's salvageable before any equipment goes in. This scope becomes the basis for the insurance claim and the written work order.
2. **Coordination with property management and tenants.** On multi-tenant properties we work out a sequence that isolates the damaged unit, protects adjacent tenants from odor and moisture migration through shared walls or return air, and sets realistic reopening windows for each affected space.
3. **Extraction, drying, or soot and odor remediation.** Water losses get extracted and dried per IICRC S500 structural drying standards, with daily moisture monitoring logged for the adjuster. Fire losses get soot characterized by type before cleaning begins, since protein residue from a kitchen fire and synthetic soot from burned plastics and electronics respond to different cleaning agents.
4. **Contents and specialty item handling.** Electronics, inventory, and paper records are inventoried and either cleaned on-site, sent to a specialty vendor, or documented as a loss, depending on condition and client priority.
5. **Reconstruction and code compliance.** Drywall, flooring, casework, and finishes are rebuilt to match commercial-grade materials and current code, including ADA clearances and fire-rated assembly requirements where they apply.

## What separates a good commercial restoration response from a bad one

The mistakes that cost commercial property owners the most money are almost always about what gets missed, not what gets done poorly. A crew that dries the visible drywall but doesn't check behind built-in casework or under raised flooring leaves hidden moisture that surfaces as mold weeks later. On fire jobs, soot particulate pulled into a shared HVAC return can contaminate suites that never saw flame, and that cross-contamination is easy to miss if the return system isn't inspected and isolated early. Adjusters reviewing a commercial claim look for daily moisture logs, clear photo documentation tied to specific rooms or suites, and an itemized scope that separates structural repair from contents loss and business interruption. A response built around those records moves through claims review faster than one built on memory and a final invoice.

## What does commercial restoration cost?

Commercial restoration costs vary widely based on the size of the space, the category of water or type of fire damage, and whether reconstruction is needed. ## Seasonal & regional considerations

Santa Maria's winter storm season brings atmospheric river events that stress flat commercial roofing systems, and ponding water on low-slope roofs is a common source of ceiling and tenant improvement damage in the strip malls and office parks along Broadway and Main. The coastal marine layer keeps ambient humidity elevated much of the year, which slows natural drying in commercial spaces with limited ventilation and makes mechanical dehumidification more important than in drier inland climates. Wildfire smoke from regional fire activity can also infiltrate commercial HVAC systems even when the structure itself is untouched by flame, leaving a smoke odor that needs to be addressed at the duct and return level, not just inside the occupied space.
