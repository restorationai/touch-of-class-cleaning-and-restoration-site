Salt air off the Pacific doesn't just corrode patio furniture — it accelerates the deterioration of exposed wood, insulation, and structural framing within hours of a storm breach or fire. When a window shatters during a coastal wind event or a fire tears through a roof along the Pismo Beach shoreline, the window between the initial damage and secondary destruction is measured in tides, not days. Getting plywood over openings and poly tarps over compromised roofing before the next marine layer rolls in is the difference between a contained repair and a gut renovation.

## Why Pismo Beach Properties Are Especially Vulnerable After a Damage Event

Pismo Beach sits at the intersection of two forces that make open-structure damage uniquely costly: persistent coastal humidity and a housing stock that includes a significant number of older wood-frame vacation cottages and bungalows built in the mid-20th century. These homes were designed for charm, not resilience — their original single-pane windows and aging roof sheathing offer little resistance once a seal is broken.

The marine environment here means that untreated lumber exposed to the ocean air can begin showing moisture intrusion and surface mold colonization in as little as 24 to 48 hours during the wetter months, roughly November through March. A roof opening that might be tolerable for a few days in a drier inland climate becomes a serious secondary damage risk by the following morning in Pismo Beach. The combination of salt, humidity, and the region's occasional heavy rain events — fed by atmospheric rivers that funnel moisture directly up the Central Coast — makes prompt board-up and tarping not a precaution but a necessity.

Fire damage compounds this further. After a structure fire, the charred framing absorbs ambient moisture rapidly. Leaving a fire-damaged home open to the coastal air accelerates wood decay, invites pest intrusion, and can compromise structural members that might otherwise be salvageable.

## Our Board-Up and Tarping Process, Calibrated for Coastal Conditions

Not all board-up work is the same. A standard 3/4-inch plywood panel fastened to interior framing works adequately in a dry climate. Along the Pismo Beach coast, we use exterior-grade plywood treated to resist moisture absorption, and we seal the perimeter of every panel with weatherproof tape to prevent salt-laden air from wicking behind the board and into the wall cavity.

For roof tarping, we use 6-mil reinforced poly tarps secured with lumber battens and screwed into the decking — not just weighted down. Wind gusts along this stretch of the coast can exceed 40 mph during storm events, and a tarp that lifts at 2 a.m. provides no protection. We also inspect the surrounding roofing material before we set the tarp, because a compromised area adjacent to the primary damage point is often the source of the next leak.

Broken window board-up on older Pismo Beach homes requires attention to the window framing itself. Many mid-century cottages have wood frames that have been repainted dozens of times; driving fasteners without pre-drilling can split the frame and create new gaps. Our crews pre-drill every anchor point on older structures.

Insurers generally expect to see photographic documentation of the damage before board-up, the materials used, and the scope of the work performed.

## Local Note

Pismo Beach's older beachside cottages — particularly those on streets that run perpendicular to the water — tend to have roof pitches that were designed for aesthetics rather than drainage. After a fire or storm breach, water pools in low-slope sections rather than shedding cleanly to the gutters. When we tarp these roofs, we add a slight ridge to the tarp installation using a lumber batten along the centerline, which encourages runoff toward the eaves rather than letting water sit and work its way under the tarp edge. It is a small adjustment that makes a meaningful difference on this specific housing stock.
