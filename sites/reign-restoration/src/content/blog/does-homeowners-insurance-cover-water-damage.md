Whether your homeowners insurance covers water damage depends almost entirely on one word: **sudden**. If water entered your home suddenly and accidentally — a pipe burst while you were at work, a washing machine hose let go overnight, a dishwasher supply line failed — most standard HO-3 policies will cover the damage. If the water crept in slowly over weeks or months, or if it came from outside during a flood, you're almost certainly looking at a denial. That's the short answer. The longer answer involves a handful of coverage categories, some common claim traps, and a few things you should do in the first hour that can make or break your payout.

## What Standard Homeowners Insurance Actually Covers

Most homeowners policies are written on an HO-3 form, which covers your dwelling and personal property against "sudden and accidental" water damage from internal sources. In plain terms, that means:

- **Burst pipes** — including pipes that freeze and rupture during a hard Texas winter
- **Appliance failures** — a water heater that cracks, a refrigerator ice-maker line that pops loose
- **Plumbing accidents** — a supply line under a sink that fails without warning
- **Accidental overflow** — a bathtub left running, a toilet that overflows due to a sudden blockage

These events are covered because they're unexpected. Your insurer is betting against accidents, not against neglect or the slow march of time.

What's almost universally *excluded*:

- **Gradual leaks** — a slow drip under a cabinet that's been rotting the subfloor for a year
- **Flood water** — water that enters from outside during heavy rain, storm surge, or overflowing creeks. Flood coverage requires a separate NFIP or private flood policy.
- **Sewer or drain backup** — unless you've added a specific sewer backup endorsement (many insurers offer this as an add-on for $50–$100/year)
- **Neglected maintenance** — a roof that's been missing shingles for two seasons, a water heater that's 20 years old and showing rust

In the Royse City and Rockwall County area, where new construction sits alongside homes built in the 1980s and 90s, the age and condition of your plumbing matters. Older galvanized pipes corrode from the inside out. If an adjuster can argue the failure was foreseeable, the claim gets complicated.

## The "Sudden vs. Gradual" Line — and Why Adjusters Look Hard at It

This is where most water damage claims get disputed. Insurance companies employ adjusters whose job includes distinguishing a pipe that failed this morning from a pipe that's been weeping behind a wall for three months.

Signs that suggest gradual damage — and that an adjuster will flag:

- Staining that has yellowed or layered over time
- Mold growth (mold typically colonizes within 24–72 hours of moisture, so heavy mold suggests the water wasn't new)
- Soft or buckled subfloor that has clearly been wet through multiple cycles
- Peeling paint or wallpaper with multiple paint layers underneath

If you discover damage and it genuinely was sudden — you just found it, even if the leak started days ago — document everything before you touch it. Photograph the source, the affected materials, and any standing water. Note the date and time. If you have a smart home water sensor or a utility bill spike that correlates with the leak, save that data. That paper trail is your evidence that this wasn't a slow, ignored problem.

## What to Do in the First Hour After You Discover Water Damage

How you respond in the first 60 minutes affects both the physical damage and your claim.

1. **Stop the water source.** For a burst pipe or appliance failure, turn off the supply valve closest to the source. If you can't find it or it won't close, shut off the main water supply to the house — typically near the meter or where the main line enters the foundation.
2. **Kill the electricity in the affected area.** Water and live circuits are a serious hazard. If you're not sure which breakers cover the wet zone, shut off the main panel.
3. **Photograph everything before moving anything.** Wide shots of the room, close-ups of the source, close-ups of affected flooring, walls, and ceilings. More photos than you think you need.
4. **Call your insurance company to open a claim.** Most carriers have 24-hour claim lines. Opening the claim promptly signals that the damage is fresh and that you acted responsibly.
5. **Begin emergency mitigation — but keep records.** You're allowed (and often required by your policy) to take reasonable steps to prevent further damage: moving furniture off wet carpet, pulling soaked rugs, placing towels. Save receipts for anything you buy. Do not throw away damaged materials before the adjuster sees them or gives you the go-ahead.

What you should *not* do: run fans and assume the floor dried out. Water migrates. It wicks into subfloor, wall cavities, and insulation. A surface that feels dry to the touch can be holding 30–40% moisture content inside the material — enough to grow mold within days. Proper drying requires moisture meters and calibrated equipment, not household box fans.

## When a Restoration Company Fits Into the Insurance Process

Your insurer will likely send an adjuster to assess the loss, but the adjuster's job is to quantify the claim for the insurance company — not to dry your house. That's a separate step, and it's one you can initiate before the adjuster even arrives.

A professional water damage restoration company will:

- Extract standing water with truck-mounted or portable extraction equipment
- Set industrial air movers and dehumidifiers calibrated to the square footage and material types
- Use thermal imaging and moisture meters to find water that migrated beyond the visible damage zone
- Document the drying process with daily moisture readings — documentation your insurance company will likely require to close the claim

Most restoration companies work directly with insurance carriers and can submit documentation in the format adjusters expect. That doesn't mean they work *for* your insurer — a good restoration contractor works for you, the homeowner, and advocates for a complete and accurate scope of repairs.

## What Happens After the Claim Is Approved

Once your insurer accepts the claim, you'll typically receive two types of payments: one for the emergency mitigation and drying work, and one for the structural repairs (replacing drywall, flooring, cabinetry, etc.). Depending on your policy, you may receive actual cash value (ACV) first, with a recoverable depreciation payment released after repairs are completed.

Keep every receipt. Get itemized invoices from every contractor. If the repair scope changes — as it often does when walls are opened and hidden damage is found — document the change and notify your adjuster before proceeding. Surprises after the fact are harder to get paid for than surprises discovered during the work.

Understanding your policy before a loss happens is the most useful thing you can do. Pull out your declarations page, find your water damage exclusions section, and check whether you have a sewer backup endorsement. If you don't, it's worth a conversation with your agent — that add-on is inexpensive and covers a scenario that's genuinely common in older neighborhoods.
