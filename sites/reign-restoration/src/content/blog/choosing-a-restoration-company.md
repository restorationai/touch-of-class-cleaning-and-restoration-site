The single most important thing you can do when choosing a restoration company is verify credentials *before* you sign anything — not after the crew is already tearing out drywall. Most homeowners and property managers get burned not because they hired a bad company, but because they hired fast without asking the right questions. This post walks you through exactly what to look for, what red flags to avoid, and how to protect yourself whether you're dealing with water damage, fire damage, or mold.

## Why the Decision Feels So Hard (and Why That's Intentional)

When a pipe bursts at 11 p.m. or you come home to the smell of smoke, your brain shifts into crisis mode. You want someone — anyone — to show up and fix it. Restoration companies know this. Some of them count on it.

The industry has a well-documented problem with storm chasers and opportunistic contractors who flood disaster-affected neighborhoods, knock on doors, and pressure homeowners into signing Assignment of Benefits (AOB) agreements before the homeowner has even called their insurance carrier. Once you sign an AOB, you've handed over your insurance claim rights to the contractor. That's not always predatory, but it's a significant legal step that deserves a clear head — not a panicked signature at midnight.

The good news: a legitimate restoration company doesn't need you to sign anything unusual before they start an emergency dry-out or board-up. They document, they stabilize, and they work with your adjuster.

## The Five Questions to Ask Before You Hire Anyone

These aren't gotcha questions. A qualified restoration contractor will answer all of them without hesitation.

1. **Are you IICRC-certified?** The Institute of Inspection, Cleaning and Restoration Certification sets the technical standards for the industry — including the S500 standard for water damage and the S520 for mold remediation. Certification means technicians have been trained and tested on those standards, not just handed a mop. Ask which certifications the *technicians* hold, not just the company.

2. **Are you licensed and insured in Texas?** Texas requires general contractors to carry liability insurance and, for certain work, specific trade licenses. Ask for a certificate of insurance and verify it's current. If a contractor hesitates, that's your answer.

3. **Do you bill insurance directly?** Most reputable restoration companies handle direct billing with major carriers. This doesn't mean you're off the hook for your deductible, but it does mean you won't be asked to pay the full invoice upfront and wait for reimbursement. The company should be able to explain the process clearly.

4. **Who actually does the work?** Some restoration companies are primarily sales operations — they sign the contract, then subcontract the actual remediation to whoever is available. That's not automatically bad, but you deserve to know. Ask whether the crew that shows up is employed by the company or subcontracted, and whether the project manager will be on-site.

5. **Can you give me a written scope of work before demolition starts?** Emergency stabilization (stopping active water intrusion, boarding broken windows) sometimes has to happen before a full estimate. But any significant demolition — cutting out drywall, removing flooring, tearing out cabinets — should be preceded by a written scope that your adjuster has reviewed or at least been notified about.

## Red Flags That Should Make You Pause

None of these are automatic dealbreakers, but each one warrants a direct question.

- **Pressure to sign immediately.** A company that tells you the price goes up if you don't sign tonight is using a sales tactic, not responding to a genuine emergency.
- **No written estimate.** Verbal estimates are worth nothing when the final invoice arrives.
- **Vague answers about moisture readings.** After water damage, a legitimate contractor should be taking baseline moisture readings with a calibrated meter and showing you the numbers. If they can't tell you what the moisture content of your subfloor is, they're not drying it scientifically.
- **Mold guarantees.** No honest contractor guarantees they've found and removed every mold spore. What they can guarantee is that they followed the remediation protocol (containment, HEPA filtration, removal of affected materials, post-clearance testing). Anyone promising a "mold-free" home is either uninformed or misleading you.
- **No mention of post-work testing or documentation.** For mold remediation especially, the job isn't done until clearance testing confirms the remediation worked. Ask who does the testing — ideally it's an independent industrial hygienist, not the same company that did the remediation.

## What the Restoration Process Actually Looks Like

Understanding the process helps you evaluate whether a company is cutting corners.

**Water damage** follows a fairly predictable sequence: extract standing water, set drying equipment (air movers and dehumidifiers), monitor moisture levels daily, remove materials that can't be dried in place (saturated drywall, wet insulation), and document everything for the insurance claim. In North Texas, where humidity can work against you in summer, the drying phase typically takes three to five days — sometimes longer if the structure absorbed water for more than 24 hours before anyone noticed. Mold can begin colonizing porous materials within 24 to 48 hours of a water event, which is why the timeline matters.

**Fire damage** is more complex than most people expect. The visible char is only part of the problem. Smoke residue — especially from synthetic materials — penetrates porous surfaces, settles in HVAC systems, and leaves behind acidic soot that continues corroding metal and discoloring surfaces long after the fire is out. Odor neutralization isn't just spraying deodorizer; it involves thermal fogging, ozone treatment, or hydroxyl generation depending on the materials affected. A company that skips the odor protocol will leave you with a house that smells like smoke every time the temperature rises.

**Mold remediation** in Texas homes often traces back to HVAC condensation issues, slow plumbing leaks behind walls, or inadequate attic ventilation — not dramatic flooding events. The remediation itself involves containment (plastic sheeting and negative air pressure to prevent cross-contamination), removal of affected materials, HEPA vacuuming, antimicrobial treatment of remaining surfaces, and clearance testing. The moisture source has to be fixed first. No remediation holds if the leak is still dripping.

## How to Use Your Insurance Carrier as a Check

Your insurance adjuster is not your enemy here — they're actually a useful reality check on contractor estimates. Before authorizing any significant work beyond emergency stabilization, call your carrier and open a claim. Most carriers have preferred vendor networks, but you are generally not required to use them; you can hire your own contractor. What matters is that your contractor documents the loss thoroughly — photos, moisture logs, itemized scope — so the adjuster has what they need to process the claim.

If a contractor tells you not to call your insurance company, or offers to "handle everything" in a way that keeps the carrier out of the loop, be very careful. That arrangement tends to benefit the contractor, not you.
