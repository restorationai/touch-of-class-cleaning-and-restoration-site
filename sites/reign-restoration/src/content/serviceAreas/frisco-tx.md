## Restoration emergencies common in Frisco

Frisco's explosive growth from farmland to one of the fastest-growing cities in the country happened almost entirely after the mid-1990s, which means the housing stock is remarkably uniform in one important way: nearly every home is slab-on-grade brick veneer, two stories, with an attic air handler sitting directly above finished living space. That combination creates a specific failure pattern. When an attic HVAC condensate line backs up or a supply run develops a pinhole leak, water doesn't hit a crawl space or a basement — it saturates drywall ceilings, soaks insulation, and pools on the slab before anyone notices. PEX and copper supply runs in homes built during different phases of Frisco's development each carry their own failure modes: PEX fittings can creep loose over years of thermal cycling through North Texas summers; older copper lines in neighborhoods like Stonebriar and Starwood are approaching the age where pinhole corrosion becomes a real risk.

Below the slab, the Houston Black clay that underlies most of Frisco's west side swells dramatically after heavy rain and shrinks during the long dry stretches that define a North Texas summer. That movement stresses plumbing penetrations and can open gaps at the slab perimeter that let moisture migrate inward. Stewart and Panther creeks drain westward toward Lewisville Lake through this same clay belt, and neighborhoods near those corridors can see yard saturation that pushes moisture toward foundations long after the rain stops. Meanwhile, Frisco's severe hail seasons — the area sits squarely in the southern end of Tornado Alley's hail corridor — routinely compromise roof membranes, and the window between a damaged roof and active interior water intrusion can be measured in the next afternoon thunderstorm.
