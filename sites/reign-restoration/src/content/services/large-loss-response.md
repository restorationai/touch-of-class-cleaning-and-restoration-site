**Multi-unit apartment flooding, a hail-damaged shopping center, a freeze event that bursts pipes across forty units in one night:** large loss and catastrophic response is what gets called when a single loss outgrows a standard restoration crew. This isn't a one-room water extraction. It's simultaneous mobilization across a building, a campus, or a portfolio, with large loss adjusters and property managers expecting daily documentation and a crew that can actually scale. Reign Restoration answers 24/7 and coordinates resources for losses that span structures, floors, or entire properties.

## What large loss and catastrophic response actually involves

A large loss is defined less by a single dollar figure than by scope: multiple affected units or buildings, business interruption exposure, or a loss large enough that a Third Party Administrator (TPA) or dedicated large loss adjuster gets assigned to the file. Think hotels, apartment complexes, HOAs, retail centers, warehouses, schools, and mixed-use buildings hit by burst pipes, roof failures, hail, or widespread fire or flood. The work involves staging multiple extraction and drying crews at once, mapping moisture across thousands of square feet instead of a few rooms, running desiccant dehumidification alongside standard refrigerant units where humidity loads are too high for conventional equipment, and keeping a live psychrometric log that a carrier's large loss team can review daily. Timelines run from days to months depending on structure count and whether reconstruction is bundled into the same scope.

## Our process

1. **Rapid mobilization and resource staging.** We pull in the crew count, drying equipment, and generator or temporary power capacity the loss actually requires, rather than sending a single-truck crew to a multi-building problem.
2. **Building-by-building damage assessment.** Moisture mapping, thermal imaging, and affected-material inventories are logged per unit or floor so nothing gets missed in a property with dozens of similar-looking rooms.
3. **Phased extraction and large-scale drying.** Water removal and structural drying run in stages, often prioritizing occupied or income-producing areas first, with negative air and containment where categories of water or smoke differ by zone.
4. **Carrier and project coordination.** Daily drying logs, photo documentation, and Xactimate-compatible estimates go directly to the assigned adjuster or TPA, with a single point of contact managing the file instead of scattered updates.
5. **Reconstruction phasing and close-out.** Repairs are sequenced to return occupied space to use as early as possible, with a final walkthrough and moisture clearance before the file closes.

## What separates a good large loss response from a bad one

The most common failure on a catastrophic file is under-resourcing: sending a crew sized for a residential job to a property that needed triple the equipment and staff on day one. That shows up as drying times that stretch for weeks, secondary mold growth in units that should have been dry by week two, and moisture readings that were never taken consistently across the whole structure. Large loss adjusters are specifically looking for daily psychrometric documentation, consistent photo logs tied to a floor plan, and an estimate that reflects the actual scope rather than a rough walkthrough number. Poor phasing is another failure point: crews that treat a forty-unit building like one big job instead of sequencing it unit by unit end up displacing tenants longer than necessary and creating business interruption claims that didn't need to happen.
