Smoke doesn't stay where the fire was. Within hours of a kitchen fire, a grease flare-up, or a wildfire smoke event drifting through open vents, residue has already settled into carpet fibers, migrated through return air ducts, and bonded to painted surfaces two or three rooms away from the actual flame. The smell that lingers after a fire is out isn't cosmetic. It's an acidic, oily film that keeps reacting with humidity and heat long after the smoke has cleared, which is why DIY wipe-downs and store-bought odor sprays almost always fail.

## What smoke damage restoration actually involves

Smoke damage restoration is a chemistry problem as much as a cleaning problem. The approach depends entirely on what burned: a protein fire (meat, fat, cooking oil) leaves a nearly invisible, yellowish, extremely pungent film that's hard to see but easy to smell. A synthetic fire (furniture foam, electronics, plastics) leaves thick, black, sticky soot that smears if you touch it. Wildfire smoke cleanup is its own category, usually a dry, powdery residue that travels through HVAC systems and settles in attics and wall cavities far from any visible damage.

Each type requires a different cleaning agent, a different mechanical approach, and in many cases different PPE for the crew. Equipment on a typical smoke residue cleanup job includes HEPA vacuums, dry chemical sponges for porous surfaces, degreasing agents for protein residue, thermal foggers to re-create the conditions that let smoke penetrate in the first place (which helps the deodorizing agent reach the same hidden spaces), and in heavier jobs, hydroxyl generators or ozone treatment in a sealed, unoccupied space. Timeline for a contained, single-room smoke event typically runs two to four days; whole-house jobs with HVAC contamination and content pack-out run longer.

## Our process

1. **Soot characterization.** Before any cleaning begins, we identify whether the residue is protein-based, synthetic, or a wildfire/ash profile. This determines the cleaning agents and technique used on every subsequent step, since the wrong cleaner can set soot into a surface instead of lifting it.
2. **Pre-cleaning and structural triage.** Loose soot is removed with HEPA vacuums and dry sponges before any liquid cleaning agent touches a surface. Wet-wiping first is one of the most common mistakes that smears soot deeper into drywall texture and grout lines.
3. **Thermal fogging and deodorization.** Thermal fogging recreates the heat and particle size of the original smoke event, allowing deodorizing agents to penetrate the same cracks, fibers, and cavities the smoke originally reached. Surface spraying alone doesn't solve odor that's embedded this way.
4. **HVAC and duct assessment.** If the fire or smoke event involved a running HVAC system, ducts, coils, and filters are inspected and cleaned separately, since recirculated smoke is a common source of odor that returns weeks after the visible cleanup is done.
5. **Contents handling and final odor verification.** Salvageable contents are cleaned, deodorized, or packed out depending on material and damage level, and the space is re-checked for odor before the job is closed out.

## What separates a good smoke damage response from a bad one

The most common mistake is treating smoke damage as a surface cleaning job. Wiping visible soot off walls without addressing what's inside the wall cavity, the duct system, or the subfloor means the odor comes back, often worse, once the weather turns humid and residue reactivates. A second common error is using an all-purpose cleaner on protein soot, which can smear the film into porous drywall rather than lifting it.

Insurance adjusters reviewing a smoke damage claim typically look for documented soot characterization (protein vs. synthetic vs. wildfire ash), moisture and odor readings before and after treatment, and an itemized scope separating cleanable contents from items requiring replacement. A restoration report that just says "cleaned and deodorized" without that documentation tends to generate disputes during the claims process. We document soot type, affected materials, and treatment method as part of every scope, which keeps the claim moving rather than stalling on a coverage question.
