## Why University Park's Housing Stock Shapes Every Renovation

Incorporated in 1924 and largely built out by the 1940s, University Park developed its residential character around Southern Methodist University and has been quietly rebuilding itself ever since. The result is a neighborhood patchwork where original pier-and-beam foundations sit alongside poured slabs, and where 1930s balloon-frame walls — sometimes filled with horsehair plaster — share a street with modern 2×6 construction.

That mix creates real complications for remodeling work. Pier-and-beam homes along the Hillcrest Avenue corridor frequently reveal uneven subfloor systems once finish flooring comes up, requiring sistering or partial replacement before tile or hardwood can go down flat. Original knob-and-tube wiring still turns up inside walls during kitchen remodels in the older Volk Estates pockets, triggering code-required panel upgrades that need to be scoped before demo begins. And because University Park is a separate municipality bordered by Dallas on three sides, permits pull through the City of University Park's own building department — not Dallas — with its own inspection cadence and plan review timeline. Knowing that upfront keeps projects on schedule.

## Our Renovation and General Contracting Process in University Park

Every project starts with a scope walk, not a sales pitch. We document existing conditions — foundation type, wall assembly, mechanical locations, any evidence of prior water intrusion or deferred maintenance — before a single number goes on paper. In University Park's older homes, that pre-scope step regularly surfaces surprises: a bathroom remodel that reveals galvanized supply lines behind the tile, or a kitchen gut that uncovers a load-bearing wall that wasn't on the original drawings.

From there the process runs in defined phases:

1. **Permitting and plan submission** through the City of University Park building department, including any required structural or MEP drawings.
2. **Selective demolition** — careful in older homes where lead paint and asbestos-containing materials (ACM) in floor tile mastic or popcorn ceilings are a real possibility in pre-1978 construction.
3. **Rough-in work** — framing corrections, plumbing and electrical rough-in, HVAC modifications — all inspected before walls close.
4. **Finish installation** — cabinetry, tile, flooring, fixtures, paint — sequenced to protect completed work.
5. **Final inspection and punch list** — we don't call a job done until the city signs off and you've walked every room.

For post-damage rebuilds — a common entry point for Reign Restoration clients — we coordinate directly with your insurance adjuster, matching scope to the approved estimate and flagging code-upgrade costs (like that panel replacement) as separate line items so there's no confusion at close-out.

## Drainage, Soil, and the Remodeling Decisions They Drive

University Park's storm-drain network dates largely from 1925 to 1955, built partly over a buried creek system. In heavy rain events, low-lying ground ponds before the infrastructure can move the water — and that ponding doesn't stay outside. Homeowners near Curtis Park and along the Preston Road corridor have learned that a finished basement or a slab addition with inadequate grading becomes a recurring water event.

When we're scoping an addition or a below-grade finish, we look at the existing grade, the downspout discharge points, and the distance to the nearest storm inlet. If the drainage math doesn't work, we build a solution into the scope — French drain, swale correction, or sump installation — rather than hand the homeowner a beautiful remodel that floods in the first big storm.
