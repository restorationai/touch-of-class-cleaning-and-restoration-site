## Why Richardson Properties See Distinctive Storm Damage

Richardson sits at the intersection of two counties and two eras of construction. The postwar subdivisions that spread along Central Expressway — Richardson Heights, Canyon Creek, Cottonwood Heights — were built through the 1950s into the early 1980s. Many of those homes still sit on original cast-iron or clay drain lines running under slab foundations. When a storm drops two or three inches of rain in under an hour, those aging lines can back up faster than modern PVC systems, and the slab itself can trap water with nowhere to drain.

The creek corridors make this worse. Duck Creek, Cottonwood Creek, Prairie Creek, and Spring Creek all thread through the city's greenbelts and backyards. Homes backing up to these corridors in the 75081 and 75082 ZIP codes see localized flash flooding that doesn't always show up in FEMA flood maps — meaning some homeowners are caught without flood coverage and don't realize it until after the event. On the newer end of the spectrum, the mid-rise development at CityLine introduced flat or low-slope commercial roofing that collects debris and standing water differently than the pitched residential roofs a mile west.

North Texas storm seasons also bring a specific sequence: a spring hail event compromises roof underlayment, summer heat bakes the damaged areas, and then a fall thunderstorm finds every weakened seam at once. By the time interior damage is visible, moisture has often been working inside the wall assembly for weeks.

## Our Storm Damage Restoration Process in Richardson

Every job starts with a full-structure assessment, not just the obvious entry point. We document wind and water intrusion with moisture meters and thermal imaging, tracing how far water has migrated from the initial breach. In older slab-on-grade homes common to Richardson Heights and the surrounding streets, that means checking under flooring and along interior wall bases where capillary wicking through concrete is easy to miss.

From there the process moves in a defined sequence:

1. **Secure and tarp** — open roof sections, broken windows, and compromised siding are covered to stop ongoing intrusion before any drying equipment goes in.
2. **Water extraction and drying** — standing water comes out first, then commercial-grade desiccant dehumidifiers and air movers are positioned to dry structural assemblies to IICRC S500 standards.
3. **Debris and damaged material removal** — storm-damaged insulation, sheathing, and interior finishes are removed and documented for the insurance claim.
4. **Mold prevention treatment** — given Richardson's humidity and the clay soil's tendency to hold ground moisture, antimicrobial treatment of affected cavities is standard practice, not an upsell.
5. **Reconstruction coordination** — once the structure is dry and cleared, we coordinate or perform the rebuild, from framing and drywall to roofing and exterior finishes.

For tree-strike damage — common after the large post oaks and pecans in Breckinridge Park-area neighborhoods come down in straight-line wind events — we also handle debris removal and assess whether root-heave has affected the foundation perimeter before closing out the job.

## Local Note: Clay Soil and Hidden Moisture Migration

One pattern that comes up repeatedly in Richardson's older neighborhoods is moisture that migrates laterally through the Houston Black clay fill around a foundation rather than draining away after a storm. After a significant rain event, water can wick back toward the slab for days as the clay slowly releases what it absorbed. We've seen interior baseboards in Canyon Creek and Prairie Creek homes show elevated moisture readings three to five days after a storm — long after the roof has been tarped and the visible water is gone. If you've had a major storm and your home felt fine the next morning, it's still worth a moisture check 48 to 72 hours later before assuming you're in the clear.
