## Why Garland Properties Are Particularly Vulnerable to Fire Damage

Garland's housing stock tells two different stories depending on which part of the city you're in. Neighborhoods developed in the 1950s and 1960s near downtown carry older wiring — aluminum branch circuits and fuse-based panels that were never designed for today's appliance loads. Overloaded circuits are one of the leading ignition sources in those homes, and the original wood-framed walls provide fast fuel once a fire gets behind the drywall.

On the newer side, subdivisions built in the 1990s and 2000s used engineered lumber and open-truss roof systems that are structurally efficient but collapse quickly under fire load — sometimes in under ten minutes of sustained flame. That speed changes the restoration equation: structural fire damage in a truss-roof home often requires a licensed engineer's assessment before any interior work begins, and Garland's building department will require permits for load-bearing repairs regardless of how the loss is categorized by your insurer.

Garland also sits in the Dallas–Fort Worth Metroplex's wind corridor. Prevailing southerly winds can push embers from a neighboring structure onto wood shake or aged composition roofing, and the city's density means fire can jump a fence line before the first engine company arrives. Secondary smoke and heat damage to adjacent structures is common, and those losses are sometimes overlooked until weeks later when odor complaints surface.

## Our Fire Damage Restoration Process in Garland

Every job starts with a structured assessment — not a walk-through with a clipboard, but a room-by-room inspection that maps soot type (wet smoke versus dry smoke versus protein residue), documents affected materials with photos and moisture readings, and identifies hidden damage paths like wall cavities, crawlspaces, and ductwork. That documentation package is built to meet insurance carrier standards from day one.

Once the scope is confirmed, the process moves in a defined sequence:

1. **Board-up and tarping** — securing the structure against weather and unauthorized entry while work is underway.
2. **Debris removal and selective demolition** — char-damaged materials come out; salvageable materials are catalogued before anything is discarded.
3. **Dry-ice blasting and HEPA vacuuming** — mechanical soot removal from framing, subfloor, and hard surfaces before any chemical cleaning begins.
4. **Odor neutralization** — thermal fogging and hydroxyl generation to break down smoke compounds embedded in soft goods, insulation, and wall cavities rather than masking them.
5. **Structural drying** — fire suppression water adds a secondary damage layer; drying is conducted per the IICRC S500 standard with calibrated desiccant and refrigerant dehumidifiers.
6. **Reconstruction** — from drywall and insulation to cabinetry and finish work, completed under the required Garland building permits.
