## Why University Park Properties Experience Fire Damage Differently

University Park was largely built out between the 1920s and the 1940s, then steadily redeveloped from the 1970s onward. Walk a single block near the Hillcrest Avenue corridor and you may pass a 1935 pier-and-beam cottage next to a 2018 two-story rebuild on a concrete slab. That mix creates real complications after a fire.

Older pier-and-beam homes draw air through subfloor gaps, which means smoke and soot travel into wall bays, attic spaces, and crawl areas that a newer sealed structure would largely protect. Original plaster walls — still common in the pre-war stock near Volk Estates — absorb odor compounds differently than modern drywall. The plaster itself may not need replacement, but the lath cavities behind it can hold smoke residue for months if not treated with the right neutralizing agents. Meanwhile, newer rebuilds often feature open floor plans and HVAC systems with higher airflow rates, which can distribute smoke particulates across the entire square footage within minutes of ignition.

## Our Fire Damage Restoration Process in University Park

Every fire job starts with a room-by-room scope before a single bag of debris leaves the property. We document structural char depth, soot type (dry, wet, or protein-based — each requires a different cleaning chemistry), and HVAC contamination. That documentation also forms the foundation of your insurance claim.

From there, the process follows a defined sequence:

**Structural stabilization** — board-up, roof tarping, and shoring if load-bearing members are compromised. University Park's older homes sometimes have balloon-frame construction, where fire can travel vertically inside wall cavities from foundation to attic without visible flame spread on the surface.

**Controlled demolition** — removing only what cannot be restored. On pre-war homes, original millwork, hardwood floors, and plaster medallions often survive and are worth saving; we scope conservatively and confirm with the homeowner before cutting.

**Soot and odor removal** — thermal fogging, hydroxyl generation, and HEPA vacuuming of ductwork. Protein fires (common in kitchen fires) leave an almost invisible residue that smells intensely for weeks; it requires enzymatic treatment, not just surface wiping.

**Drying and dehumidification** — firefighting water adds a secondary moisture problem. We treat the structure to IICRC S500 drying standards before any reconstruction begins, because trapping moisture behind new drywall creates a mold problem on top of the fire damage.

**Reconstruction** — framing, insulation, drywall, paint, and finish work coordinated with University Park's permit and inspection process.
