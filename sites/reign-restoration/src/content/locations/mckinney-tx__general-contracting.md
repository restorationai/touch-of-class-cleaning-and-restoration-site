## Why McKinney's Housing Stock Shapes Every Renovation Decision

The older neighborhoods clustered around the courthouse square — ZIP codes 75069 and 75070 — carry homes built on pier-and-beam foundations over open crawlspaces. Those systems breathe, which is an asset in North Texas heat, but they also shift. Expansive clay soils throughout Collin County swell after rain and contract hard during summer drought, and that seasonal movement telegraphs into door frames, subfloors, and load-bearing walls over decades. Before a single cabinet goes in on a kitchen remodel in one of these homes, a thorough structural assessment of the floor system isn't optional — it's the difference between a renovation that lasts twenty years and one that starts showing cracks in eighteen months.

West of US 75, master-planned communities like Craig Ranch and Trinity Falls were built on engineered slabs with post-tension cables. Those slabs perform well, but any remodel that involves cutting concrete — for a bathroom drain relocation, a wet bar addition, or radiant floor prep — requires knowing exactly where those cables run before a saw blade touches the floor. Reign Restoration's team coordinates ground-penetrating scans on post-tension slabs before any concrete work begins.

McKinney also sits inside Collin County's adopted International Residential Code cycle, and the city enforces energy-efficiency updates on permitted remodels. Adding square footage or converting a garage triggers insulation and fenestration requirements that didn't exist when many of these homes were built. Pulling permits correctly — and passing inspections the first time — is part of the job, not an afterthought.
