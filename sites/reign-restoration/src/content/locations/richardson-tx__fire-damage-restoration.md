## Why Richardson's Housing Stock Complicates Fire Damage

Richardson grew fast during the postwar decades, and most of that growth happened before energy codes required vapor barriers, sealed wall cavities, or fire-rated assemblies in residential construction. Homes in Richardson Heights, Canyon Creek, and Cottonwood Heights — many still on original slab foundations over reactive Houston Black clay — were built with wood framing techniques that create interconnected void spaces behind walls and under floors. When fire moves through those cavities, smoke and soot travel far beyond the room of origin. A kitchen fire in a 1960s ranch can push char residue into the attic, through the soffit, and into adjacent bedrooms before the fire department arrives.

That same construction era often means original plaster-and-lath ceilings rather than modern drywall. Plaster is denser, which slows smoke penetration in some ways — but once soot embeds in a plaster surface, standard dry-sponge cleaning isn't enough. The texture holds particulates differently, and aggressive scrubbing can fracture the surface, creating a new problem on top of the original one. Knowing what's behind the walls before touching them is half the job.

## Our Fire Damage Restoration Process in Richardson

The first step on every fire loss is a systematic scope — not a visual walk-through, but a structured assessment using thermal imaging and air sampling to map where smoke traveled beyond the burn zone. In a slab-on-grade home common to ZIP code 75080 and 75081, that often means checking the HVAC return air system, which can distribute soot throughout the entire house within minutes of ignition.

From there, the process moves in a defined sequence:

- **Emergency board-up and tarping** to secure the structure against weather and unauthorized entry while the claim is being processed.
- **Contents pack-out** — salvageable items are catalogued, packed, and transported to a climate-controlled facility for ozone and thermal fogging treatment.
- **Structural demo and dry-out** — fire suppression water saturates framing and insulation, and mold colonization can begin within 48 to 72 hours. Drying happens concurrently with smoke remediation, not after.
- **Soot and smoke residue removal** — we use dry chemical sponges, HEPA vacuuming, and wet-cleaning methods matched to the specific surface type, whether that's original plaster, brick veneer, or modern drywall.
- **Odor neutralization** — thermal fogging and hydroxyl generation reach into wall cavities and ductwork where surface cleaning can't. This step is not optional on any full-loss fire job.
- **Structural rebuild** — as an IICRC Certified Firm holding the FSRT (Fire and Smoke Restoration Technician) credential, we carry the scope from demo through finished reconstruction under one contract.

## Local Note

Homes built on Houston Black clay — which runs under much of Richardson along the Duck Creek and Spring Creek corridors — shift seasonally as the soil expands and contracts with moisture changes. After a fire, when fire suppression water soaks into the ground around the foundation perimeter, that clay can swell unevenly. We've seen post-fire foundation movement in older Richardson Heights properties that wasn't present before the loss. It's worth having a foundation assessment included in the scope on any ground-floor fire with significant suppression water, particularly on homes built before 1975.
