## Why Richardson's Housing Stock Shapes Every Renovation Decision

Richardson's development timeline creates a specific set of conditions that any contractor working here needs to account for. Homes in Richardson Heights, Canyon Creek, and Cottonwood Heights were built predominantly between the 1950s and early 1980s — which means original cast-iron or clay drain lines under slab are common, galvanized supply lines haven't always been replaced, and load-bearing wall configurations don't always match what modern open-plan buyers expect. Opening up a kitchen in a 1965 ranch isn't the same project as it would be in a 2005 suburb.

The soil beneath these neighborhoods adds another layer. Richardson sits on Houston Black clay — some of the most reactive expansive soil in North Texas. Seasonal moisture swings cause measurable foundation movement, which over decades translates into out-of-square door frames, cracked tile, and shifting subfloors. Before any remodel begins, we assess whether existing conditions are stable or whether foundation movement is still active, because finishing work on top of an unresolved structural issue is money spent twice.

For commercial and mixed-use properties near CityLine, the considerations shift toward tenant improvement standards, ADA compliance, and coordinating work around occupied adjacent spaces — a different discipline, but one we handle under the same license.

## Our Renovation and General Contracting Process in Richardson

Every project starts with a scope walkthrough, not a sales pitch. We walk the property with you, identify what the space needs structurally and mechanically, and build a written scope before any number is attached to it. For post-damage rebuilds — after a pipe failure, a kitchen fire, or storm intrusion — we cross-reference the insurance adjuster's estimate line by line so nothing gets missed and no scope creep surprises you later.

Demolition in older Richardson homes requires care. Homes built before 1978 may contain lead-based paint in trim and doors; homes with original acoustic ceiling texture or vinyl floor tile from the same era may contain asbestos-containing materials. We test before we demo, and we handle abatement through proper protocols before any renovation work begins. This isn't a formality — it's the step that protects your family and keeps the project legally compliant with Texas Department of State Health Services requirements.

From framing and rough-in through finish carpentry, tile, cabinetry, and final inspection, we self-perform the core trades and pull licensed subcontractors for electrical and plumbing under our general contractor license (#MRC2276). One point of contact, one contract, one warranty.

## Richardson Permits, HOAs, and Code Coordination

Richardson falls under the City of Richardson's Building Inspection Division, which enforces the current adopted IBC and IRC with local amendments. Permit timelines for residential remodels typically run one to three weeks for plan review depending on project complexity — we build that into the schedule rather than treating it as a surprise. For structural changes, additions, or anything touching the building envelope, engineered drawings are often required, and we coordinate with licensed engineers when the scope demands it.

Several of Richardson's established neighborhoods have active HOAs with architectural review requirements. In areas like Canyon Creek, exterior changes — new windows, door replacements, additions, roofing material changes — may require HOA approval before the city permit is even submitted. We've worked through that two-step process enough times to know how to sequence it so approvals don't stack and delay your start date.

## Local Note

One detail that catches out-of-area contractors working in the Duck Creek and Prairie Creek corridors: the reactive clay soil doesn't just affect foundations — it affects flatwork and exterior drainage grades in ways that show up inside the house years later. Patios and sidewalks that have heaved away from the foundation create water intrusion pathways that look like plumbing leaks or roof failures on the interior. When we're scoping a renovation in these areas and a homeowner mentions recurring moisture in a specific corner or along a particular wall, we look outside first. Correcting the grade and repairing the flatwork before finishing the interior is the fix that actually holds.
