SeaTac sits directly under one of the busiest flight corridors in the Pacific Northwest, and the same marine air that rolls off Puget Sound and collects against the rooftops here does real damage over time. Asphalt shingles that might last 25 years in a drier climate often show granule loss, moss infiltration, and lifted edges well before that benchmark in this part of King County. When it is time for a full roof replacement or a new installation on a build or addition, the conditions here demand materials and methods chosen for sustained moisture exposure, not just a dry-climate standard.

## Why SeaTac Rooftops Wear Differently Than the Industry Average

The rainfall pattern in SeaTac is less about dramatic storms and more about relentless low-grade saturation from October through April. That kind of moisture does its worst work in the details: around pipe boots, along valleys where two roof planes meet, and at the edges where gutters pull away slightly from the fascia. Moss and algae establish themselves in those shaded zones first, and once a mat of organic growth holds moisture against the shingle surface, the granule layer starts breaking down faster than the manufacturer's warranty assumes.

The housing stock in SeaTac also matters. Many residential properties here were built during the postwar expansion of the 1950s and 1960s to house workers connected to what became Seattle-Tacoma International Airport. Those homes often have low-slope gable roofs with minimal overhang, which means water drains more slowly and the eave zone stays wet longer after each rain event. Identifying that pattern early in a project changes the underlayment specification and the flashing approach.

## Our Roofing Installation and Replacement Process in SeaTac

Every project starts with a documented inspection: we photograph the existing deck, probe for soft spots that indicate sheathing rot, and note any penetrations that were flashed with materials that have since corroded or separated. In SeaTac's climate, we consistently find that the original installation did not include an ice-and-water shield membrane at the eaves, which is now standard practice for this region. Correcting that during a full replacement adds meaningful protection against the wind-driven rain that pushes water back up under the first course of shingles.

Once the old material is stripped, the deck is inspected again with the underlayment off. Any sheathing that shows delamination or compression from long-term moisture gets replaced before anything new goes down. We then install a synthetic underlayment, apply the ice-and-water shield at eaves and valleys, and set the new shingles or roofing material to manufacturer specifications. All work is performed under National Restoration Construction's General Contractor Certificate of Registration with Washington State's Department of Labor and Industries.

Roof replacements in SeaTac fall under King County's building permit requirements when the scope involves structural deck repair or changes to the roofline. A straight shingle-over-shingle replacement on an unchanged deck may qualify for a simpler permit pathway, but that determination depends on the specific scope of work. We handle permit applications and coordinate inspections as part of the project, so the homeowner or property manager is not tracking down the paperwork independently. ## Local Note

Properties immediately adjacent to the airport's noise abatement corridors sometimes have upgraded attic insulation installed under FAA sound mitigation programs. That insulation is dense-pack or batt material blown into the rafter bays, and it changes how the attic ventilates. When we replace a roof on one of these properties, we verify that the ridge and soffit ventilation is still functioning correctly after the new roof is set, because a blocked ventilation path in a well-insulated attic accelerates moisture buildup against the new deck from the inside. It is a detail specific to this part of SeaTac that does not come up on most roofing jobs elsewhere in King County.
