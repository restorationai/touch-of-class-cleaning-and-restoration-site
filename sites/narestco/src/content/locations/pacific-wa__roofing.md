Pacific, WA sits in a narrow corridor between the Green River and the rising terrain of the Auburn foothills, and that geography does real work on roofs. The combination of persistent marine moisture pushing in from Puget Sound, heavy winter rainfall that pools against low-slope sections, and the occasional wind event channeled through the valley means roofing systems here take a different kind of beating than they do in drier parts of King County. Whether you're replacing a worn asphalt system on a mid-century ranch or installing new roofing on a remodeled property, National Restoration Construction brings the same methodical approach to every project in this community.

## Why Pacific Properties See Accelerated Roof Wear

The Green River valley's rainfall totals are not dramatically different from the broader Seattle metro, but the way moisture lingers matters more than the raw inches. Pacific's relatively flat valley floor means standing water on low-pitched roofs has fewer places to drain quickly. Algae and moss colonize shingles faster in shaded, slow-draining conditions, and once moss takes hold it lifts shingle edges and creates pathways for water to reach the decking beneath. Older homes in the city, many built during the post-war expansion of the 1950s and 1960s, often have original roof decking that has absorbed decades of moisture cycling. When that decking softens, a surface shingle replacement is not enough. A proper installation means probing the decking before any new material goes down.

Wind events in the valley also tend to arrive from the south and southwest, and rooflines oriented in that direction take the brunt of uplift stress at the rake edges. Fastener patterns and starter-strip installation matter more here than in sheltered urban neighborhoods.

## Our Roofing Installation and Replacement Process in Pacific

Every project starts with a documented inspection, not an estimate written from the driveway. A crew member walks the roof, checks decking integrity, inspects the flashing at every penetration, and photographs existing conditions before a single shingle is removed. That documentation matters later if an insurance claim is involved.

Once the scope is confirmed, the sequence follows a consistent pattern:

- **Tear-off and decking assessment**: Old roofing material is removed and the decking is inspected for soft spots, delamination, or rot. Damaged sections are replaced before anything new is installed.
- **Underlayment and ice-and-water barrier**: In the Pacific climate, a self-adhering ice-and-water barrier along eaves and in valleys is not optional. It is the layer that keeps water out when wind-driven rain gets under shingles.
- **Shingle or material installation**: Fastener pattern, overlap, and alignment are checked against manufacturer specs, which also protects the product warranty.
- **Flashing and penetration sealing**: Chimney, pipe boot, and skylight flashing are replaced or resealed as part of every full replacement, not treated as add-ons.
- **Final inspection and debris removal**: The site is swept with a magnet for fasteners before the crew leaves.

## Local Note

One thing that comes up specifically in Pacific and the adjacent Green River corridor: homes built on the valley floor sometimes have attic ventilation systems that were never sized for the moisture load the climate actually delivers. Inadequate soffit-to-ridge airflow causes condensation to build up on the underside of the decking, which looks from above like a roof leak but originates from inside. A new roof installed over a poorly ventilated attic will develop the same problem within a few years. During every inspection in this area, the crew checks ridge vent continuity and soffit clearance, because fixing the roof surface without addressing ventilation just restarts the clock.
