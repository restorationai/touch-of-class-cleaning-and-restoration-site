## How to Choose a Biohazard Cleanup Company

Choosing the right company comes down to four things: certification, licensing, response time, and scope.

**Certification**: Look for [IICRC certification](https://www.iicrc.org) as a baseline. The IICRC sets the industry standard for remediation protocols, including biohazard work. For properties built before 1978, EPA Lead-Safe certification is also required before disturbing painted surfaces during cleanup.

**Licensing**: In Washington State, any company performing structural repairs after remediation must hold a General Contractor Certificate of Registration from the [WA Department of Labor and Industries](https://www.lni.wa.gov). A biohazard-only company that cannot also handle drywall removal, subfloor replacement, or structural repairs will require you to hire a second contractor, which complicates the insurance claim and extends the timeline.

**Response time**: For biohazard situations, every hour matters for both health and property reasons. Confirm whether the company you are calling is actually available 24/7 or whether after-hours calls go to an answering service with a next-day callback.

**Scope of work**: Ask specifically whether the company handles containment, removal, disposal, decontamination, odor neutralization, and any necessary structural repairs in-house, or whether any phase is subcontracted. Subcontracting adds handoff risk and can slow the insurance documentation process.

## What Happens During a Professional Biohazard Cleanup

A properly executed biohazard cleanup follows a structured process regardless of which company you hire. Here is what each phase involves:

1. **Scene assessment and containment**: The crew evaluates the extent of contamination, establishes containment barriers (poly sheeting, negative air pressure), and identifies affected materials including flooring, drywall, and subfloor.
2. **Personal protective equipment**: Technicians work in full Tyvek suits, N95 or higher respirators, face shields, and double-layer chemical-resistant gloves throughout the job.
3. **Removal of contaminated materials**: Porous materials that cannot be decontaminated (carpet, pad, drywall, insulation) are bagged in approved biohazard containers and disposed of per [OSHA bloodborne pathogen standards (29 CFR 1910.1030)](https://www.osha.gov/bloodborne-pathogens).
4. **Surface decontamination**: Hard surfaces are treated with EPA-registered hospital-grade disinfectants. Multiple applications and dwell times are required to achieve pathogen reduction.
5. **Odor neutralization**: Hydroxyl generators or thermal fogging may be used to address odor in structural cavities, HVAC systems, and adjacent spaces.
6. **Clearance verification**: A post-remediation verification step confirms that contamination levels are within safe limits before the space is re-occupied or reconstruction begins.
7. **Reconstruction (if needed)**: Structural repairs to flooring, drywall, or framing are completed under the contractor's license. This is where having a General Contractor license in-house, rather than subcontracting, saves significant time.
