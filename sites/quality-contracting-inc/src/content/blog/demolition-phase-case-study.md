Large-loss restoration jobs, the kind involving major water damage, fire damage, or storm damage, almost always start with demolition. Before anything gets rebuilt, damaged drywall, flooring, insulation, and framing have to come out. It's dusty, physical work, and it sets the tone for everything that follows. When a homeowner is living through that phase of a huge project, the crew's conduct on site matters as much as their technical skill.

## What "leads by example" actually means on a demo crew

On a demolition crew, leading by example means the foreman is the first one suited up and the last one to leave, setting the pace for safety and quality instead of just supervising from a clipboard. That matters because demo work involves real hazards: dust containment, sharp debris, exposed nails, and sometimes hidden electrical or plumbing lines behind the walls being opened up.

A good foreman walks the space before tools come out, flags anything unexpected (a hidden pipe, old knob-and-tube wiring, a section of subfloor that's rotted further than expected), and adjusts the plan on the spot rather than pushing through. That's also where the "skill" Denise mentions shows up, knowing which materials can be selectively removed and which need to come out entirely, and doing it without damaging the parts of the structure that are staying.

## Why safety and clean-up show up together in reviews like this

Safety and daily clean-up are connected because a demo site that isn't cleaned up at the end of each day becomes a safety hazard the next morning. Dust barriers, floor protection, and swept debris aren't cosmetic extras, they're part of how a crew keeps a job site livable for a family still occupying part of the home.

On a project of any real size, that discipline has to repeat daily for weeks, not just look good on day one. Homeowners notice when a crew treats their house like it's still a home and not just a job site, which is likely why punctuality and clean-up made it into Denise's review right alongside safety and skill.

On a large loss, the same company that handles the teardown typically needs to carry the file into framing, drywall, flooring, and finish work, because the reconstruction plan depends on exactly what the demo crew found once the walls opened up.

## What this means if you're facing a similar project

If you're staring down a demolition phase on your own home, the process usually runs in this order: a walkthrough to scope what has to come out, containment and protection of the areas that aren't affected, the actual teardown with daily debris removal, and then a handoff into the rebuild plan. A crew that documents what they find as they go (moisture readings, photos of framing condition, notes on anything unexpected) makes that handoff faster and keeps the reconstruction estimate accurate instead of full of change orders later.

If your project is large enough to need a dedicated demo phase, ask upfront whether the same company will carry the job through reconstruction. Coordinating that yourself across separate contractors adds time and risk to a project that's already disruptive.

## FAQ

**What happens during the demolition phase of a large restoration project?**
The demolition phase is when damaged materials, drywall, flooring, insulation, and sometimes framing, are removed to expose the full extent of the damage. Crews also protect unaffected areas with containment barriers and remove debris daily so the site stays safe and livable.

**How long does the demolition phase typically take on a large-loss job?**
It depends on the size of the loss and how much of the structure is affected, but a single room might take a few days while a whole-floor or whole-house demo can run one to three weeks. The timeline usually becomes clearer once the crew opens the walls and sees what's actually behind them.

**Do I need to hire a separate contractor for the rebuild after demolition?**
Not with Quality Contracting, Inc.'s One Call Does It All approach. The same team that handles demolition carries the project into framing, drywall, and finish work, so you're not re-explaining the job to a new contractor or coordinating separate schedules.

**What should I expect from a demolition crew while I'm still living in the home?**
Expect daily containment and clean-up, clear communication about what's being removed and why, and a crew that respects the parts of the home you're still using. A well-run demo phase should feel disruptive but organized, not chaotic.

**Does insurance typically cover the demolition phase separately from the rebuild?**
Most policies bundle demolition and reconstruction costs under the same claim once a covered loss (like a burst pipe or fire) is established, but documentation matters. A written scope and photo record from the demo phase helps support the reconstruction estimate with the adjuster.
