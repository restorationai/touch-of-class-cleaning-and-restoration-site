If water is actively spreading through your home right now, stop reading and do three things: shut off the main water supply, cut power to any flooded rooms at the breaker, and move anything irreplaceable off the floor. Once those three things are done, come back. The decisions you make in the next 24 hours will determine whether you're dealing with a straightforward cleanup or a months-long mold and structural repair — and the difference often comes down to how fast moisture gets controlled.

## Stop the Source and Make the Space Safe

Before anything else, water needs to stop entering the building. If a pipe burst, the main shutoff is usually near the water meter — in most Auburn-area homes, that's in the basement near the front foundation wall or in a utility closet. Turn it clockwise until it stops. If the source is a roof leak or a failed sump pump during a storm, you can't stop the weather, but you can redirect: use buckets, move furniture, and if the ceiling is visibly sagging and holding water, carefully puncture it with a screwdriver in one spot to drain it before it collapses.

Electricity is the second priority. Water and live circuits are a lethal combination. If any standing water is near outlets, appliances, or your electrical panel, don't wade into it — go to the breaker box and kill power to the affected area first. If the panel itself is in a flooded space, call your utility company (Eversource serves most of central Massachusetts) and ask them to cut service at the meter before you enter.

Once the source is stopped and the space is safe to enter, take a quick visual inventory. Note which rooms are affected, whether water has reached subfloor materials or just surface flooring, and whether walls feel soft or show discoloration above the waterline. This assessment matters for both your insurance claim and for prioritizing what gets dried first.

## Document Everything Before You Touch It

This step feels counterintuitive when your instinct is to start cleaning — but spend 10 to 15 minutes photographing and videoing the damage before you move a single piece of furniture. Walk every affected room. Shoot close-ups of waterlines on walls, saturated flooring, damaged belongings, and any visible structural materials. Check the ceiling below a leak even if it looks fine; water travels along joists and can pool far from the original source.

Save those files somewhere off the device — email them to yourself, upload to cloud storage, or text them to a family member. Insurance adjusters work from documentation, and photos taken after cleanup are far less useful than photos taken at the moment of loss. If you have a homeowner's policy, call your insurer as soon as the immediate safety steps are done. Most policies require "prompt notice" of a loss, and delays can complicate claims.

## Start Drying Immediately — the Mold Clock Is Running

Mold can begin colonizing wet organic material — drywall paper, wood framing, carpet backing — within 24 to 48 hours under the right conditions. In a Massachusetts summer, when indoor humidity is already elevated, that window can be even shorter. The goal in the first 24 hours isn't to finish the job; it's to remove standing water and get airflow moving before microbial growth takes hold.

Here's what you can do with equipment most people have on hand:

1. **Remove standing water** with a wet/dry shop vac. Work from the edges of the room toward the lowest point. Empty the tank frequently.
2. **Pull up saturated rugs and area rugs** and move them outside or to a garage. Wall-to-wall carpet can sometimes be saved if it's dried within 24–48 hours, but the pad underneath almost never is — it holds water like a sponge and should be cut out and discarded.
3. **Open cabinets and closet doors** in affected areas so air can circulate into spaces that would otherwise stay damp for days.
4. **Run fans across wet surfaces** — box fans aimed low, moving air parallel to the floor, work better than fans blowing straight down.
5. **Run your HVAC or a dehumidifier** to pull moisture out of the air. In summer, air conditioning helps; in winter, heat alone can dry a space if ventilation is adequate.

What you should NOT do: don't use a regular household vacuum on standing water (it will destroy the motor and potentially shock you). Don't run a gas generator inside the home to power fans. Don't blast heat above 80°F — warm, stagnant air without dehumidification actually accelerates mold growth rather than preventing it.

## What NOT to Do in the First 24 Hours

A few common mistakes can turn a manageable water loss into a much bigger project:

- **Don't assume it's dry because the surface feels dry.** Moisture migrates into wall cavities, under tile, and into subfloor layers. A surface that feels dry to the touch can have 30–40% moisture content inside the framing — enough to grow mold for weeks.
- **Don't run a ceiling fan in a room with a wet ceiling.** If the ceiling structure above is saturated, a fan can pull the ceiling down or spread contaminated water.
- **Don't use bleach on visible mold yet.** If you see mold growth in the first 24 hours, that means the moisture problem predates this event. Bleach on a porous surface like drywall kills surface mold but doesn't address the root cause, and the EPA does not recommend it as a primary remediation method on building materials.
- **Don't throw away damaged materials before the adjuster sees them** — or at minimum, before you photograph them thoroughly. Insurers sometimes dispute claims when evidence has been discarded.
- **Don't ignore a musty smell.** If you notice an earthy, damp odor within the first 24 hours, that's a sign moisture has already reached a space where airflow is limited. It warrants investigation, not air freshener.

## When the Damage Is Beyond DIY

Some water losses are genuinely manageable with shop vacs and fans — a small appliance leak caught quickly, a toilet overflow limited to a bathroom floor. But several situations call for professional water damage restoration:

- The water covered more than one room or affected more than one floor.
- The source was a sewage backup, a dishwasher drain, or any water that may have contacted waste (this is Category 2 or 3 contamination and requires different handling than clean water).
- Water sat for more than a few hours before you discovered it.
- The affected area includes finished walls, insulated ceilings, or wood subfloor — materials that trap moisture and can't be adequately dried without professional equipment.
- You see or smell mold within the first 24 hours.

Professional restoration crews use moisture meters, thermal imaging cameras, and industrial desiccant dehumidifiers that pull dramatically more water vapor from a structure than consumer units. They can also document moisture readings over time — the kind of data that supports an insurance claim and confirms that a structure is genuinely dry before repairs begin.

## The Road from Here

The first 24 hours are about stopping damage from compounding. What comes after — removing wet materials, drying the structure to baseline, repairing or replacing drywall and flooring, addressing any mold — is a process that typically takes days to weeks depending on the scope.
