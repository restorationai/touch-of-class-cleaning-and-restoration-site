**TL;DR:** Hardwood floors soaked by water can often be dried in place if restoration starts within 24-48 hours of the loss. The key decision points are how the wood is deforming (cupping, crowning, or buckling), how long it has been wet, and whether the subfloor is also saturated. Mat drying systems applied directly to the floor surface are the most effective in-place method. Floors that have buckled, delaminated, or sat wet for more than 72 hours usually require replacement.

You just found water on your hardwood floors. Maybe a pipe burst overnight, a dishwasher line failed, or a slow roof leak finally showed up on the surface. Whatever the source, you are staring at boards that may be swelling, and you want to know: can these floors be saved? The answer depends almost entirely on how fast you move and what the wood is doing right now.

## How long do you have before hardwood floors are unsalvageable?

You have a 24-48 hour window where in-place drying is still a realistic option for most solid hardwood and engineered wood floors. After 48 hours, the wood has absorbed enough moisture that significant deformation becomes likely, and after 72 hours, secondary damage like mold growth and subfloor deterioration starts compounding the problem. The [IICRC S500 standard](https://www.iicrc.org/page/IICRCS500), the industry benchmark for water damage restoration, classifies response time as one of the primary factors in whether structural materials can be restored or must be replaced.

The water source matters too. Category 1 water (clean supply-line water from a burst pipe) gives you the best odds. Category 2 water (gray water from an appliance or toilet overflow) narrows your window. Category 3 water, also called black water, includes sewage and floodwater. Hardwood floors contaminated by Category 3 water almost always require removal regardless of timing, because the contamination penetrates the wood grain and cannot be adequately sanitized in place.

If you have not already stopped the water source and documented the damage for your insurance claim, read through the [burst pipe checklist](/blog/burst-pipe-emergency-checklist/) before going further.

## What do cupping, crowning, and buckling actually mean?

These three terms describe how wet wood deforms, and they tell a restoration crew a lot about what stage the damage is in.

**Cupping** happens when the bottom of a board absorbs more moisture than the top. The edges of each board rise higher than the center, giving the floor a washboard or concave look. Cupping is the earliest deformation stage and the most recoverable. If the floor is cupped but still flat to the subfloor, in-place drying with mat systems has a high success rate.

**Crowning** is the opposite: the center of each board is higher than the edges. This usually happens after a cupped floor dries too fast on top, or when someone sands a cupped floor before it fully dries (a common and costly mistake). Crowning is harder to reverse and often requires controlled re-drying followed by sanding and refinishing.

**Buckling** means the boards have physically lifted off the subfloor, sometimes by several inches. Buckling indicates severe moisture absorption and usually means the wood has expanded past its elastic limit. Buckled boards rarely return to a flat, stable state and typically need replacement. If you are seeing buckling, the subfloor underneath is almost certainly saturated as well.

Engineered hardwood behaves differently from solid wood. It tolerates moisture fluctuations better in some ways, but once the core layers delaminate (you can hear a hollow sound when you tap the board), the floor is done regardless of how quickly drying starts.

## How do restoration crews actually dry hardwood floors in place?

The most effective method for in-place hardwood drying is a mat drying system, sometimes called a floor mat drying system or a floor drying mat. These are flat, perforated mats laid directly on the wet floor surface and connected to a desiccant or LGR (low-grain refrigerant) dehumidifier. The system draws air through the mat and directly through the wood surface, pulling moisture out from inside the boards rather than just drying the surface layer.

Mat drying systems work significantly faster than simply running air movers around the perimeter of a room. They also reduce the risk of over-drying the surface while the core of the board stays wet, which is what causes crowning. A properly configured mat drying setup typically runs for 3-5 days with daily moisture readings taken using a pin or pinless moisture meter to track progress toward the wood's equilibrium moisture content (EMC) for the region.

In addition to floor mats, a crew will typically deploy:

- **LGR dehumidifiers** to control ambient humidity in the affected rooms
- **Air movers** directed at walls and under cabinets where moisture migrates
- **Thermal imaging cameras** to locate moisture that has wicked into adjacent walls or under baseboards
- **Moisture meters** calibrated to the specific wood species to set drying targets

If there is a crawl space beneath the affected area, a technician will check it separately. Moisture that drops through the subfloor into a crawl space creates a secondary problem that mat drying alone will not solve. [Crawl space encapsulation](/services/crawl-space-encapsulation/) may be part of the recovery plan if the crawl space was already poorly sealed.

## When does hardwood floor replacement become unavoidable?

Replacement is the right call in several situations, and an honest assessment early saves money compared to a failed drying attempt followed by replacement anyway.

Replace rather than dry when:

- **Buckling is present.** Boards that have lifted off the subfloor will not lie flat again.
- **The loss is Category 3.** Sewage-contaminated wood cannot be adequately decontaminated in place.
- **The floor has been wet for more than 72 hours** and significant cupping or swelling is visible.
- **The subfloor is OSB or particleboard and is saturated.** These materials swell and lose structural integrity quickly. A restored hardwood surface over a compromised subfloor will fail.
- **Delamination is present** in engineered wood (hollow sound when tapped, visible separation at seams).
- **Mold is already visible or detected** beneath the floor or in the subfloor. Remediation comes before any flooring reinstallation.

If mold is a concern after a water loss, the [signs of hidden mold](/blog/signs-of-hidden-mold/) post covers what to look for before and during the drying process.

For insurance purposes, document everything before any work begins: photos of the deformation, moisture meter readings, the water source, and the timeline. Your adjuster will want to see that the decision to replace was supported by documented conditions, not a judgment call made without evidence. The [homeowners insurance and water damage guide](/blog/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-water-damage/) explains what most policies cover and what documentation protects your claim.

## What should you do right now if your hardwood floors are wet?

Stop the water source first if it is still active. Then call a restoration company before you start running fans or turning up the heat. Well-meaning DIY drying attempts frequently cause crowning by drying the surface too fast, and they do not address moisture in the subfloor or walls.

A restoration crew will take baseline moisture readings within the first hour on site, classify the water source, and give you a written assessment of whether in-place drying is viable or whether replacement is the honest recommendation. That assessment is what your insurance company needs to process the claim.
