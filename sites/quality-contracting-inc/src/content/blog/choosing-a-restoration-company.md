The honest answer: most people pick the wrong restoration company because they're scared and moving fast. A burst pipe, a house fire, or a patch of black mold on the basement wall creates real urgency — and contractors who specialize in disaster work know that. Some of them exploit it. This guide gives you a framework for vetting any restoration company in a few hours, even when you're stressed, so you hire someone who will actually fix the problem rather than someone who will maximize your insurance claim and disappear.

## Why the Restoration Industry Attracts Both Great Contractors and Bad Ones

Restoration work — water damage, fire and smoke cleanup, mold remediation — sits at an unusual intersection: the jobs are large, they're often insurance-funded, and the homeowner rarely has any baseline for what the work should cost or how long it should take. That's a recipe for price gouging, unnecessary scope creep, and shoddy drying that leaves moisture behind a wall for mold to find six months later.

None of that means you should be paralyzed. It means you should ask specific questions before you sign anything.

Massachusetts also has a quirk worth knowing: the state requires a Construction Supervisor License (CSL) for structural repair work above a certain threshold. If a restoration company is tearing out and rebuilding framing, subfloor, or drywall, ask to see that license. A mitigation-only crew — one that extracts water and runs drying equipment but hands reconstruction off to someone else — may operate under a different structure, but you still want to know who is doing what and who is liable for each phase.

## The Five Questions to Ask Before You Hire Anyone

These questions work whether you're calling about a flooded basement in Auburn, a kitchen fire, or a musty smell coming from the crawlspace.

**1. Are you IICRC-certified, and which technicians on your crew hold the certification?**

The Institute of Inspection, Cleaning and Restoration Certification sets the industry's technical standards — the S500 for water damage, the S520 for mold, the S700 for fire and smoke. A company can *say* it follows IICRC standards without holding the credential. Ask specifically which technicians are certified, not just whether the company is affiliated. The answer tells you whether the person actually touching your property knows what they're doing.

**2. Do you carry general liability and workers' compensation insurance, and can you send me a certificate of insurance today?**

A legitimate contractor will email you a COI within the hour. If they hedge — "we're covered, don't worry" — that's a red flag. Workers' comp matters because restoration work involves ladders, confined spaces, and wet floors. If an uninsured worker is injured on your property, you may be liable.

**3. Will you bill my insurance company directly, or do I pay you and get reimbursed?**

Direct billing is standard for established restoration firms. It's not a dealbreaker if they don't offer it, but it does shift paperwork burden onto you during an already stressful time. More importantly, ask how they document the loss — moisture readings, thermal imaging, photo logs. Good documentation protects you if the insurance company disputes the claim later.

**4. What does your drying protocol look like, and how do you know when the structure is actually dry?**

This is the technical question that separates real restoration contractors from extraction-only crews. The answer should involve psychrometric readings, moisture mapping with a pin or pinless meter, and a target moisture content for the specific material (wood subfloor, drywall, concrete slab). If they say "we run fans for a few days and check back," that's not a protocol — that's guesswork. Inadequately dried materials are the single most common cause of mold growth after a water loss.

**5. Who handles reconstruction, and is that the same company or a subcontractor?**

Many restoration companies do mitigation (demo, drying, cleaning) and then hand off to a separate rebuild crew. There's nothing wrong with that model, but you need to know who is responsible for what, and you need both parties' names in writing before work begins. A single contractor who handles both mitigation and reconstruction can simplify communication and accountability significantly.

## Red Flags That Should Make You Walk Away

These aren't hypothetical — they're patterns that show up repeatedly in insurance fraud cases and contractor complaints filed with the Massachusetts Office of Consumer Affairs.

- **They show up unsolicited after a storm or news event.** Storm chasers follow disaster coverage. A contractor who knocks on your door 48 hours after a major weather event and pressures you to sign an Assignment of Benefits (AOB) form on the spot is transferring your insurance rights to them, not to you. Never sign an AOB without your insurance agent reviewing it first.
- **The estimate is verbal or vague.** Any legitimate scope of work for a restoration project should be itemized — line by line, with quantities and unit costs. Xactimate is the industry-standard estimating software; most insurance carriers expect to see estimates in that format.
- **They can't tell you their license number.** In Massachusetts, you can verify a contractor's CSL status through the state's online license lookup. A company that fumbles or deflects on this question likely has something to hide.
- **They guarantee mold will never come back.** No one can promise that. Mold remediation removes the existing colony and addresses the moisture source. Future mold growth depends on future moisture conditions. Any contractor who offers a lifetime guarantee against mold is either misinformed or misleading you.
- **They push you to skip the insurance company.** Sometimes contractors suggest you take a cash payment from them and not file a claim. This is occasionally legitimate for very small losses where the repair cost is close to your deductible, but if someone is actively discouraging you from involving your insurer on a large loss, ask yourself why.

## What to Do in the First 24 Hours While You're Still Vetting Contractors

For water damage, the clock matters. Mold can begin colonizing wet organic material — drywall paper, wood framing, carpet backing — within 24 to 48 hours under the right temperature and humidity conditions. You don't have to wait for the perfect contractor to start protecting your property.

1. Stop the source if you can. For a burst pipe, that means the main shutoff valve. For a roof leak, that means a tarp if it's safe to place one.
2. Move wet belongings off saturated flooring. Furniture sitting in water will wick moisture upward and create secondary damage.
3. Document everything with photos and video before you move or discard anything. Your insurance claim depends on this.
4. Call your insurance company to open a claim. You're not committing to anything — you're starting the process and getting a claim number.
5. Get at least two written estimates before signing a contract with anyone.

For fire damage, ventilate if it's safe, but don't start cleaning smoke residue yourself. Dry soot smears when wiped and can permanently stain surfaces that would otherwise be restorable. Leave that work to a crew with the right chemical sponges and cleaning agents.

For mold, don't disturb a large colony without containment in place. Cutting into a mold-affected wall without negative air pressure and proper PPE can spread spores throughout the house.

## The Longer Recovery: What Comes After Mitigation

Most homeowners are surprised to learn that restoration is a two-phase process. Mitigation — stopping the damage and stabilizing the structure — is phase one. Reconstruction is phase two, and it's often where projects stall.

A good restoration contractor will give you a reconstruction estimate before mitigation is complete, so you're not left with a gutted bathroom and no timeline for putting it back together. Ask for a projected completion date in writing, and ask what happens to that date if hidden damage is discovered during demo (it often is).

For mold remediation specifically, a post-remediation verification (PRV) clearance test — conducted by an independent industrial hygienist, not the same company that did the work — is the only objective way to confirm the remediation was successful. Some contractors will push back on this because it adds cost and creates accountability. That pushback itself is useful information.
