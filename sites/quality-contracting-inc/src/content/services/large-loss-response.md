When a structure fire takes out three floors of a mill-building conversion, or a burst main floods forty units in a multi-family complex overnight, the damage stops being a single-crew job. Large loss and catastrophic response is what happens when a loss is big enough, or spread across enough square footage, that it needs a coordinated team working multiple zones at once, not a van and a dehumidifier. This is the work for property managers, commercial owners, and large-loss adjusters who need a contractor that can scale.

## What large loss and catastrophic response actually involves

A large loss is defined less by the peril and more by its scope: multiple units or buildings affected, six or seven figures in projected damage, or a loss significant enough that the carrier assigns a large-loss adjuster or general adjuster instead of a standard desk adjuster. That could be a commercial roof failure that floods three floors of office space, a fire that spreads through shared attic space in a row of triple-deckers, or storm damage across an entire apartment complex. The work pulls in multiple crews simultaneously, often running water extraction in one wing while demo and board-up happen in another, with a project manager coordinating schedule, trades, and documentation across the whole site rather than one address at a time.

## Our process

1. **Site assessment and stakeholder coordination.** We walk the full scope with the property manager, owner, GC, and adjuster present where possible, and map the affected structures or units before any equipment moves in.
2. **Securing the property.** Board-up, fencing, temporary power, and structural shoring go in first on any site where occupants, contents, or adjacent units are at risk.
3. **Parallel mitigation by zone.** Extraction, demo, and drying run simultaneously across separately affected areas instead of sequentially, which is the main thing that separates a large-loss timeline from a standard one.
4. **Scope documentation for the carrier.** Every zone gets its own photo record, moisture logs, and line-item scope so the large-loss adjuster can review and approve work in stages rather than waiting on a single final estimate.
5. **Reconstruction sequencing.** Once mitigation clears, trades are scheduled in the order that lets occupied or partially occupied buildings come back online fastest, unit by unit or floor by floor.

## What separates a good large loss response from a bad one

The most common failure on a catastrophic job is treating it like a big version of a small job: one crew, one schedule, one point of contact trying to cover a dozen units. That creates bottlenecks, and bottlenecks on a large loss mean carrying costs the owner didn't budget for. A second common mistake is undocumented scope creep. On a loss this size, adjusters expect itemized, zone-by-zone documentation, not a lump estimate, and gaps in that paper trail are where claims stall or get disputed. A third is skipping engineering review on structural questions, moisture intrusion into load-bearing assemblies, fire damage to framing members, or snow-load related roof stress, which can turn into a much bigger problem once finishes go back on. Good large-loss response means a dedicated project manager, phased work that keeps unaffected units occupiable where safe, and documentation built for the adjuster from day one, not reconstructed afterward.

## What does large loss and catastrophic response cost?

Costs on large-loss work vary enormously because the scope varies enormously, a flooded basement in one building and a multi-structure fire are both "large loss" but nowhere near the same number. Every loss gets a written scope of work before anything starts, because estimating a catastrophic job off a phone call isn't realistic for anyone. Most commercial and multi-family policies cover large-loss mitigation and reconstruction under the same business or property policy that covers a smaller claim, though limits, sub-limits for things like ordinance and law upgrades, and business interruption coverage vary by policy.

| Scenario | Typical range |
|---|---|
| Multi-unit water loss, 10-20 units affected | $150,000 - $500,000 |
| Commercial fire, single structure | $300,000 - $1,500,000+ |
| Multi-building storm or wind damage | $500,000 - $3,000,000+ |
| Partial structural collapse or roof failure | $1,000,000+ |
| Large-loss reconstruction only (post-mitigation) | $250,000 - $2,000,000+ |

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Worcester County's winters bring the two conditions that most often turn a bad situation into a catastrophic one: heavy snow load on flat and low-slope commercial roofs, and sudden freeze-thaw cycles that split pipes in the older mill buildings and triple-deckers common throughout Auburn and the surrounding area. A pipe failure in an occupied multi-family building during a cold snap can affect a dozen units before anyone notices standing water above a finished ceiling. Spring thaw and Nor'easter season bring the storm-driven version of the same problem, roof and window failures across whole complexes rather than a single unit.
