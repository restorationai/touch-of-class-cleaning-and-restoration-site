Upholstery holds onto odor, oil, and dust in ways carpet doesn't, because the fill and frame trap what the surface fabric absorbs. ## What Upholstery Cleaning actually involves

Upholstery cleaning isn't a once-over with a handheld extractor. Fabric type drives the whole approach: natural fibers like cotton, linen, and silk blends react differently to moisture and heat than synthetics like polyester or olefin, and leather needs a completely separate process from either. A technician identifies the fiber content and dye stability first, because the wrong cleaning method on the wrong material can set a stain permanently or cause shrinkage, bleeding, or watermarking that wasn't there before.

Most jobs run on a hot water extraction method, sometimes called furniture steam cleaning, where a cleaning solution is worked into the fabric and then extracted along with the loosened soil using a low-moisture upholstery tool. Leather upholstery cleaning uses a different track entirely: pH-balanced cleaners, conditioning treatments, and manual work to restore suppleness without stripping the finish. A standard sofa or sectional typically takes under two hours from setup to finish, with dry time running a few hours depending on fill density and airflow in the room.

## Our process

1. **Fabric and fiber identification.** Before anything touches the furniture, we check the manufacturer's cleaning code (W, S, WS, or X) and test the fiber and dye in an inconspicuous spot.
2. **Pre-treatment of stains and traffic areas.** Ground-in soil along armrests, cushion seams, and headrests gets targeted pre-treatment based on what's causing the discoloration, whether that's body oil, food residue, or pet contact.
3. **Cleaning by material type.** Fabric upholstery cleaning generally uses hot water extraction with a low-moisture setting to prevent over-wetting the padding underneath. Leather gets cleaned and conditioned by hand with products matched to the leather's finish (aniline, semi-aniline, or protected/pigmented).
4. **Extraction and grooming.** Fabric nap gets groomed in one direction to even out the finish and speed drying; leather gets buffed to bring back sheen without leaving a greasy residue.
5. **Dry time and final inspection.** We check for even drying, watch for wicking (where a stain resurfaces as it dries), and walk the piece with you before we call it done.

## What separates a good upholstery cleaning response from a bad one

The most common mistake is over-wetting. Pushing too much moisture into a cushion without adequate extraction leaves padding damp for days, which invites mildew odor and can cause the wood frame underneath to swell or the glue joints to loosen. A rushed job also skips the pre-inspection step, meaning a technician applies a general-purpose cleaner to a delicate silk blend or an untested leather finish and ends up with a bleed mark or a stiff patch that wasn't there before.

Color bleeding and shrinkage are the two failure modes that show up after the fact, sometimes a day or two later once the fabric has fully dried. A careful operator tests dye stability first and adjusts water temperature and dwell time accordingly rather than running every piece through the same setting. On leather, skipping the conditioning step after cleaning is the fastest way to end up with cracking along stress points like the seat edge and armrests within a few months.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

New England humidity swings matter more for upholstery than people expect. During Auburn's humid summer stretches, damp cushions take longer to dry fully, which is part of why we run low-moisture extraction rather than saturating the fabric. In winter, closed-up houses with the heat running can trap dust and pet dander in upholstery fibers faster, which is why fabric that looked fine in October can look noticeably duller by February.
