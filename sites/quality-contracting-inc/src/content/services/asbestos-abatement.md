A popcorn ceiling that's been painted over, a basement with crumbling pipe insulation from the 1960s, old vinyl floor tile that's starting to curl at the seams: these are the quiet asbestos risks hiding in Auburn's older housing stock. Many homes built before the mid-1980s still carry asbestos-containing materials in ceiling texture, floor tile mastic, siding, and boiler or pipe wrap. The material itself isn't dangerous until it's disturbed, cut, sanded, or crumbled, which is exactly what happens during a renovation, a water leak repair, or a DIY ceiling scrape.

## What Asbestos Abatement actually involves

Asbestos abatement is the controlled removal or encapsulation of asbestos-containing material so fibers don't become airborne. The scope depends entirely on what's being removed: a single popcorn ceiling in a small bedroom is a different job than stripping asbestos floor tile across a finished basement, or removing deteriorated pipe insulation that's been shedding fibers into a mechanical room for years. Work happens inside sealed plastic containment with negative air pressure, using HEPA-filtered equipment to keep fibers from spreading into the rest of the house. Depending on the material and square footage, a project can run from a single day for a small ceiling to several days for a whole-house tile or insulation removal.

## Our process

1. **Inspection and sampling.** We identify suspect material (ceiling texture, tile, mastic, insulation, siding) and collect samples for lab testing to confirm whether asbestos is present and at what concentration.
2. **Written scope and permitting.** Once results come back, we put together a scope that covers the specific material, square footage, and disposal method, and file the required state notifications before work starts.
3. **Containment setup.** The work area is sealed with polyethylene sheeting, negative air machines are installed, and a decontamination chamber is set up for crew entry and exit.
4. **Removal and HEPA filtration.** Material is wetted down to limit fiber release, removed by hand or controlled tool work, and bagged in labeled asbestos waste containers while HEPA air scrubbers run continuously.
5. **Clearance and manifested disposal.** The area is inspected (and air-tested where required) before containment comes down, and waste is transported and disposed of through a licensed facility with a documented manifest trail.

## What separates a good asbestos response from a bad one

The most common mistake isn't in the removal itself, it's skipping the sampling step. A contractor who assumes a ceiling or tile isn't asbestos without lab confirmation is gambling with air quality for everyone in the house afterward. The second most common issue is poor containment discipline: negative air machines placed incorrectly, plastic sheeting that isn't actually sealed at seams, or crews moving between contaminated and clean areas without going through decon. Insurance adjusters and inspectors tend to look for the same things: lab results on file, a written scope that matches the invoice, proper waste manifests, and, where applicable, documentation that state notification requirements were met before demolition began. A rushed job might look clean on the surface and still leave fiber contamination in HVAC ductwork or wall cavities that weren't part of the original containment.
