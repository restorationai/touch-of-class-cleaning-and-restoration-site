## Restoration emergencies common in Wellesley

Wellesley sits in a part of eastern Massachusetts where the ground holds water. The town's topography — rolling hills draining into the Charles River watershed — means that heavy rain events push water toward foundations fast, especially on lots where the grade has settled over decades. Winters here regularly cycle through freeze-thaw patterns that stress pipe joints, crack masonry, and force ice dams along rooflines. A single January thaw after a hard freeze can send water through an attic ceiling before a homeowner realizes the dam has formed.

The housing stock amplifies these risks. A large share of Wellesley's single-family homes were built between the 1920s and the 1960s, and those structures carry the vulnerabilities of their era: full basements with stone or brick foundations, older cast-iron and galvanized supply lines, and plaster-and-lath wall assemblies that absorb moisture quietly and hold it long after the surface reads dry. Mold can begin colonizing wet organic material within 24 to 48 hours — in a plaster wall cavity, that process is invisible until the smell arrives or a renovation opens the wall.

**Mold remediation** in older Wellesley homes often traces back to a slow leak that went unnoticed — a dripping supply line behind a vanity, a failed window flashing, or chronic basement humidity. **Reconstruction** rounds out the scope. When water, fire, or mold remediation leaves framing, drywall, flooring, or trim in need of replacement, the rebuild happens under the same project rather than requiring a homeowner to find a separate contractor.

## Building stock, site conditions, and permits in Wellesley

Most of Wellesley's residential housing was built before 1970, with a substantial concentration from the 1920s through the 1950s. These homes typically have full basements — poured concrete or stone-and-mortar foundations — rather than slabs, which means water intrusion almost always involves below-grade spaces. Wall assemblies in this era are commonly plaster over wood lath, which holds moisture differently than modern drywall and requires longer drying times and more careful moisture mapping.

Plumbing in pre-1960 homes often includes galvanized steel supply lines that have narrowed with corrosion over decades, and cast-iron drain stacks. Both are prone to failure in ways that can go undetected until a significant leak develops. Homes of this age may also contain asbestos-containing materials in floor tile, pipe insulation, or joint compound — a restoration scope that involves demolition needs to account for that before work begins.

The soils in the greater Wellesley area include glacial till with clay-heavy layers that drain slowly and transmit hydrostatic pressure against foundation walls during wet seasons.
