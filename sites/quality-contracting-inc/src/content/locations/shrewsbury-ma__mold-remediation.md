## Why Shrewsbury Properties See Mold Issues

Shrewsbury's position directly across Lake Quinsigamond from Worcester means it catches the same humid continental weather — cold, wet winters followed by muggy summers — but with an added wrinkle: the town's lakefront lots often have walk-out or daylight basements that are partially below grade. That design puts concrete block or poured foundation walls in near-constant contact with groundwater pressure, especially during spring snowmelt and the heavy rain events that roll through central Massachusetts between March and May.

The postwar housing stock compounds the issue. Many capes and ranches built in the 1950s and 1960s in areas like Edgemere used fiberglass batt insulation in rim joists and crawl spaces without a proper vapor barrier. When that insulation gets wet — from a slow foundation leak, a failed sump, or condensation off a cold basement wall — it holds moisture against wood framing for weeks. Mold colonization can begin in as little as 48 hours under those conditions, and by the time a homeowner notices a musty smell, the growth is often behind a finished wall or under a subfloor, not visible on the surface.

Commercial properties along the White City area and the Route 9 corridor face a different pattern: flat or low-slope roofs on retail and mixed-use buildings where ponding water and aging membrane seams allow slow intrusion into ceiling assemblies. That kind of hidden saturation is particularly stubborn because HVAC systems can spread spores through ductwork before anyone realizes there's a problem.

## Our Mold Remediation Process in Shrewsbury

Every job starts with a thorough visual inspection and moisture mapping — we use thermal imaging and calibrated moisture meters to locate wet building materials behind finished surfaces before any demolition begins. This step matters especially in Shrewsbury's older homes, where original plaster-and-lath walls can mask moisture pockets that drywall-era construction would reveal more readily.

Once we've mapped the affected area, we establish negative-air containment using 6-mil poly barriers and HEPA-filtered negative air machines. This keeps spores from migrating to unaffected rooms during the removal process — a critical step in occupied homes and in properties where HVAC returns are nearby. Contaminated materials (insulation, drywall, subfloor sections) are bagged and removed per Massachusetts Department of Environmental Protection guidelines for mold waste.

After physical removal, affected structural surfaces are HEPA-vacuumed and treated with an EPA-registered antimicrobial agent. We then run commercial-grade dehumidification and air scrubbing until moisture readings in the structural wood return to acceptable levels — typically below 16% for framing lumber. We document conditions throughout with photographs and moisture logs, which become part of the remediation report we provide at job close.
