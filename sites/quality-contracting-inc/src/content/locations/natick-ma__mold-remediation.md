## Why Natick Properties See Mold Issues

Natick's housing stock tells a lot of the story. A significant portion of the town's single-family homes were built between the 1940s and 1970s, when vapor barriers were either minimal or absent entirely. Basement walls in those homes are often uninsulated concrete block — porous enough to wick groundwater during prolonged wet periods. When that moisture migrates inward and meets a finished basement ceiling or wood framing, the conditions for mold growth are essentially ideal.

The town's proximity to Lake Cochituate and the smaller ponds and wetlands scattered throughout the area also means that the water table in lower-lying neighborhoods can rise significantly after sustained rainfall. Homes with slab-on-grade construction or shallow foundations are particularly susceptible. Even a slow, undetected plumbing leak inside a wall cavity — the kind that shows no visible staining for weeks — can produce enough sustained humidity to support a mold colony well before a homeowner notices a musty odor.

Natick also sees its share of ice dam damage each winter. When heat escapes through an attic and melts roof snow, the meltwater refreezes at the cold eaves and backs up under shingles. That water finds its way into wall assemblies and attic insulation, and by the time it's discovered in spring, mold growth in the attic sheathing or along the top plates of exterior walls is already underway.

## Our Mold Remediation Process in Natick

Every remediation starts with a thorough inspection — not just the visible surface where mold is apparent, but the surrounding building materials, HVAC pathways, and any area where moisture could have traveled. Moisture meters and thermal imaging help locate saturation behind surfaces that look dry to the eye. That step matters in Natick's older homes especially, where plaster walls and original wood lath can hold moisture deep in the assembly while the painted surface appears fine.

Once the scope is established, the affected area is isolated with physical containment barriers and placed under negative air pressure using HEPA-filtered air scrubbers. This prevents spores from migrating to unaffected parts of the home during the removal process. Contaminated materials — drywall, insulation, wood framing where necessary — are removed, bagged, and disposed of properly. Remaining structural surfaces are treated and dried to documented moisture levels before any reconstruction begins.

The final step is clearance. We don't consider a job complete because the visible mold is gone. The area needs to meet acceptable spore-count thresholds before containment comes down and the space is returned to use.

## Local Note

One pattern worth knowing about Natick specifically: homes built in the 1950s and 1960s along the older residential streets near Lake Cochituate frequently have partial fieldstone or poured concrete foundations with no interior drainage system — just the original construction. When those foundations are finished with wood framing and drywall in a later renovation, the assembly traps any moisture that migrates through the foundation wall. Mold in those spaces often reads dry at the drywall surface because the paper facing dries first, while the wood studs behind it remain saturated. Standard surface moisture readings can be misleading; probe measurements into the framing are essential before concluding that drying is complete.
