## Our Water Damage Restoration Process in Millbury

Every job starts with a moisture mapping inspection — thermal imaging and pin-type meters across walls, subfloor, and framing — before a single piece of equipment is placed. In Millbury's older stock, that step is especially important because water migrates laterally through plaster lath and stone mortar in ways that aren't visible to the eye. What looks like a wet corner can be a saturated wall cavity running eight feet in either direction.

Once the scope is clear, we move to water extraction using truck-mounted and portable units calibrated for the volume and contamination level present. Structural drying follows with commercial-grade desiccant and refrigerant dehumidifiers, axial air movers positioned to create directed airflow through cavities, and daily moisture readings logged against a drying goal. We don't pull equipment until the numbers confirm the structure has returned to its pre-loss moisture baseline — not before, because residual moisture left behind is what feeds mold colonization within 24 to 72 hours of initial saturation.

Documentation runs parallel to the physical work: photos, moisture logs, and a written scope that your insurance adjuster can work from directly.

## Local Note: What Millbury's Stone Foundations Change About Drying

One pattern that comes up repeatedly in the older sections of Millbury — particularly on the streets that run toward the Blackstone River — is that stone foundation walls don't dry the same way poured concrete does. Stone and mortar assemblies hold moisture in the voids between courses long after the visible surface feels dry to the touch. Standard drying timelines built around modern construction will underestimate what's needed here. In practice, that means extending the dehumidification phase, repositioning equipment to direct airflow into the lower wall cavity, and taking readings at multiple depths rather than just at the surface.
