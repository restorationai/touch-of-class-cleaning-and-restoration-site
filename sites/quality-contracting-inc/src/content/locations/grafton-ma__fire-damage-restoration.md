## Why Grafton Properties Present Unique Fire Damage Challenges

Grafton's housing stock spans nearly three centuries, and that variety shapes how fire damage behaves and how restoration has to respond. The 18th and 19th-century homes clustered around Grafton Center and along the roads radiating from the Common were built with dense old-growth timber framing, fieldstone foundations, and thick plaster-and-lath walls. When fire moves through that structure, it travels differently than it does in modern stick-frame construction — char patterns are less predictable, and smoke infiltrates wall cavities that weren't designed with vapor barriers or sealed penetrations.

The mill-village homes in Fisherville and Farnumsville, many built to house workers along the Blackstone River corridor in the late 1800s, often have balloon-frame construction. In a balloon-frame house, wall cavities run continuously from the basement sill plate to the roof rafters with no fire blocking between floors. That means smoke and heat can travel vertically through the wall long after the visible fire is out, leaving hidden char and soot deposits that only show up when walls are opened during remediation.

Newer commuter subdivisions near the Grafton MBTA station — built largely in the 1990s and 2000s — use platform framing with engineered lumber and synthetic materials that produce different combustion byproducts. Melted foam insulation, PVC wiring jackets, and composite flooring release chemicals that require specific cleaning protocols and extended air scrubbing.

## Our Fire Damage Restoration Process in Grafton

The first priority after a fire is stabilizing the structure. That means boarding windows and doors, tarping any roof breaches, and assessing load-bearing members before anyone walks the interior. Once the site is secured, the real work begins in a defined sequence.

**Damage assessment and documentation** comes first. Every affected room is photographed and mapped — ceiling, walls, floor, contents — with notes on smoke migration, water intrusion from suppression, and structural integrity. This documentation drives both the scope of work and the insurance claim.

**Debris removal and demo** follows. Unsalvageable materials — charred framing, saturated insulation, smoke-saturated drywall — are removed to clean substrate. In older Grafton homes with plaster walls, this step requires judgment: intact plaster that hasn't absorbed smoke can sometimes be cleaned and preserved rather than demolished, which matters both for historic character and for cost.

**Soot and smoke cleaning** is the most labor-intensive phase. Dry soot from fast-burning fires is swept and HEPA-vacuumed before any wet cleaning; applying water or chemical cleaners to dry soot first drives it deeper into porous surfaces. Wet smoke residue from slow, smoldering fires — common when upholstered furniture or insulation is involved — requires alkaline cleaners and extended dwell time. Thermal fogging or hydroxyl generation addresses embedded odor in wall cavities and HVAC systems.

**Structural drying** runs concurrently with cleaning when suppression water is present. Industrial dehumidifiers and air movers are positioned to dry framing and subfloor before reconstruction begins.

**Reconstruction** closes the job — framing repairs, insulation, drywall, finish work, and painting — to bring the home back to pre-loss condition.

## Local Note: Plaster Walls and Smoke Absorption in Older Grafton Homes

In the historic homes around Grafton Common and along the older village roads, original horsehair plaster is still common. Plaster is more alkaline and denser than modern drywall, which changes how it interacts with smoke residue. Dry soot can be surface-cleaned from intact plaster more effectively than from paper-faced drywall, which tends to absorb and stain. However, if suppression water has reached the plaster, it can delaminate from the lath in sheets days after the fire — a failure mode that isn't always obvious on initial inspection. Restoration crews working in these homes need to probe for soft spots and monitor plaster adhesion through the drying phase, not just assess it once at the start.
