## Why Wellesley Properties Present Unique Biohazard Considerations

Wellesley's housing stock skews older. A significant share of the town's single-family homes were built before 1960, and many retain original plaster-and-lath walls, hardwood subfloors over unventilated crawl spaces, and cast-iron or galvanized plumbing. These materials absorb biological contaminants differently than modern construction — porous plaster and aged hardwood can harbor pathogens well below the surface, meaning a cleanup that looks complete at the surface may not be. Thorough remediation in these homes requires moisture readings, careful material assessment, and in some cases the removal of structural materials that cannot be adequately disinfected.

The town's proximity to the Charles River watershed also means that soil and groundwater are closely regulated. Biohazardous waste generated during cleanup — including contaminated materials, sharps, and disposables — cannot be handled as ordinary construction debris. Proper containment, manifesting, and transport to a licensed medical waste facility is required under Massachusetts Department of Public Health guidelines, and that chain of custody matters both legally and for the health of anyone who enters the property afterward.

## Our Biohazard Cleanup Process in Wellesley

Every job begins with an assessment before any work starts. The affected area is identified, contained with physical barriers and negative air pressure where appropriate, and access is restricted. This protects the rest of the property — including adjacent rooms, HVAC systems, and shared spaces in multi-unit buildings — from cross-contamination.

Cleaning and disinfection follow a structured sequence: removal of contaminated porous materials, application of EPA-registered disinfectants to all affected surfaces, and verification testing where warranted. Sharps and needle cleanup requires puncture-resistant containment and specific disposal protocols separate from other biohazardous waste. Odor control is addressed as part of the remediation, not as an afterthought — residual odor in an older Wellesley home can persist in plaster, wood trim, and subfloor cavities if not treated at the source.

Documentation is produced throughout: photographs, a written scope of work, and a disposal manifest. This record matters for insurance claims and for any future disclosure obligations on the property.
