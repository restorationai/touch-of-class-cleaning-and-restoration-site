## Why Newton Properties See Mold Issues

Newton sits in a part of eastern Massachusetts where freeze-thaw cycling is a real structural stressor. Every winter, water infiltrates small cracks in foundation walls, window frames, and flashing; every spring thaw releases that moisture inward. Older homes — and Newton has a substantial stock of homes built before 1950 — often have rubble-stone or brick foundations that wick ground moisture continuously. Crawl spaces under these structures rarely have adequate vapor barriers, and unfinished basements with stone walls stay cool and humid even in summer, giving mold spores a near-ideal environment year-round.

Newton's tree canopy, while beautiful, contributes too. Gutters clogged with oak and maple debris redirect water against fascia boards and soffits. In neighborhoods like Waban and Chestnut Hill, where mature trees overhang older rooflines, we see recurring moisture intrusion at the eave line that feeds attic mold long before it becomes visible from inside the living space.

Beyond the structure itself, Newton's older plumbing stock matters. Homes with galvanized supply lines or cast-iron drain stacks are more prone to slow pinhole leaks inside wall cavities — the kind that read dry at the surface while the framing behind the plaster stays saturated for months.

## Our Mold Remediation Process in Newton

Every project starts with a thorough assessment — not just a visual scan, but moisture mapping with a calibrated meter and, where warranted, thermal imaging to find cold spots behind walls where condensation accumulates. In Newton's older homes, this step matters more than in new construction because the building envelope is rarely simple: you may have plaster over wood lath over balloon-frame studs, each layer holding moisture differently.

Once the scope is defined, containment goes up before any disturbing work begins. Negative air pressure with HEPA filtration isolates the affected area so spores don't travel to clean parts of the home during removal. Affected porous materials — drywall, insulation, sections of wood framing with active growth — are removed, bagged, and disposed of properly. Non-porous surfaces are treated with EPA-registered antimicrobial agents.

Drying is the step that gets rushed elsewhere and shouldn't be. In Newton's climate, especially during humid summer months or after a wet New England spring, ambient moisture levels slow structural drying considerably. We monitor moisture readings daily and don't close up walls until readings confirm the substrate has reached acceptable levels — because sealing damp framing is how mold comes back.

## Newton Insurance & HOA Coordination

Mold claims in Massachusetts can be complicated. Homeowners' policies vary significantly in how they treat mold — some cover remediation when the underlying cause (a burst pipe, storm-driven water intrusion) is a covered peril; others exclude mold explicitly or cap coverage. We document the loss thoroughly: photographs, moisture readings, scope of affected materials, and a written assessment that gives your adjuster what they need to evaluate the claim.

Newton also has a number of condominium associations and multi-family buildings where the line between unit-owner responsibility and common-area responsibility isn't always clear. If you're dealing with mold that crosses that boundary — say, a leak from a shared roof that affected your unit's ceiling — we can help you document what's in your space while you work through the association's process.

## Local Note

Newton's pre-1950 plaster-and-lath construction behaves differently from modern drywall during mold remediation. Plaster is denser and less porous than drywall, so surface mold on plaster can sometimes be addressed without full demolition — but the wood lath behind it is another story. Lath holds moisture longer than the plaster face suggests, and mold that has colonized the lath is invisible until you open the wall. In Newton's older neighborhoods, we build extra time into the assessment phase specifically to probe behind plaster surfaces before finalizing a scope, because underestimating lath involvement is one of the most common reasons mold returns after remediation.
