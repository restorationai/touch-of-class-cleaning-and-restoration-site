## Why Framingham Properties Present Unique Fire Damage Challenges

The same dense residential patterns that make Framingham one of Middlesex County's most populated communities also shape how fire damage spreads and lingers. Older homes built before modern vapor barriers were standard tend to absorb smoke odor into wood framing, plaster lath, and masonry in ways that synthetic materials don't. That absorption isn't just cosmetic — smoke residue is chemically active, and in humid New England air it continues to corrode metal fixtures, discolor surfaces, and off-gas long after the fire is out.

Framingham also sits in a climate zone where late-winter and early-spring fires — often started by heating equipment running hard through a cold snap — are followed immediately by damp, cool conditions. That combination slows the drying of water used in suppression and creates a narrow window where secondary mold growth can establish itself inside walls that were soaked by hose lines. Addressing fire damage here means thinking about water damage at the same time, not as a separate job.

## Our Fire Damage Restoration Process in Framingham

The first step on any fire loss is a thorough scope — not just the rooms with visible damage, but the adjacent spaces where smoke migrated through HVAC ducts, wall chases, and attic voids. In Framingham's older balloon-frame homes, that migration path is rarely obvious from the surface.

From there, the process moves through board-up and structural stabilization to protect the property while the full extent of damage is documented. Smoke and soot removal follows, using dry chemical sponges for dry smoke residue and wet-cleaning methods for the oily, sticky residue that protein fires (kitchen grease, for example) leave behind — those two residue types behave completely differently and require different chemistry. Odor neutralization comes next, using thermal fogging or hydroxyl generation to reach the same surfaces smoke penetrated, not just the ones visible to the eye. Structural repairs — framing, sheathing, drywall, plaster where it can be matched — close out the job.

Throughout, every affected material is photographed and documented in a format that supports insurance claims, which matters when you're working with a carrier that requires line-item scope before authorizing repairs.

## Working with Insurance on Framingham Fire Claims

Fire losses in Massachusetts are covered under standard homeowners policies, but the scope of what gets approved — and how quickly — depends heavily on documentation. Carriers want to see photos of every affected surface, a written scope that distinguishes between smoke-damaged materials that can be cleaned and those that require replacement, and evidence that emergency services (board-up, tarping, water extraction from suppression) were performed promptly to prevent further loss.

## Local Note

Framingham's older neighborhoods include a meaningful number of homes with original plaster-and-lath interior walls rather than drywall. After a fire, these walls present a specific challenge: plaster is dense enough that smoke odor can penetrate several inches into the substrate, and the lath behind it — often old-growth wood — holds odor compounds tenaciously. Simply repainting over cleaned plaster is rarely sufficient. In our experience with New England homes of this era, encapsulation with an odor-blocking primer is a necessary step before any finish coat, and in severe cases the lath itself needs to come out. Skipping that step is the most common reason a homeowner notices smoke smell returning months after a "completed" restoration.

If your Framingham home was built before 1960, ask specifically about plaster-specific odor treatment when you call — it changes the scope and the timeline.
