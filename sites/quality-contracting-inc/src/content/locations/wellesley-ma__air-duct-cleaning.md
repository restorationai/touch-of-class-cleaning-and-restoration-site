## Why Wellesley Homes See Duct Buildup Faster Than You'd Expect

Wellesley sits in a part of eastern Massachusetts where seasonal humidity swings are pronounced. Summers bring stretches of high dew-point air that the air conditioning system pulls across the evaporator coil and, if there's any gap in the duct sealing, into the duct cavity itself. That moisture doesn't evaporate cleanly — it settles on accumulated dust and creates conditions where mold spores and bacteria can colonize. Come fall, when the system switches to heat, that same biological material gets pushed through every room in the house.

Homes in Wellesley's established residential neighborhoods — many with original cast-iron radiator systems that were converted to forced air at some point — often have duct runs that travel through uninsulated attic spaces or crawlspaces. Those transitions between conditioned and unconditioned zones are exactly where condensation forms on the duct exterior and, eventually, works its way through seams. The result is duct interiors that are damp, dusty, and in some cases harboring growth that a standard furnace filter never touches.

Dryer vent lines in these older homes are another common problem. Original construction often routed dryer vents through long, horizontal runs with multiple elbows before reaching an exterior wall — configurations that trap lint and restrict airflow far more than a short, straight run would. A partially blocked dryer vent isn't just an efficiency problem; it's a fire hazard that building inspectors in Massachusetts take seriously.

## Our Air Duct Cleaning Process in Wellesley

The process begins with a camera inspection of the main supply and return trunks before any equipment is connected. This step matters in older Wellesley homes because duct systems from the mid-20th century sometimes have internal baffles, dampers, or flex-duct patches added by previous owners that aren't visible from the register openings. Knowing the layout before applying negative pressure prevents dislodging material into areas that can't be reached.

Once the system is mapped, we connect a high-powered vacuum collection unit to the main trunk and work each branch line with agitation tools — rotary brushes and compressed-air whips — that break up compacted debris rather than just surface dust. The vacuum maintains negative pressure throughout, so loosened material travels toward the collection unit rather than back into the living space. Supply registers, return grilles, and the air handler cabinet are cleaned as part of the same visit. If dryer vent cleaning is needed, that's handled as a separate line item with its own inspection and cleaning pass.

For systems where biological growth is confirmed or strongly suspected, we can apply an EPA-registered antimicrobial treatment to duct interiors after the mechanical cleaning is complete. This is not a substitute for physical debris removal — it's a follow-on step for situations where conditions in the duct have been favorable to microbial growth.
