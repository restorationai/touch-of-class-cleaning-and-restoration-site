## Why Wellesley Properties See Sewage Backup Issues

The soil beneath much of Wellesley is glacially deposited — dense, low-permeability clay and silt that drains slowly. During sustained rainfall or snowmelt, that soil stays saturated for days, and the hydrostatic pressure it generates against older clay-tile or cast-iron laterals is relentless. Roots from Wellesley's mature tree canopy — the same oaks and maples that make the town's residential streets so distinctive — are drawn to the moisture around aging joints, and once they find a hairline crack, they expand it season by season.

The town's older neighborhoods contain homes built in the 1920s through the 1950s, many of which have never had their original sewer laterals replaced. Those clay-tile sections are brittle and prone to offset joints. When a municipal main backs up during a storm event, the weakest point in the system is usually that aging private lateral — and the overflow ends up in the basement. Homes with finished lower levels, which are common throughout Wellesley's residential stock, face a compounding problem: sewage doesn't just pool on a concrete slab; it saturates carpet padding, penetrates drywall, and soaks into the framing before anyone realizes the scope.

## Our Sewage Cleanup and Sanitization Process in Wellesley

Raw sewage is classified as Category 3 water — the highest contamination level — and the process for addressing it is more involved than a standard water loss. The sequence matters.

**Containment and assessment first.** Before extraction begins, affected areas are isolated to prevent cross-contamination to unaffected spaces. In Wellesley's older homes, this is especially important because open floor plans and balloon-frame construction can allow contaminated air to migrate freely.

**Extraction and removal.** Standing sewage and saturated porous materials — carpet, padding, drywall below the flood line, insulation — are removed. Porous materials that have contacted Category 3 water are not salvageable and are disposed of properly.

**Structural drying.** After gross contamination is removed, the underlying framing, subfloor, and concrete are dried using commercial dehumidifiers and air movers. In Wellesley's finished basements, this often means drying wall cavities and rim joist areas that aren't visible from the surface.

**Sanitization and deodorization.** Every affected surface is treated with EPA-registered antimicrobial agents. Sewage carries bacteria, viruses, and parasites; surface drying alone does not address biological contamination. Odor treatment follows, targeting the compounds that persist in porous substrates even after visible contamination is removed.

## Local Note

Wellesley's finished basements — particularly in the larger Colonials and Tudors built between the 1930s and 1960s — frequently have plaster-and-lath wall systems below grade rather than the paper-faced drywall common in newer construction. Plaster itself is more water-resistant than drywall and sometimes survives a sewage event structurally intact, but the lath behind it and the wood framing behind the lath can hold moisture and biological contamination long after the surface reads dry on a pin meter.
