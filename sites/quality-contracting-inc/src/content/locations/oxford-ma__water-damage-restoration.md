## Why Oxford Properties See Water Damage Issues

Oxford's geography is the starting point for understanding why water intrusion is such a persistent problem here. The Hodges Village Dam and Buffumville Lake exist precisely because the French River valley has a long history of flooding — the Army Corps of Engineers built those flood-control structures after repeated flood disasters, and they do their job, but they don't eliminate the risk for homes on lower ground. When the river runs high, the water table in surrounding soil rises with it, and that hydrostatic pressure finds its way through foundation walls, floor cracks, and window wells.

The housing stock compounds the issue. Many homes in North Oxford and Oxford Center were built in the 1940s through 1960s, when basement waterproofing standards were minimal. Block foundations common in that era are porous by nature — they wick moisture even without a visible crack. Newer subdivisions that pushed into hillier terrain along the I-395 and Route 12 corridor have their own challenges: grading that drains toward the foundation rather than away from it, and sump pits that weren't sized for back-to-back storm events.

Indoor plumbing failures add to the picture year-round. Supply lines to older fixtures, corroded copper in homes that haven't been replumbed, and washing machine hoses that quietly weaken over years are responsible for a significant share of the calls we receive from the Texas Village area and surrounding streets.

## Our Water Damage Restoration Process in Oxford

When we arrive at an Oxford property, the first priority is stopping the source — whether that's locating a burst pipe, clearing a backed-up floor drain, or confirming the sump pump has failed. Once the source is controlled, we use truck-mounted extraction equipment to pull standing water from floors, carpets, and crawlspaces before it has time to migrate further into wall cavities or subfloor assemblies.

Moisture mapping comes next. Thermal imaging cameras and calibrated moisture meters let us trace water movement behind drywall and under hardwood without tearing open every surface unnecessarily. This matters in Oxford's older homes, where original plaster walls and tongue-and-groove subfloors hold moisture differently than modern materials — and where unnecessary demolition adds cost without adding drying speed.

Structural drying follows a measured protocol: industrial air movers and refrigerant dehumidifiers are positioned based on the moisture readings, not just placed at random. We monitor readings daily and adjust equipment placement as the structure dries. Most residential losses in this area reach drying goals within three to five days, though homes with plaster walls or thick subfloor assemblies sometimes require additional time. Every reading is logged so that your insurance adjuster has a complete drying record.

## Local Note: What the Flood-Control Dams Don't Tell You

Homeowners near Buffumville Lake sometimes assume that because the dam is upstream, their property is protected from river-related flooding. That's partially true for surface flooding — but it doesn't account for groundwater. When the lake and river levels are elevated for an extended period, the surrounding soil becomes saturated, and that saturation can persist for days after the surface water recedes. Basements in low-lying parts of Oxford can take on water through the floor slab or block walls even when there's no visible flooding on the street. If your basement has dried out on its own after past events, it may have dried incompletely — residual moisture in block walls and concrete is enough to support mold colonization within 24 to 48 hours.
