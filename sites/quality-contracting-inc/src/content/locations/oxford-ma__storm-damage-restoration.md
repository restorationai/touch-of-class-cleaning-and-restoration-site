## Why Oxford Properties See Repeated Storm Damage

Oxford sits in a river valley that was engineered around flooding — which tells you something about how much water this area moves during a serious storm. The French River and its tributaries collect runoff from the surrounding hills quickly, and even homes that aren't in a mapped flood zone can find water pushing against foundation walls or pooling in crawl spaces within hours of a heavy rain event. The Hodges Village Dam upstream provides some buffer, but it doesn't eliminate the pressure that low-lying lots absorb after a fast-moving storm drops two or three inches in an afternoon.

The housing stock adds another layer of complexity. Many capes and ranch-style homes built in Oxford in the 1950s and 1960s used materials — asphalt shingles with minimal pitch, older aluminum flashing, single-pane windows — that weren't designed for the wind speeds modern storms deliver. When a tree limb punches through a low-slope roof or wind lifts a section of flashing along a dormer, interior water intrusion can spread across an attic in minutes before anyone notices. Newer subdivisions on the town's eastern edge tend to have better envelope details but often sit on larger lots with mature trees — which means more exposure to falling limbs during high-wind events.

## Our Storm Damage Restoration Process in Oxford

The first priority after any storm event is stopping the damage from compounding. That means emergency tarping or board-up on breached roofs and broken windows before any interior work begins. Once the structure is stabilized, the assessment phase looks at every system the storm touched: roofing, siding, soffit, gutters, windows, and — critically — what water made it inside and how far it traveled.

Water that enters through a storm breach behaves differently from a plumbing leak. It often carries debris, follows gravity through insulation and wall cavities, and can reach subfloor framing before it's visible on a ceiling below. The team uses moisture meters and thermal imaging to trace the actual migration path, not just the obvious wet spots. Affected materials are documented with photographs before anything is removed — a step that matters when you're filing a homeowner's insurance claim and need to show the adjuster exactly what the storm caused versus what was pre-existing.

Drying equipment — commercial dehumidifiers and air movers — is sized to the affected area and monitored over multiple days. Restoration work (framing repairs, sheathing replacement, roofing, interior finishes) follows only after moisture readings confirm the structure is dry. Skipping that sequence is how mold problems develop weeks after a storm, and it's a pattern we see in homes where the visible damage was patched quickly without addressing what was hidden inside the wall or ceiling assembly.
