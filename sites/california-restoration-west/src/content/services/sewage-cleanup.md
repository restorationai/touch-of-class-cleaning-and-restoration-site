Sewage backups are classified as Category 3 water, meaning the water is carrying bacteria, viruses, and waste that can make a home unsafe within hours, not days. Whether the cause is a blocked sewer lateral, a failed septic tank, or a municipal line surcharge during a storm, the longer contaminated water sits against drywall, flooring, and cabinetry, the more material has to come out.

## What sewage cleanup and sanitization actually involves

Sewage cleanup is not the same scope as a clean-water leak. Because the water carries pathogens such as E. coli and hepatitis A, every porous material it touches, carpet, carpet pad, baseboard, drywall below the waterline, insulation, is treated as contaminated and removed rather than dried in place. Crews work in full PPE (gloves, respirators, protective suits) under OSHA bloodborne pathogen guidelines, extract standing wastewater with trash pumps built for solids, and bag contaminated debris for proper disposal. Once the source is confirmed stopped, typically a plumber clears the line or the septic issue is addressed, the real work is sanitization: antimicrobial application, enzymatic treatment to break down organic waste, and HEPA air scrubbing to pull odor-causing particulates out of the air. A straightforward single-room backup is often extraction and demo in one day, with drying running several additional days; a whole-house event or crawlspace intrusion takes longer.

## Our process

1. **PPE staging and containment.** The affected area is sealed off from the rest of the home before anyone enters, limiting the spread of contaminated water and airborne particulates.
2. **Raw sewage extraction and contaminated material removal.** Standing wastewater is pumped out first, then saturated porous materials, carpet, pad, baseboard, drywall up to a flood-cut line, are removed and bagged, not dried.
3. **Antimicrobial and enzymatic treatment.** Remaining surfaces are treated with hospital-grade antimicrobial product and enzymatic cleaners that break down residual organic waste rather than just masking odor.
4. **Structural drying and moisture monitoring.** Remaining structure is dried and monitored per IICRC S500 guidelines, with daily moisture readings until subfloor and framing return to a dry standard.
5. **Clearance check before reconstruction.** Odor, surface, and moisture checks confirm the space is sanitary before drywall, flooring, or cabinetry go back in.

## What separates a good sewage cleanup response from a bad one

The most common mistake is treating a sewage event like a regular water loss: drying carpet or drywall that should have been cut out and discarded. Carpet and pad that have absorbed blackwater cannot be sanitized to a safe standard and should always be removed, not dried. A second common miss is not pulling toe kicks and baseboards to check for wicking behind cabinetry, which leaves hidden contamination and odor that resurfaces weeks later. Crews also need to identify the actual source, a sewer line blockage versus a failing septic tank versus municipal backup, because that determines whether a plumber needs to clear the line before cleanup can safely proceed, and it affects how the loss gets documented for insurance. Adjusters reviewing sewage claims generally want photos of the contamination level, a list of what was removed and why, and moisture logs showing the structure dried to standard, which is also the paperwork that protects a homeowner if mold or odor issues come up later.
