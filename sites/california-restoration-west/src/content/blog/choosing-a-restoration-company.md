## The Short Answer: Vet Before You're Desperate

The worst time to choose a restoration company is at 11 p.m. with water spreading across your living room floor. Contractors who know that will charge accordingly. The best defense is knowing what to look for *before* you need it — and knowing the red flags that signal a company will make a bad situation worse. This post walks you through the questions to ask, the credentials to verify, and the contract terms that should give you pause, whether you're dealing with water damage, fire and smoke residue, or a mold problem that's been quietly growing behind drywall.

## Why Restoration Is Different From a Normal Contractor Hire

Hiring a plumber or a roofer is stressful enough. Hiring a restoration contractor is harder for a few reasons that don't get talked about enough.

First, you're often doing it under duress — a burst pipe, a kitchen fire, a smell you can't locate. Urgency is the enemy of good decision-making, and some contractors exploit it.

Second, the work is largely invisible. Drying equipment runs inside walls. Mold remediation happens behind containment barriers. You can't easily tell whether a crew did the job right until months later, when a musty smell returns or a wall starts to bubble.

Third, the insurance billing relationship adds a layer of complexity. The contractor, your adjuster, and your policy language all have to align. A company that isn't fluent in that process can leave you holding costs you thought were covered — or, worse, can overbill in ways that complicate your claim.

Ventura's housing stock makes this especially relevant. A lot of homes here were built in the 1950s through 1980s, when plaster-over-lath construction and galvanized steel supply lines were standard. Both age poorly. Galvanized pipe corrodes from the inside out, and plaster walls can read dry at the surface on a moisture meter while the wood lath behind them stays saturated for weeks. A crew without the right equipment or training can miss that entirely, sign off on a dry structure, and leave you with a mold problem six weeks later.

## The Credentials That Actually Matter

Not every certification in the restoration industry is meaningful, but a few are worth asking about directly.

**IICRC certification** is the industry standard. The Institute of Inspection, Cleaning and Restoration Certification sets the technical protocols — the S500 standard for water damage, the S520 for mold remediation — that define what a proper dry-out or remediation looks like. Ask whether the company's technicians (not just the owner) hold current IICRC credentials. Technicians turn over; a company that certified one person in 2015 and hasn't invested since is not the same as one with a certified crew.

**State contractor licensing** matters for reconstruction work. In California, any contractor performing structural repairs — replacing drywall, reframing, restoring flooring — is required to hold a CSLB license. You can verify a license number at the California State License Board's public database in about 60 seconds. If a company can't give you a license number, don't let them touch your walls.

**Insurance** should include both general liability and workers' compensation. If a technician is injured on your property and the company doesn't carry workers' comp, your homeowner's policy may be on the hook. Ask for a certificate of insurance, not just a verbal confirmation.

For mold work specifically, ask whether the company follows EPA guidelines for remediation and whether they use a third-party clearance test after the work is complete. A reputable remediator welcomes independent verification; one who discourages it is telling you something.

## Red Flags to Watch For Before You Sign Anything

These patterns show up often enough that they're worth naming explicitly.

- **Pressure to sign a direction-to-pay or assignment-of-benefits form immediately.** These documents transfer your insurance claim rights to the contractor. Some states have banned them outright for this reason. California has not, but signing one before you understand the scope of work removes your leverage if a dispute arises.
- **No written scope of work before equipment is placed.** Drying equipment rental is one of the largest line items in a water damage claim. A company that places dehumidifiers and air movers before giving you a written estimate is creating costs you haven't agreed to.
- **Unusually fast mold clearance.** Mold remediation done correctly takes time — containment setup, HEPA vacuuming, antimicrobial treatment, air scrubbing, and then a waiting period before clearance testing. If a company tells you they can remediate and clear a significant mold problem in a single day, ask them to walk you through the protocol step by step.
- **No mention of moisture readings or drying logs.** Legitimate water damage restoration generates documentation: daily moisture readings at multiple points, equipment settings, ambient temperature and humidity. This documentation protects you with your insurer and proves the work was done to standard. If a company doesn't mention it, ask for it explicitly.
- **Storm chasers after a declared disaster.** After events like the Thomas Fire or the atmospheric river flooding that periodically hits the Ventura River basin, out-of-state contractors arrive in force. Some do good work. Many are not licensed in California, don't carry California-compliant insurance, and will be unreachable if problems emerge after they leave.

## Questions to Ask Before You Hire

Treat this like a brief interview. A company doing good work will answer these without hesitation.

1. **Are your technicians IICRC-certified, and can you show me their credentials?** Not just the company — the people who will be in my home.
2. **What is your CSLB license number?** (For any company doing reconstruction, not just mitigation.)
3. **Do you bill insurance directly, and do you have experience with my carrier?** This matters. Some carriers have preferred vendor lists; others don't. A contractor who has worked with your insurer before knows what documentation the adjuster will require.
4. **Will I receive daily drying logs or a written remediation protocol?** Yes is the right answer.
5. **Who performs the work — your employees or subcontractors?** Neither answer is automatically wrong, but you should know. If subcontractors are used, ask whether they're vetted and whether the primary contractor remains responsible for the work quality.
6. **What does your warranty cover, and for how long?** Workmanship warranties vary. Get it in writing.

## What the Recovery Process Actually Looks Like

Understanding the timeline helps you evaluate whether a contractor's proposal is realistic.

For **water damage**, the mitigation phase — extracting standing water, removing unsalvageable materials, and drying the structure — typically runs three to five days for a standard loss, longer for large areas or materials like concrete subfloors. The structure has to reach documented drying goals before reconstruction begins. Rushing that step is how mold problems start.

For **fire and smoke damage**, the timeline depends heavily on the type of fire. Protein fires (cooking fires, for example) leave a nearly invisible residue that penetrates porous surfaces and is notoriously difficult to deodorize. Synthetic material fires leave heavy soot that can migrate through HVAC systems. A thorough assessment before any cleaning begins is not optional — it determines the entire scope of work.

For **mold remediation**, expect containment to go up before any disturbance of mold-affected material. Disturbing mold without containment spreads spores to unaffected areas. After remediation, a clearance test by an independent industrial hygienist — not the same company that did the work — is the only objective confirmation that the problem is resolved.
