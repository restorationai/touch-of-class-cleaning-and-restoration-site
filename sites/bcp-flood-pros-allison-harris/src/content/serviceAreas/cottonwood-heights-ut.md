## Restoration emergencies common in Cottonwood Heights

Cottonwood Heights sits at the mouth of Big and Little Cottonwood Canyons, which shapes almost every weather-driven loss in the area. Snowmelt in late March and April pushes groundwater against foundation walls and window wells faster than most drainage systems can handle. Ice dams form on rooflines during the hard freeze-thaw cycles of January and February, forcing meltwater under shingles and into attic insulation or wall cavities before a homeowner notices anything on the ceiling below. Summer monsoon cells, brief but intense, overwhelm gutters and window wells on the south-facing slopes. And because a significant portion of the housing stock dates to the 1960s through the 1980s, older plumbing systems fail at higher rates than newer construction, making supply-line and drain failures a year-round concern.

Wildfire smoke from canyons and surrounding ranges is also a seasonal reality. When smoke infiltrates a home through HVAC systems or open windows during a fire event, the odor and particulate residue require the same methodical cleaning approach as a structural fire.

## Building stock, site conditions, and permits in Cottonwood Heights

Most of Cottonwood Heights was developed between the late 1950s and the mid-1980s, with a meaningful share of that housing built on full basements, a foundation type common in the Salt Lake Valley where frost depth and sloped terrain both favor below-grade space. Basement construction means water intrusion paths are different from slab-on-grade: water enters through cracks in poured concrete or block walls, through window wells, and through floor-wall joints. Plumbing in homes of this era typically includes copper supply lines and cast-iron drain stacks, both of which age in predictable ways. Copper develops pinhole leaks; cast iron corrodes and cracks. Any scope involving pre-1980 construction should account for the possibility of asbestos-containing materials in floor tile, pipe insulation, and textured ceilings.
