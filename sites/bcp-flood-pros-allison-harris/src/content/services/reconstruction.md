A burst pipe gets dried out in a few days. A kitchen fire gets the soot wiped down and the odor treated in a week. But then the drywall is still gone, the subfloor is still exposed plywood, the cabinets are still ripped out, and the bathroom you can't use is still a bathroom you can't use. That gap between mitigation ending and your house being livable again is where reconstruction services come in, and it's often the longest, least understood part of a property loss.

## What reconstruction services actually involve

Post-damage reconstruction is the rebuild phase that starts once water, fire, or mold damage has been mitigated and the structure is dry, clean, and safe to work in. It covers everything from replacing a single section of drywall and baseboard to full structural reconstruction: framing, subfloor, electrical and plumbing rough-in, insulation, drywall, paint, flooring, trim, and cabinetry. Fire damage reconstruction tends to go deeper than water-related rebuilds because char and heat often compromise framing members, not just finishes, which means a structural engineer's sign-off may be needed before framing can be rebuilt. Timelines vary widely: a single-room drywall and flooring job might run one to two weeks, while a post-disaster rebuild involving multiple rooms, permits, and inspections can take several months from scope approval to final walkthrough.

## Our process

1. **Scope of work and documentation.** We walk the affected areas room by room, document every material that was removed or compromised during mitigation, and write a scope that matches what your insurance adjuster will review.
2. **Permits and code review.** Any structural work, electrical, plumbing relocation, or load-bearing framing changes gets pulled through the proper permitting process before a hammer touches the wall.
3. **Framing and rough-in.** Damaged studs, joists, or subfloor are replaced to match existing structure, and electrical, plumbing, and HVAC rough-in is inspected before anything gets closed up.
4. **Insulation, drywall, and finishing.** Walls are insulated, hung, taped, and textured to match the surrounding surfaces, followed by paint, trim, and flooring.
5. **Final walkthrough and inspection sign-off.** We confirm the finished work against the approved scope and make sure any required inspections are closed out before calling the job complete.

## What separates a good reconstruction response from a bad one

The most common mistake is starting the rebuild before the structure is verifiably dry or before hidden damage has been fully scoped. Closing up a wall over framing that still reads elevated moisture, or over studs with unaddressed char, just moves the problem behind new drywall where it's harder and more expensive to find later. A second common gap is mismatched materials: subfloor height, trim profiles, or cabinet depths that don't match the original construction, which creates visible seams and can trigger a second round of adjuster scrutiny. Adjusters are generally looking for a scope that lines up line-item with what was actually removed during mitigation, photo documentation at each phase, and proof that any structural repair was either permitted or didn't require it. Reconstruction that skips the paperwork trail tends to create disputes at claim closeout, not during the work itself.

## What does reconstruction cost?

Reconstruction costs vary more than almost any other restoration service because the scope ranges from a patch job to a full structural rebuild. Most homeowners pay based on square footage, the number of trades involved (framing, electrical, plumbing, drywall, flooring), and whether permits and inspections are required. Every loss is different, and FIX Restoration provides a written scope of work with itemized pricing before any reconstruction begins. Homeowners insurance typically covers reconstruction that is directly tied to a covered peril, such as fire or a qualifying water loss, but it generally won't cover unrelated upgrades or code-driven changes beyond what's required to restore the space to its prior condition.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

American Fork and the surrounding Utah County bench sit at the base of the Wasatch Range, and many homes here were built from the 1970s through the 2000s on slab or crawlspace foundations with standard wood framing. That construction era generally rebuilds in a straightforward way, but permitting timelines through local building departments can affect how fast a reconstruction project moves, especially for projects that touch structural framing or electrical panels. Winter cold can also slow drywall mudding and paint curing times if a home's heat was affected by the original loss, which is worth planning for if your rebuild falls between late fall and early spring.
