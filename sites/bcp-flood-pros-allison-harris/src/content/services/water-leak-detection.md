**Is your water bill climbing with no obvious cause, or does one spot on the drywall stay damp no matter how many times you wipe it down?** That's usually a hidden leak, not a one-time spill, and it rarely announces itself. Water can travel along a joist, down a wall cavity, or through a slab for weeks before it surfaces somewhere visible, which means the stain you're looking at is often feet away from the actual source.

## What Water Leak Detection Actually Involves

Leak detection is diagnostic work, not demolition. The goal is to find the source of moisture without tearing out more drywall, flooring, or slab than necessary. That means acoustic listening devices to pick up the sound of water moving through supply lines, infrared thermal imaging to spot temperature differences where water is cooling a wall or ceiling cavity, and moisture meters to map how far damp material actually extends. For suspected slab leaks, pressure testing on the plumbing system isolates whether the loss is on the hot or cold line before anyone cuts into concrete. A straightforward wall leak might take under an hour to pinpoint. A slab leak under a finished floor, or a leak inside a multi-layer exterior wall, can take longer because the signal has to be read through more material.

## Our Process

1. **Symptom intake.** We ask about water bill history, where staining or odor first appeared, and when it started, since timing narrows down which line or fixture is the likely culprit.
2. **Non-invasive scanning.** Thermal imaging and acoustic equipment scan the suspect area first. This is how we avoid opening walls or flooring on a guess.
3. **Moisture mapping.** A moisture meter traces the actual spread of saturation through framing, subfloor, or slab, which tells us how far the damage has already traveled, not just where it's visible.
4. **Pinpoint confirmation.** For slab or in-wall leaks, pressure testing and, where needed, a small access point confirm the exact source before any larger repair work is scoped.
5. **Written findings.** You get a clear report of what was found, where, and what it will take to access and repair it, so you're not guessing what happens next.

## What Separates a Good Leak Detection Response From a Bad One

The most common mistake is cutting open the nearest wall to the stain and hoping. Water doesn't travel in straight lines; it follows the path of least resistance along framing members, insulation, and subfloor, so the entry point is frequently not where the damage shows up. A rushed inspection also skips checking whether the water is clean supply-line water or has picked up contamination from a drain line, which changes both the health risk and the restoration scope.

Insurance adjusters look closely at whether the source was actually located and documented, or whether a contractor just dried out visible damage and moved on. A leak that isn't found tends to come back, and a second claim for the same root cause is a much harder conversation with a carrier than one well-documented find. Good detection work includes photos, moisture readings, and a written explanation of the source, because that paper trail is what supports the claim and prevents a repeat call six months later.

## What Does Water Leak Detection Cost?

Costs vary with how accessible the leak is and what equipment the job requires. Every property is different, so FIX Restoration provides a written scope before any work begins rather than a flat number over the phone. Homeowners insurance will often cover the resulting damage from a sudden leak, but the detection itself and the cost to access the leak (cutting into a wall or slab) are sometimes treated differently by a policy, so it's worth checking your declarations page.

## Seasonal & Regional Considerations

American Fork and the surrounding Utah Valley see hard freeze-thaw cycles each winter, and a pipe that cracked slightly during a cold snap in January often doesn't show a visible leak until the ground thaws and water starts moving again. Many homes in this area were built on slab foundations with copper supply lines now decades old, and copper pinhole leaks inside a slab are one of the more common calls we get from older neighborhoods. Expansive clay soils common along the Wasatch Front can also shift slab foundations slightly over time, stressing underground plumbing joints in ways that show up as a slow, hard-to-trace leak rather than a sudden break.
