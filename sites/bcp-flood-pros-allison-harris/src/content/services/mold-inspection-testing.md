## What mold inspection and testing actually involves

A musty smell in a basement that won't go away, a water stain on drywall that keeps coming back, or a family member with unexplained allergy symptoms that ease up on vacation and return at home: these are the signals that prompt most mold inspection calls. Mold inspection and testing is a diagnostic process, not a cleanup job. It answers two questions before anyone touches a wall: is there an active mold problem, and if so, where is it and how far has it spread.

A proper inspection combines a visual and moisture survey with targeted sampling. That means a moisture meter run along baseboards, window frames, and subfloor areas, a thermal imaging pass to spot temperature differences that hint at trapped moisture behind finished surfaces, and air or surface sampling sent to an independent, third-party laboratory for analysis. Results typically take two to five business days depending on the lab. The inspection itself usually runs one to three hours depending on the size of the home and whether crawl spaces or attics need to be accessed.

## Our process

1. **Visual walkthrough and symptom mapping.** We ask about the history: past leaks, flooring, HVAC changes, and where odors or symptoms are worst, then walk the structure room by room.
2. **Moisture and thermal survey.** Moisture meters and thermal imaging identify wet building materials and temperature anomalies that aren't visible to the eye, including behind tile, under cabinets, and inside wall cavities.
3. **Sampling strategy.** Depending on what the walkthrough turns up, we collect air samples (to measure airborne spore counts and compare indoor versus outdoor baseline), surface swabs or tape lifts (for visible growth), or both.
4. **Lab analysis.** Samples go to an independent laboratory, not an in-house lab, so results aren't influenced by who's selling the remediation work.
5. **Written report and recommendation.** You get a report with spore counts, species identified where relevant, moisture readings, and a clear recommendation: no action needed, monitor, or remediate, with an outline of scope if remediation is warranted.

## What separates a good mold inspection response from a bad one

The biggest mistake in this industry is skipping independent lab verification and instead eyeballing a wall and calling it mold, or worse, selling remediation off a visual guess alone. Mold species matter less to most homeowners than moisture source does: if the inspector doesn't identify and document the water intrusion point, whatever gets remediated will likely come back.

Another common gap is testing only one room when symptoms suggest whole-house air quality issues. Spore counts vary significantly room to room and floor to floor, especially in homes with forced-air HVAC systems that circulate spores between levels. A thorough inspection tests enough locations to build an accurate picture, and compares indoor air samples against an outdoor control sample taken the same day, since mold spores are present in all outdoor air and the comparison is what makes indoor results meaningful.

Insurance adjusters, when a claim is involved, look for documentation that separates the original covered peril (a burst pipe, a roof leak) from pre-existing or maintenance-related moisture issues. A report that clearly ties moisture readings to a specific intrusion event, with photos and meter readings timestamped, holds up far better during claims review than a vague "mold was found" summary.

Costs vary based on square footage, the number of samples collected, and whether lab analysis is billed separately. ## Seasonal & regional considerations

Utah's dry climate keeps overall ambient humidity lower than coastal regions, but that works against homeowners in one specific way: basements and crawl spaces in American Fork and the surrounding Utah Valley foothill communities often have condensation issues from swamp coolers, humidifiers run during dry winter months, or grading that channels snowmelt toward foundations each spring. Homes built before the 1990s commonly lack vapor barriers in crawl spaces, which creates a steady low-level moisture source that doesn't show up until an inspector actually gets under the house with a meter. Spring runoff and irrigation season (roughly April through September) are when we see the most moisture-related inspection requests tied to foundation seepage and sprinkler overspray against siding.
