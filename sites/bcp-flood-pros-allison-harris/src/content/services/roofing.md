A roof that looks fine from the curb can be hiding failing underlayment, cracked decking, or flashing that has long since separated from the chimney or dormers. By the time water shows up on a ceiling, the damage underneath is usually months old. Whether you're replacing a roof that has reached the end of its service life or installing new roofing on an addition or new build, the quality of what goes under the shingles matters just as much as what you see from the street.

## What roofing installation and replacement actually involves

A full roof replacement is more than pulling off old shingles and nailing down new ones. The work begins at the deck: inspecting and replacing any rotted or delaminated sheathing before a single layer of new material goes on. From there, a proper installation includes a code-compliant underlayment (typically a synthetic or self-adhering ice-and-water barrier in Utah's climate zones), correctly lapped and fastened so wind-driven rain and snowmelt cannot migrate underneath. Drip edge, valley flashing, step flashing around penetrations, and ridge ventilation all have to be sequenced correctly, each one sets up the next.

On a standard single-family home, a full replacement typically takes one to two days for the tear-off and installation crew, with a follow-up inspection to confirm ridge cap alignment, fastener patterns, and flashing seals. New construction installations follow a similar sequence but are coordinated around framing and rough inspection schedules.

Material choices affect both cost and longevity: architectural (dimensional) asphalt shingles are the most common choice in American Fork and the broader Utah Valley area, but metal roofing, concrete tile, and synthetic slate each have specific installation requirements that differ from standard shingle work.

## Our process

1. **Roof assessment and scope development.** Before any material is ordered, a detailed inspection documents the current deck condition, existing layers, flashing status, ridge and soffit ventilation, and any penetrations (plumbing vents, HVAC, skylights). The written scope identifies what is being replaced versus what can be retained, and flags any structural repairs needed before installation begins.

2. **Material selection and permitting.** FIX Restoration pulls the required building permit with Utah County or the relevant municipality. Material selection is confirmed at this stage, including shingle manufacturer, color, and any upgraded underlayment or ventilation components. Permit requirements in Utah Valley typically include a final inspection by the local building department.

3. **Tear-off and deck preparation.** Existing roofing is removed down to the sheathing. Every square foot of decking is inspected; soft spots, delamination, and rot are cut out and replaced with matching thickness material before anything else proceeds. This step is non-negotiable: new roofing installed over compromised decking will fail prematurely regardless of shingle quality.

4. **Underlayment, flashing, and installation.** Ice-and-water shield is applied at all eaves, valleys, and penetration points. Synthetic underlayment covers the field. Drip edge is installed to code. Shingles are laid in the manufacturer's specified pattern with the correct fastener count per shingle, a detail that directly affects wind-resistance ratings and warranty validity. All flashing is set in roofing sealant and mechanically fastened.

5. **Final inspection and cleanup.** A post-installation walkthrough confirms fastener patterns, ridge cap alignment, flashing seals, and that all penetrations are properly counter-flashed. Magnetic rollers are used across the yard and driveway to recover roofing nails. The building department inspection is scheduled and the permit is closed out.

## What separates a good roof installation from a bad one

The most common failure point in residential roofing is flashing, not shingles. Step flashing at walls and dormers, counter-flashing at chimneys, and valley flashing at roof intersections are where water finds its way in. Contractors who use continuous bent flashing instead of properly overlapped step flashing, or who rely on sealant alone without mechanical fastening, are setting up a leak that may not show up for two or three seasons.

Fastener count is the second most-missed detail. Most architectural shingles require four to six nails per shingle depending on the roof pitch and wind zone. Under-fastened shingles can void the manufacturer's wind warranty and are the first to lift in a storm. Insurance adjusters reviewing storm damage claims often look at fastener patterns when determining whether a failure was installation-related or weather-related.

Ventilation is the third area where shortcuts cost homeowners money. Inadequate attic ventilation causes heat to build up under the roof deck in summer and moisture to accumulate in winter, both of which shorten shingle life and can cause decking to delaminate. A properly designed system balances intake at the soffits with exhaust at the ridge.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Utah Valley's climate creates a specific set of roofing challenges. Freeze-thaw cycles through late fall and early spring put stress on flashing seals and can force water under shingles that have even minor lifting at the edges. Ice damming is a real risk on lower-pitched sections of roofs that face north or are shaded by mature trees, particularly in neighborhoods along the Wasatch foothills where snow sits longer. Ice-and-water shield installed well above the eave line is the correct response, not just at the first course.

Hail is the dominant insurance claim driver in Utah County. Hailstorms tracking northeast from the west desert can deposit large-diameter hail with minimal warning, and granule loss from hail impact is not always visible without getting on the roof. If your neighbors are having roofs replaced after a storm, it is worth having your own roof inspected before filing a claim deadline passes.

Summer installation is generally straightforward in American Fork's dry climate, but high-UV exposure means shingles should be installed with the manufacturer's recommended exposure and not left on the pallet in direct sun for extended periods before installation.
