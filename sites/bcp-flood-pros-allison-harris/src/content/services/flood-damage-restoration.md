## What flood damage restoration actually involves

Flood cleanup starts with identifying the water's source and contamination level, which changes almost everything about how the job is scoped. Water from a clean supply line is handled differently than water that backed up from a sewer line or ran off a flooded street, lawn, or canal bank. Because floodwater is typically classified as Category 2 or 3 (contaminated), a larger share of porous materials, carpet, carpet pad, baseboard, and sometimes the bottom section of drywall, usually cannot be dried in place and has to come out. Equipment on a flood job tends to run bigger and longer than on a simple leak: truck-mount extraction units pull standing water first, then industrial air movers and low-grain-refrigerant dehumidifiers run for several days while moisture readings are tracked in the wood framing, subfloor, and any remaining wall cavities.

## Our process

1. **Water categorization and safety check.** Before anyone starts extracting, we identify the water source and contamination level, which determines what protective equipment the crew uses and what materials can realistically be saved.
2. **Standing water extraction.** Pumps and truck-mount vacuums remove bulk water from floors, carpet, and any pooling in basements or crawlspaces, which in flood scenarios is often the largest volume of water on the job.
3. **Removal of contaminated porous materials.** Carpet, pad, baseboard, and insulation that have been in contact with contaminated floodwater are removed and bagged for disposal rather than dried, since drying them does not remove the contamination risk.
4. **Structural drying and monitoring.** Air movers, dehumidifiers, and daily moisture meter readings track drying progress in studs, subfloor, and masonry, following the drying benchmarks laid out in the IICRC S500 water damage standard.
5. **Antimicrobial application and documentation.** Affected framing and remaining surfaces are treated to limit microbial growth, and the entire process, from initial moisture maps through final dry readings, is photographed and logged for the insurance claim.

## What separates a good flood response from a bad one

The most common mistake on flood jobs is treating them like a routine water leak: drying carpet and pad that should have been removed, or skipping behind baseboard and inside wall cavities where contaminated water sits longest. Adjusters reviewing flood claims look for documented moisture readings at multiple points over several days, not just a single before-and-after photo, because that's what proves the structure was actually dried to standard rather than just dried on the surface. Another frequent gap is failing to separate flood water claims from standard water damage claims early: many homeowners' policies exclude ground-level flooding entirely, which is covered instead under a separate flood policy, so correctly documenting the water's origin from day one matters for how the claim gets paid.

## What does flood damage restoration cost?

Costs vary widely depending on how much standing water was involved, how long it sat before cleanup started, and how much porous material has to be removed versus dried in place. Every loss is scoped individually, and FIX Restoration provides a written scope before any work begins so there are no surprises once the drying equipment is running. Homeowners insurance typically covers sudden, accidental water damage from things like a failed appliance or pipe, while ground-level or overland flooding is usually only covered under a separate flood insurance policy, so it's worth confirming which type of coverage applies before assuming what's paid.

American Fork sits below the Wasatch Range, and spring snowmelt runoff from American Fork Canyon has historically pushed water levels up in local creeks and the irrigation canal network that runs through Utah Valley neighborhoods.
