A storm can leave a roof open to the sky, a tree pinning a wall, and standing water spreading across hardwood floors, all at the same time. What makes storm damage uniquely complicated isn't any single problem; it's the layered sequence of them. Wind lifts shingles, rain follows through the gap, and within 24 to 48 hours, the moisture that soaked into framing and insulation is already creating conditions for mold. Getting the sequence of repairs right, and documented correctly for your insurance carrier, is what storm damage restoration actually demands.

## What Storm Damage Restoration actually involves

Storm damage restoration spans three overlapping phases: emergency stabilization, water and debris mitigation, and structural repair. Each phase has its own equipment and timeline, and skipping ahead costs money.

Emergency stabilization means stopping additional loss. That's tarping a compromised roof before the next rain cell arrives, boarding windows where glass failed, and securing any structural elements that are unstable. This work happens in hours, not days, because Utah County weather doesn't pause while you wait for a contractor.

Mitigation follows immediately: extracting standing water with truck-mounted or portable extraction units, removing saturated insulation and drywall that cannot be dried in place, and setting commercial desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers alongside high-velocity air movers. Moisture readings are logged daily with calibrated meters, both pin-type for surface readings and non-invasive sensors for what's hiding behind plaster or under subflooring.

Structural repair, framing, sheathing, roofing, siding, drywall, and finish work, begins only after affected materials reach documented dry standard. Rebuilding over wet framing is one of the most common and expensive mistakes in storm recovery.

## Our process

1. **Emergency stabilization and site safety**, Before any assessment happens, the site has to be safe to enter and protected from further weather exposure. Roof tarps, temporary boarding, and debris hazard evaluation come first. If a tree is still loading a structure, that gets addressed before crews work inside.

2. **Damage documentation and scope development**, Every affected surface is photographed, moisture-mapped, and logged. This isn't just for the insurance file, it's the foundation of an accurate repair scope. We note the difference between wind-driven rain intrusion (which soaks wall cavities from the top down) and roof-deck failure (which saturates insulation and ceiling assemblies), because the drying strategy differs.

3. **Water extraction and structural drying**, Extraction removes bulk water; drying removes what's left in materials. We place equipment based on psychrometric readings, temperature, relative humidity, and dew point, not by room count. Monitoring continues until affected assemblies reach dry standard, typically documented over three to five days depending on material depth and ambient conditions.

4. **Debris removal and selective demolition**, Storm debris, damaged roofing materials, compromised insulation, and unsalvageable drywall are removed and disposed of properly. Selective demolition, cutting out only what can't be dried or repaired, keeps the rebuild scope and cost as tight as possible.

5. **Reconstruction and final inspection**, Framing, sheathing, roofing, insulation, drywall, and finishes are restored to pre-loss condition. Work is scoped in writing before it begins, and the finished scope supports your insurance claim documentation from start to close.

## What separates a good storm damage response from a bad one

The most common failure in storm damage restoration is treating it like a single trade job. A roofing contractor who patches the roof but doesn't assess the water that already entered, or a drywall crew that rebuilds before moisture readings confirm the framing is dry, leaves a homeowner with a repaired surface over a hidden problem.

Insurance adjusters look for a few things that less-experienced operators routinely miss:

- **Moisture mapping on entry, not just visible damage.** Wind-driven rain penetrates wall cavities through gaps that look fine from the outside. An adjuster's scope that only documents what's visibly wet will miss saturated wall assemblies that show up as mold six weeks later.
- **Separation of emergency services from repair costs.** Carriers want to see tarping, boarding, and extraction billed distinctly from reconstruction. Bundling them together slows claims and sometimes triggers disputes.
- **Photo documentation at every phase.** Before demolition, during drying, after demo, and at dry standard, each phase needs a dated photo record. Gaps in documentation give adjusters grounds to reduce or deny line items.
- **Code-compliant reconstruction.** Older homes in American Fork and throughout Utah County may have roofing, insulation, or electrical systems that were grandfathered under previous codes. A storm repair that triggers a permit may also require bringing those systems up to current standard, something a written scope should address before work begins, not after.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Utah County sits at the intersection of two storm patterns that most Front Range states don't share. Late spring and early summer bring severe thunderstorms capable of large hail and straight-line winds that strip shingles and push water into wall assemblies faster than drains can handle. Late summer monsoon moisture, drawn up from the Gulf of California, produces intense, short-duration downpours that overwhelm gutters and window flashing on homes not designed for that volume of water in that short a window.

Winter brings a different risk: heavy, wet snow loading on roofs, ice dam formation at eaves, and freeze-thaw cycles that work existing flashing and caulk joints loose over time. A roof that held through ten winters can fail in the eleventh after enough thermal cycling, and the resulting leak often goes unnoticed until spring.

Older neighborhoods in American Fork, particularly areas with homes built in the 1970s through early 1990s, tend to have wood-framed roofs with lower-slope sections that are more vulnerable to wind uplift and ice damming than steeper contemporary designs. If your home is in that era, a post-storm inspection should include those low-slope transitions even if the main roof looks intact.
