## What Does a General Contractor Actually Do on a Reconstruction Project?

A general contractor manages every trade on your project: framers, drywall crews, electricians, plumbers, painters, and finish carpenters. They pull the permits, schedule inspections, and are the single person accountable for the finished result. On a reconstruction job after a covered loss, they also work directly with your insurance adjuster to document the scope of repairs so the rebuild matches what was damaged.

For homeowners coming out of a water or fire loss, the GC role is especially important because the line between mitigation (stopping the damage) and reconstruction (fixing it) is where most delays happen. When the same company handles both phases, the scope is already documented, the moisture readings are on file, and the rebuild can start as soon as the structure is dry and the claim is approved. You are not starting over with a new contractor who has never seen the property.

On a planned renovation project, the GC role is similar: one licensed professional coordinates all the trades, keeps the project on schedule, and is responsible for code compliance at every inspection point.

## What Reconstruction Projects Look Like After a Covered Loss

Post-disaster reconstruction in Lehi typically falls into a few categories depending on what caused the damage.

**Water damage reconstruction** is the most common. After a burst pipe, appliance failure, or Category 2 or 3 water intrusion, drywall is cut out, flooring is removed, and sometimes cabinetry or structural framing needs to be replaced. Once the structure passes a final moisture clearance, the rebuild phase begins: framing repairs, insulation, drywall, texture, paint, flooring, and cabinet reinstallation. The [IICRC S500 standard](https://www.iicrc.org) defines the drying benchmarks that must be met before reconstruction starts. Rebuilding over wet materials causes mold and voids the repair.

**Fire and smoke damage reconstruction** involves more complexity. Charred framing may need to be sistered or replaced. Smoke-affected drywall and insulation are removed. HVAC systems are inspected and often cleaned or replaced. Electrical panels and wiring in affected areas are reviewed by a licensed electrician before drywall goes back up. The [EPA's guidance on indoor air quality after fire damage](https://www.epa.gov) applies here, particularly if the fire involved synthetic materials that leave chemical residue.

**Storm damage reconstruction** in Utah County often means roof decking, fascia, and exterior siding after hail or wind events. In Lehi's newer developments, composite roofing and vinyl siding are common, and matching materials to existing finishes is part of the scope. See our post on [storm and wind damage restoration](/blog/storm-wind-damage-restoration/) for what the mitigation phase looks like before reconstruction begins.

**Sewage backup reconstruction** requires extra steps. After a Category 3 water loss, affected materials are removed and the area is sanitized before any rebuild work begins. Drywall, subfloor, and insulation in the affected zone are typically replaced entirely, not dried in place.

## What the Reconstruction Process Looks Like, Step by Step

Knowing the sequence helps you set realistic expectations and have the right conversations with your adjuster.

1. **Scope and estimate.** The GC walks the property, reviews the mitigation documentation, and writes a line-item scope of repairs. On insurance jobs, this scope is submitted to the adjuster for approval before work starts. On renovation projects, this becomes the contract.

2. **Permit pull.** Most structural, electrical, and plumbing work in Lehi requires a permit through the Lehi City Building Department. Your GC handles this. Skipping permits creates problems at resale and can void your homeowners policy coverage for the repair.

3. **Demo and rough work.** Any remaining damaged material is removed. Framing repairs, rough plumbing, and rough electrical happen before walls close.

4. **Inspections.** The city inspector signs off on rough work before insulation and drywall go in. This is non-negotiable and protects you.

5. **Drywall, texture, and paint.** Walls and ceilings are rebuilt to match existing finishes. Texture matching in Utah County homes (which often have knockdown or orange peel) takes skill to blend invisibly.

6. **Finish work.** Flooring, trim, cabinets, fixtures, and hardware are installed. This is where the project starts looking like a home again.

7. **Final inspection and punch list.** The city does a final inspection. The GC walks the project with you and addresses any items that need adjustment.

8. **Insurance closeout.** On covered losses, the GC provides final invoices and documentation to close the claim. Supplements for hidden damage discovered during demo are submitted at this stage.
