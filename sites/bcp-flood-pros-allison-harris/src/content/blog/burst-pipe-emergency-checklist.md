A burst pipe can dump dozens of gallons into your home in the time it takes to find your phone. The moment you suspect one, shut off the main water supply, that single action limits almost all of the damage that follows. Then cut power to any circuits near the water, open a faucet downstream to relieve pressure in the line, and start documenting with your phone camera before you touch anything else. Everything after that is about drying fast and assessing what got wet.

## Step 1, Stop the Water First

Your main shutoff valve is usually one of three places: in a utility room or basement near the water heater, in a crawl space access panel, or outside near the foundation in a buried box (common in Utah's colder climate zones where lines run close to exterior walls). Turn it clockwise until it stops. If the valve is corroded or won't seat fully, call your water utility's emergency line, they can shut off at the meter.

Once the main is closed, open the lowest faucet in the house (a laundry sink or hose bib) to drain residual pressure from the line. This stops the slow seep that continues even after the valve is closed.

**What to document before cleanup begins:**
- Photos and video of standing water, wet walls, and any visible pipe damage
- The approximate time you discovered the leak
- Which rooms are affected and whether any ceilings are sagging
- Serial numbers or model info on any appliances that were running near the break

Your insurance adjuster will ask for all of this.

## Step 2, Reduce Immediate Damage

Once the water is off and you've documented the scene, work through this sequence:

1. **Cut power to wet areas.** Go to your breaker panel and switch off any circuit that serves a room with standing water. Don't wade through water to flip a switch, cut the breaker first.
2. **Move valuables off the floor.** Furniture legs wick water upward into upholstery and wood within minutes. Get rugs, electronics, and anything irreplaceable onto dry surfaces or outside.
3. **Mop or wet-vac standing water.** The sooner bulk water is removed, the less time it has to migrate under flooring or into wall cavities. A standard shop vac works fine for this stage.
4. **Open windows and interior doors.** Airflow slows the humidity spike that starts mold colonization. In American Fork winters, balance this against freezing, don't leave the house open if temps are dropping.
5. **Pull up saturated rugs and padding.** Carpet padding holds water like a sponge and is almost never worth saving after a major pipe burst. Pulling it up also lets you see whether the subfloor underneath is wet.

If the leak originated from a washing machine supply line or a dishwasher connection rather than a supply pipe in the wall, the cleanup process is similar, but the source is worth noting for your claim, since appliance-related losses are sometimes handled differently under homeowner's policies.

## What NOT to Do

Some instincts make the situation worse:

- **Don't use a regular household fan as your only drying tool.** Fans move air but don't pull moisture out of it. In a sealed room, you can actually raise the relative humidity and accelerate mold growth. Proper drying requires dehumidification alongside airflow.
- **Don't assume the surface feels dry means it is dry.** Water behind plaster or drywall can read dry at the surface while the lath, framing, and insulation behind it stay saturated for days. Moisture meters tell the real story.
- **Don't skip the subfloor.** Hardwood and laminate flooring are almost always the last place you'd think to check, and the first place water travels. If the subfloor is wet, the finish floor above it will cup, buckle, or delaminate regardless of how quickly you dried the surface.
- **Don't wait to call your insurance company.** Most policies require prompt notice of a loss. Waiting several days before reporting, even if you're still assessing damage, can complicate your claim.
- **Don't bleach a wet area and call it done.** Bleach doesn't penetrate porous materials. If mold is going to grow, it grows in the paper facing of drywall and in wood framing, surfaces bleach can't reach effectively.

## When to Call a Professional

You can handle the first hour yourself. The decision point comes around hour two or three, when you need to assess whether the structure is actually drying, or just appears to be.

Call a water damage restoration professional when:

- Water reached more than one room, or traveled to a floor below the break
- The leak was running for more than an hour before you found it
- Ceilings are sagging, discolored, or soft to the touch
- You can't locate or confirm the source of the moisture
- The affected area includes insulation, hardwood flooring over a subfloor, or any wall cavity you can't visually inspect
- You smell a musty or earthy odor, mold can begin colonizing wet organic material within 24 to 48 hours under the right conditions

A professional team uses thermal imaging cameras and calibrated moisture meters to find water that isn't visible. They'll also place commercial-grade drying equipment, air movers and refrigerant or desiccant dehumidifiers, that pull moisture out of structural materials rather than just circulating surface air. ## The Longer Recovery: What Happens After Drying

Drying is not the end of the process, it's the prerequisite for everything else.

Once moisture readings in walls and floors return to baseline (typically measured in wood moisture content percentage and relative humidity), the rebuild phase begins. Depending on what got wet, that can include:

- **Drywall replacement.** Wet drywall that was saturated for more than a few hours is generally removed rather than dried in place. The paper facing is a food source for mold, and dried-in-place drywall often shows staining and texture changes even if it tests dry.
- **Insulation replacement.** Fiberglass batt insulation loses its R-value when wet and doesn't reliably dry out inside a wall cavity. Blown-in cellulose is even more problematic, it compacts and holds moisture against framing.
- **Flooring assessment.** Hardwood floors sometimes respond well to aggressive drying with floor mat systems if caught early. More often, boards that have already cupped need to be removed and replaced after the subfloor dries.
- **Mold inspection.** If drying was delayed or incomplete, a visual inspection and sometimes air sampling may be recommended before walls are closed up. This is worth doing, closing wet framing inside new drywall creates a problem that's far more expensive to fix later.
- **Pipe repair and pressure testing.** The burst pipe itself needs to be repaired and the repaired section pressure-tested before walls are closed. This is plumbing work separate from restoration, though some restoration contractors coordinate both.

The full timeline from burst pipe to finished repair varies widely, a single wet wall caught within an hour might be fully dried and patched in under two weeks. A loss that ran overnight into multiple rooms and a finished basement can take six to eight weeks or longer.
