## Why Sandy Properties See Elevated Water Intrusion Risk

Sandy's position along the Wasatch Front gives it a climate that swings hard between seasons. Winter snowpack builds steadily in the mountains above the city, and when temperatures climb in March and April, that melt moves quickly through soil that is already near saturation. Homes built on the valley floor, many of them constructed in the 1970s and 1980s during Sandy's rapid suburban expansion, often have window-well drains and perimeter drainage systems that were sized for average years, not the heavy runoff seasons that have become more common. Crawl spaces and unfinished basements in that era of construction frequently lack vapor barriers or have barriers that have degraded, leaving concrete block and poured-wall foundations vulnerable to lateral seepage.

Sandy also sits within Salt Lake County's urban core, where aging municipal infrastructure occasionally contributes to sewer surcharge events during heavy rain. A backed-up floor drain can deposit contaminated water across a finished basement faster than a homeowner realizes, and the cleanup window is even tighter when the source is a Category 2 or Category 3 intrusion.

## Local Note

Sandy homes built in the 1970s and early 1980s frequently used a combination of concrete block foundation walls and wood-framed interior partition walls that sit directly on slab. When water enters at the base of a block wall, it wicks upward through the mortar joints and into the bottom plates of those interior walls before it ever pools visibly on the floor. Homeowners sometimes assume the affected area is limited to what they can see standing in the room. Placing air movers only at floor level misses the moisture that has traveled up into the wall cavity. Positioning equipment to address both the slab surface and the lower wall assembly is a detail that makes a measurable difference in drying time and in whether the wall framing stays salvageable.
