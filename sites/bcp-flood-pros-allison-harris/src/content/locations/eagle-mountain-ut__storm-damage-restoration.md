## Why Eagle Mountain Properties See Distinctive Storm Damage

Most of Eagle Mountain's housing stock was built after 2000, which means the construction is relatively modern, but that cuts both ways. Newer homes in this area often feature large open-plan roof sections with long ridge lines that catch lateral wind loads hard. Composite shingles installed at high elevation are rated for UV exposure but can become brittle faster than manufacturers' charts suggest when they cycle through freeze-thaw repeatedly each winter. When a spring microburst or a late-season hail event hits, those already-stressed shingles crack rather than flex, and water finds its way into the roof deck before the storm has even passed.

The soil composition across much of Eagle Mountain also matters after heavy rain. The clay-heavy ground common to this part of Utah County sheds water rather than absorbing it, which means runoff volumes spike quickly and window wells, basement stairwells, and garage thresholds become entry points for water that has nowhere else to go. A storm that drops an inch of rain in forty minutes here behaves more like three inches would on sandy soil.

## Our Storm Damage Restoration Process in Eagle Mountain

The first priority after any storm loss is stopping additional water from entering the structure. That means emergency tarping of compromised roof sections, boarding of broken windows or doors, and a perimeter check for ground-level water intrusion points. Once the building envelope is secured, the scope work begins: moisture mapping with thermal imaging and calibrated meters to find water that has already migrated behind drywall or under flooring, documentation of structural and cosmetic damage for the insurance claim, and a written restoration plan before any demolition starts.

For wind and tree-strike damage, the structural assessment comes before cosmetic repairs. A branch that punches through a roof deck can transfer load to rafters in ways that aren't visible from inside the attic without a proper inspection. FIX Restoration coordinates with structural assessors when the damage warrants it, and we photograph every affected area in detail so that nothing gets missed in the insurance documentation.

Drying in Eagle Mountain's climate requires attention to outdoor humidity levels, which fluctuate considerably between summer monsoon season and the dry winter months. Equipment settings that work in July may over-dry materials in February and cause secondary cracking in drywall mud or hardwood floors. Crews adjust dehumidifier and airflow targets based on current conditions rather than running a fixed protocol.
