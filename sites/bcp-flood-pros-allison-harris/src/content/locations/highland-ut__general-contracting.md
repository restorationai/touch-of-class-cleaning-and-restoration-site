## Why Highland's Climate and Housing Stock Shape Every Renovation

Most of Highland's residential neighborhoods developed from the late 1980s through the 2000s, which means the dominant housing stock is tract construction from that era, wood-framed, often with OSB sheathing, and built when Utah County's growth was outpacing its inspection capacity. That combination produces predictable renovation discoveries: vapor barriers that were installed incorrectly or skipped entirely, HVAC ductwork routed through unconditioned attic space that loses efficiency every winter, and bathroom tile set over cement board that was never waterproofed behind the shower pan. None of these are catastrophic, but they do mean that a straightforward bathroom remodel in Highland frequently opens up into a conversation about what's behind the wall before the new tile goes up.

The freeze-thaw cycle at this elevation also matters for exterior work. Stucco and EIFS finishes, common on Highland homes built in the 1990s, develop hairline cracks as the substrate moves seasonally. Left unaddressed during a remodel, those cracks become water intrusion points. A general contractor working regularly in this area knows to inspect and seal exterior penetrations before closing up any interior renovation that touches an outside wall.

## The Renovation and Remodel Process in Highland

Every project starts with a scope walk, not a sales visit, but a working conversation about what the space needs to do, what's behind the surfaces, and what Utah County permitting will require. Highland falls under Utah County jurisdiction for building permits, and the county's review timelines for structural work or additions can affect your project schedule in ways that a contractor unfamiliar with the area might not anticipate.

Once scope and permits are aligned, the sequence typically runs: demolition and hazardous material assessment, rough-in trades (plumbing, electrical, HVAC), framing or structural work if needed, inspections, insulation, drywall, finish work, and final punch-out. ## Coordinating with HOAs and Utah County Permitting

A number of Highland's planned communities have active HOA covenants that govern exterior finishes, roofline changes, and even window styles. Before any exterior renovation or addition breaks ground, it's worth confirming whether your HOA requires architectural review board approval separate from the county building permit. These are two different approval tracks with two different timelines, and running them in parallel, rather than sequentially, is one of the more practical ways to keep a project on schedule.
