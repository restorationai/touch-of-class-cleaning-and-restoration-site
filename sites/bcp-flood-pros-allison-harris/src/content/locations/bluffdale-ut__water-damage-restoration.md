Bluffdale's growth has been rapid. Much of the city's housing stock was built within the last two decades on land that was previously agricultural, and that history matters for water damage. Subdivisions built quickly during Utah County's expansion years sometimes have grading that settles unevenly over time, creating low spots against foundation walls where irrigation runoff and snowmelt collect.

## Our Water Damage Restoration Process in Bluffdale

Water removal is the first priority. FIX Restoration uses truck-mounted and portable extraction units to pull standing water from hard floors, carpet, and crawl spaces before it migrates further into structural cavities. Once visible water is removed, the real work begins: locating moisture that has already wicked into wall cavities, subfloor assemblies, and insulation.

In Bluffdale's newer construction, open-web floor trusses are common. Water travels through those cavities quickly and can saturate the bottom chord of a truss system while the finished ceiling below still looks dry. Thermal imaging and calibrated moisture meters map exactly where drying equipment needs to be placed rather than guessing by appearance.

Structural drying in Utah benefits from low ambient humidity during most of the year, but that advantage disappears when a home is sealed up and moisture is releasing from multiple materials at once. FIX Restoration deploys commercial-grade desiccant and refrigerant dehumidifiers alongside high-velocity air movers, adjusting the drying plan daily based on moisture readings until materials return to acceptable levels. Documentation of readings is kept throughout the process, which matters when an insurance claim is involved.

## Local Note

One pattern that shows up repeatedly in Bluffdale homes built during the mid-2000s construction boom: spray foam insulation was applied directly against foundation walls in many finished basements as a cost-effective alternative to rigid board. That foam bonds tightly to concrete and holds moisture against the wall face rather than allowing it to drain. When a basement floods, the foam can appear dry on the interior-facing surface while the concrete behind it stays saturated for weeks. Standard moisture meters read the foam, not the concrete, so without thermal imaging or a pin probe reaching behind the foam, a drying job can be closed out prematurely.
