Lindon sits at the base of the Wasatch Front where snowmelt from the mountains above rushes down through drainage channels each spring, and the clay-heavy soils common to this stretch of Utah County don't absorb that water quickly. When a supply line fails behind a wall or a sump pump can't keep up during a heavy snowmelt event, that standing water has nowhere to go but into your subfloor, your framing, and eventually your living space. FIX Restoration responds to water damage calls throughout Lindon and the surrounding area, handling everything from initial water extraction to full structural drying so the damage stops where it started.

## Why Lindon Properties See Water Damage Issues

Lindon's growth over the past two decades means the city has a wide mix of housing stock, from older ranch-style homes built when the area was still transitioning away from agricultural use, to newer construction subdivisions that went up quickly during Utah County's population boom. That mix matters for water damage response. Older homes in Lindon often have galvanized steel supply lines that have been corroding quietly for decades, and those pipes tend to fail without warning. Newer tract homes, built fast to meet demand, sometimes have plumbing connections that weren't torqued to spec, and those fittings can weep slowly inside wall cavities for months before anyone notices the discoloration on the drywall.

The clay-dominant soil throughout much of Utah County also creates hydrostatic pressure against basement and crawl space walls during wet seasons. When snowpack in the Wasatch Range above Lindon melts faster than usual, that pressure builds, and even a small crack in a foundation wall becomes a channel for water intrusion. Homeowners sometimes assume a damp crawl space is normal in spring. It isn't, and the window between first moisture and active mold colonization is shorter than most people expect, typically 24 to 72 hours under the right temperature and humidity conditions.

## Our Water Damage Restoration Process in Lindon

Every water damage job starts with finding all of the water, not just the water that's visible. FIX Restoration uses thermal imaging cameras and moisture meters to map saturation behind walls, under flooring, and inside ceiling cavities before any equipment is staged. In Lindon homes with finished basements, that step is especially important because water migrating through a foundation wall can travel laterally under a slab before surfacing somewhere unexpected.

Once the extent of the damage is mapped, extraction comes first. Standing water is removed with truck-mounted and portable extraction units, and wet materials that can't be dried in place are carefully removed and documented. After extraction, the structural drying phase begins. Industrial air movers and refrigerant or desiccant dehumidifiers are positioned based on the moisture map, not just placed near the obvious wet spots. Drying logs are recorded daily so the process is documentable for your insurance carrier and so we know when the structure has actually reached its dry standard, not just when it looks dry.

## Lindon Insurance Coordination

Most homeowner policies cover sudden and accidental water damage, but the documentation requirements vary by carrier and adjuster. FIX Restoration photographs affected materials before anything is moved, documents moisture readings at each measurement point, and produces a scope of loss that aligns with the line-item format most carriers use. That paperwork matters more than homeowners often realize. A scope that's vague or missing moisture data gives adjusters room to reduce a claim. A scope built on daily drying logs and thermal images is much harder to dispute.

If your HOA has rules about exterior staging of equipment or dumpsters for material removal, let the team know when you call. Lindon's newer subdivisions often have CC&Rs that govern what can sit in a driveway or on the street, and coordinating that ahead of time avoids delays on the job.

## Local Note

Lindon's position at the base of the Wasatch means that irrigation infrastructure, both the pressurized secondary water systems common in Utah County and older agricultural ditch remnants, runs through or near many residential properties. Secondary water lines, which carry untreated irrigation water for landscaping, operate under significant pressure during the irrigation season and are not always clearly marked on older plats. A secondary water line failure near a home's foundation is a different category of problem than a municipal water line break, both in terms of the volume of water involved and the contamination level of the water itself. If you're not sure what type of water you're dealing with, don't assume it's clean. Call before you start moving things.
