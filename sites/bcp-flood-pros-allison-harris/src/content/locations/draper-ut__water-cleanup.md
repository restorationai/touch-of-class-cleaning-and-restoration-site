Snowmelt from the peaks above Suncrest and Corner Canyon drains fast in spring, and when it finds a crack in a foundation or a saturated window well, it moves into a finished basement before most homeowners realize anything is wrong. ## Why Draper Properties Are Vulnerable to Water Intrusion

Draper's development pattern tells part of the story. The newer subdivisions climbing toward the foothills above Suncrest Drive are built on expansive clay soils that shift with seasonal moisture. When those soils saturate during a heavy spring storm or a prolonged snowmelt cycle, hydrostatic pressure builds against foundation walls. Window wells fill. French drains back up. Water finds the path of least resistance, and that path often leads to a finished lower level.

The older neighborhoods closer to the valley floor, including areas near the Draper Historic District, have a different set of risks. Homes built in the 1970s and 1980s commonly used polybutylene supply lines that have aged past their reliable service life. A pinhole failure in one of those lines inside a wall cavity can saturate insulation and framing for days before the water migrates to a visible surface. By the time a homeowner notices a soft spot in the drywall or a musty smell near a bathroom wall, the moisture has already traveled further than it appears.

Irrigation systems are another local factor. Draper's long growing season means residential irrigation runs from April through October. A slow leak at a valve box or a line that ruptures against a foundation can push water into a crawl space or slab over weeks, creating conditions that are harder to trace than a dramatic pipe burst.

## Our Emergency Water Cleanup Process in Draper

When you call FIX Restoration, the first priority is stopping the source if it is still active. That means coordinating with Jordan Valley Water Conservancy District for main shutoffs when the homeowner cannot isolate the supply, or working alongside a plumber on a simultaneous response when the break is inside the structure.

Once the source is controlled, the process moves to extraction. Industrial truck-mounted and portable extractors pull standing water from carpet, hardwood, tile, and concrete. Moisture mapping follows immediately using thermal imaging and calibrated moisture meters to locate water that has migrated behind walls, under flooring, and into subfloor assemblies. In Draper's newer construction, engineered lumber and OSB subfloor panels absorb water quickly and lose structural integrity faster than solid wood, so this step is not optional.

Drying equipment is then positioned based on the moisture map, not placed by guesswork. High-capacity dehumidifiers and axial air movers run continuously, and readings are logged daily to track the drying curve. Draper's relatively low ambient humidity in summer actually helps drying times, but winter calls are different: cold exterior temperatures slow evaporation, and equipment placement has to account for the thermal dynamics of a structure that may be losing heat through an open or damaged area.

## Local Note

Draper homes built on the clay-heavy soils in the upper elevations near the foothills tend to have basement walls that wick moisture laterally through the block or poured concrete long after the initial intrusion event is over. Even after visible water is extracted, those walls can continue releasing moisture into the air and into adjacent framing for days. Drying protocols here require extended dehumidification runs and secondary moisture checks at the wall base that would not be necessary in a home built on sandier, faster-draining soil closer to the valley floor. Knowing this going in prevents a situation where a job looks finished on day three but fails a final moisture reading on day five.
