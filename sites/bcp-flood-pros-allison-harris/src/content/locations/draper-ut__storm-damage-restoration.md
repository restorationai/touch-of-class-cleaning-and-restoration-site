Draper sits at the foot of the Wasatch Front where the valley floor meets the steep terrain of the Point of the Mountain, and that geography shapes how storms behave here. Cold fronts funneling down from the peaks can drop hail the size of marbles on one block while the street behind it stays dry. High-velocity canyon winds snap mature trees and send debris across rooftops with little warning. When that kind of storm hits your home or property, the damage compounds quickly: a breached roof lets water into the framing, and Utah's dry air can be deceptive, masking moisture that is already wicking through insulation and into wall cavities.

## Why Draper Properties See Storm Damage Differently Than the Rest of the Valley

Draper's elevation and position along the Wasatch corridor create microclimates that catch homeowners off guard. The upper benches, where many newer subdivisions were developed in the 2000s and 2010s, sit exposed to wind patterns that accelerate as they drop off the ridge. Homes on those slopes often have tile or concrete roofing systems that handle normal weather well but can crack or shift when hail strikes at an angle driven by canyon gusts.

Lower-elevation areas closer to the I-15 corridor tend to collect runoff when storm drains are overwhelmed. Draper's clay-heavy soils do not absorb water quickly, so even a short but intense cloudburst can push water toward foundations and into crawl spaces before it has anywhere to go. That combination of roof exposure at elevation and drainage pressure at lower grades means storm damage here rarely follows a single pattern.

## Our Storm Damage Restoration Process in Draper

Every storm loss is different, but the sequence of work follows a consistent logic. The first priority is stabilizing the structure: tarping breached roof sections, boarding broken windows, and securing any structural elements that are at immediate risk. That protective work happens before any detailed assessment, because additional weather exposure during the inspection window makes everything worse.

Once the property is stabilized, the team documents the full scope of damage with photographs and moisture readings. In Draper homes with finished basements, which are common in the area's family-oriented subdivisions, that documentation includes checking below-grade spaces for water intrusion that may not be visible from the main floor. Drying equipment, including commercial-grade air movers and dehumidifiers, is deployed based on actual moisture readings rather than visual inspection alone, because Draper's low ambient humidity can make wet framing appear surface-dry while the interior stays saturated.

After materials are confirmed dry, structural repairs begin: roofing, siding, windows, insulation, and interior finishes are restored to pre-loss condition. ## Draper Insurance and HOA Coordination

Most storm damage claims in Draper run through standard homeowners insurance, and the documentation gathered during the initial assessment is structured with the adjuster's review in mind. Photographs, moisture logs, and a written scope of damage give the carrier what it needs to process the claim without requiring multiple site visits.

## Local Note

One pattern that comes up specifically in Draper's newer hillside subdivisions: homes built on cut-and-fill pads, where the lot was graded to create a flat building site on a slope, can develop drainage issues after a severe storm that were not present before. The fill material around the foundation settles differently than native soil, and a heavy rain event can expose gaps in the original grading that now direct water toward the structure. If your property sits on a sloped lot and you notice new water intrusion after a storm, the entry point may not be the roof or windows but the grade around the foundation itself.
