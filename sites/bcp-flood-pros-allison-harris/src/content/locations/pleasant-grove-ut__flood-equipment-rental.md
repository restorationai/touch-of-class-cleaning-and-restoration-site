## Why Pleasant Grove Homes Are Particularly Vulnerable to Water Intrusion

The bench land Pleasant Grove occupies drains toward Utah Lake, and the clay-heavy soils common along that slope don't absorb standing water quickly. After a heavy rain or a rapid snowmelt event, that water has to go somewhere, and it often finds the path of least resistance into a basement wall or through a window well. Homes built in the 1960s through the 1980s, which make up a substantial portion of Pleasant Grove's residential stock, frequently have block-foundation basements with aging waterproofing membranes. Those membranes crack, and when they do, water seeps in slowly enough that a homeowner might not notice until the carpet squishes underfoot or drywall begins to bubble.

Beyond the foundation, older supply lines in mid-century construction are often galvanized steel or early copper with compression fittings that fatigue over time. A fitting failure behind a laundry room wall or under a kitchen sink can release enough water overnight to saturate subfloor, insulation, and the ceiling of a lower level before anyone wakes up.

## Local Note: What Drying Takes Longer Here Than You'd Expect

One thing that catches Pleasant Grove homeowners off guard: the region's low average humidity can create a false sense of progress. Ambient air in Utah Valley feels dry, so a room may smell fine and feel comfortable within 24 to 48 hours of a water event, but moisture trapped inside wall cavities, under hardwood, or within concrete block doesn't follow the same timeline as surface air. Without a moisture meter reading the actual material content, it's easy to pull equipment too early and discover a mold problem three weeks later. If you're renting equipment and managing the drying yourself, take daily readings at multiple depths and don't call it done until materials read within a few points of their pre-loss baseline. FIX Restoration's team can advise on target readings for the specific materials in your home when you pick up or receive delivery.
