## Why Highland Properties See Water Damage Issues

Highland's housing stock skews toward large, single-family homes built from the late 1980s through the 2000s, many with finished basements that sit partially below grade. That below-grade square footage is particularly vulnerable. Utah County's soil profile includes expansive clay layers that hold moisture rather than drain it, so a heavy rain event or a slow irrigation leak doesn't just wet the surface, it builds hydrostatic pressure against basement walls and slab edges over days.

Winter brings its own risks. Ice damming along rooflines is common at Highland's elevation, and when that ice melts unevenly, water can track behind fascia boards and into attic insulation or top-floor ceilings. Interior plumbing failures, supply line bursts, water heater tank failures, dishwasher hose separations, spike during cold snaps when pipes in exterior walls or uninsulated garages are most stressed. Any of these scenarios can leave standing water across hundreds of square feet within an hour.

## Our Water Damage Restoration Process in Highland

The first priority on any water loss is stopping what can be stopped, shutting the source, whether that's a supply valve or a roof tarp, and then measuring what can't be seen. Moisture doesn't stay where it lands. In Highland's typical two-story construction, water from an upstairs bathroom leak will wick into subfloor plywood, travel along joists, and appear as a stain on the ceiling below before anyone notices the origin. Thermal imaging and calibrated moisture meters let us trace that path without tearing out materials blindly.

Once the scope is mapped, water extraction comes first, truck-mounted or portable extractors pulling standing water from carpet, hardwood, and concrete. After extraction, the drying phase begins: industrial air movers accelerate evaporation from wet materials while commercial dehumidifiers capture that moisture vapor before it can migrate into unaffected areas. Readings are logged daily so drying progress is documented, something insurance carriers and Utah County building inspectors both want to see if permitted reconstruction follows.

## Local Note

One pattern worth knowing in Highland: homes built in the 1990s in this area frequently used OSB (oriented strand board) subfloor rather than plywood.
