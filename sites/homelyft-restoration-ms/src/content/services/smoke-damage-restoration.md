Smoke behaves differently than fire. Long after flames are out, soot particles keep working their way into drywall pores, grout lines, and ductwork, turning a one-room incident into a whole-house odor problem within days. A kitchen grease fire, an electrical short in the attic, or smoke drifting in from a nearby wildfire can all leave behind acidic residue that etches metal, yellows grout, and off-gasses for weeks if it's only painted over instead of actually removed.

## What smoke damage restoration actually involves

Smoke damage restoration is residue identification and removal, not just a deep clean. Different fuel sources leave chemically different soot: protein residue from a stovetop fire is nearly invisible but carries an intense odor, while synthetic soot from burning plastics, foam, or electronics is thick, oily, and smears if wiped with the wrong agent. Our techs, trained to IICRC FSRT standards, test the residue type before choosing a cleaning method, because the wrong solvent on the wrong soot can set the stain permanently.

The work covers hard surfaces, HVAC components, and often textiles and contents. Walls, ceilings, and trim get cleaned with dry sponges, degreasers, or encapsulating sealers depending on porosity. Ductwork gets inspected and cleaned when soot has been pulled through the return air system, which is common in homes where the fire source was near an air handler or when a fire burned for more than a few minutes before suppression.

## Our process

1. **Residue and source characterization.** We identify whether the soot is wet (protein, low heat, sticky), dry (fast-burning, powdery), or synthetic (plastics, oily film), because this determines every cleaning decision after it.
2. **Pre-cleaning and HEPA vacuuming.** Loose soot is removed from surfaces and fabrics with HEPA-filtered equipment before any wet cleaning begins, to avoid grinding particles deeper into the material.
3. **Surface and content cleaning.** Washable surfaces are degreased or dry-cleaned by residue type; non-salvageable porous materials (some insulation, certain drywall sections) are documented and removed.
4. **Odor neutralization.** Thermal fogging, ozone, or hydroxyl treatment is used to break down odor molecules trapped in porous materials, not just mask them with fragrance.
5. **Sealing and clearance.** Affected framing or subfloor is sealed where needed before finish work begins, and we walk the property with you to confirm the space reads and smells clean.

## What separates a good smoke damage response from a bad one

The most common mistake is treating smoke damage as a painting problem. Repainting over unsealed soot traps the odor instead of removing it, and the smell returns within weeks, especially in humid Gulf Coast conditions that reactivate residue. A second common gap is skipping the HVAC system entirely: if soot was pulled through the return air, every time the system runs it can recirculate odor into rooms that were never near the fire.

Adjusters generally want to see documentation of residue type, the cleaning method used for each surface, and photos before and after thermal fogging or ozone treatment. A response that only addresses visible staining, without testing for protein residue hiding in cabinet interiors or wall cavities, tends to get flagged later when odor complaints resurface after the claim is closed.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Gulfport's humidity accelerates the chemical bonding of soot to surfaces, so residue that might sit relatively inert in a dry climate can etch into grout and metal fixtures faster here if cleanup is delayed. Agricultural and prescribed burns across the region, along with occasional wildfire smoke drifting in from further inland, can also leave a fine soot film on exterior surfaces and HVAC intakes even when there was no fire on the property itself. Hurricane season brings its own spike in smoke-related calls, tied to candle use and portable generators during power outages.
