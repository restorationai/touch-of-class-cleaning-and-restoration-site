**Water stains that keep coming back after you've repainted. A water bill that's climbed for no reason you can explain. A damp spot on the carpet with no obvious source.** These are the signs of a hidden leak, and in Gulfport's slab-on-grade homes and older copper and cast-iron plumbing, that leak can be running for weeks before it ever shows on a wall or ceiling. Water leak detection finds the source before it becomes a mold problem or a collapsed subfloor.

## What Water Leak Detection actually involves

Finding a hidden leak is a diagnostic process, not a demolition project. A technician uses acoustic listening equipment to pick up the sound of water moving under pressure through pipe or slab, paired with infrared thermal imaging to map temperature differences in flooring, drywall, and ceilings where moisture is migrating. Moisture meters confirm what the camera shows. For suspected slab leaks, that often means correlating sound readings across the floor to narrow the leak to a small, specific area rather than guessing at a room.

A typical leak detection visit runs one to three hours depending on the size of the home and how far the water has traveled from its source. The goal at every step is to locate the leak with the least amount of invasive opening possible, so homeowners aren't paying to repair holes that didn't need to exist.

## Our process

1. **Meter and fixture check.** Before any equipment comes out, we shut off interior fixtures and watch the water meter for movement, which confirms whether there's an active leak somewhere in the system and roughly how fast it's losing water.
2. **Acoustic and thermal scan.** Listening equipment and infrared imaging trace the path of water through walls, under flooring, and across slab, flagging the areas where sound and temperature readings point to a hidden leak.
3. **Isolation and confirmation.** Readings are cross-checked at multiple points to narrow the location to a specific wall section, slab area, or plumbing run, using moisture meters and, where needed, a borescope through a small inspection hole.
4. **Verification before any opening.** We confirm the finding against a second reading method before recommending any cut-in, so a homeowner isn't paying for exploratory demolition based on a single data point.
5. **Documented report.** Photos, moisture readings, and the pinpointed location go into a written report that a plumber can act on and that an insurance adjuster can review if the loss is a covered claim.

## What separates a good leak detection response from a bad one

The most common mistake is skipping the water meter test and going straight to guesswork based on a visible stain, which often points to where water pooled, not where it entered. A stain on a ceiling can be several feet from the actual pipe failure, since water travels along joists and framing before it shows itself. Cutting open drywall or slab before confirming the source with at least two detection methods leads to repeated, unnecessary openings and a homeowner paying for drywall repair twice.

Adjusters reviewing a water leak claim want to see the moisture readings and thermal images that support the location before any repair, along with a clear timeline distinguishing a sudden pipe failure from gradual seepage, since the two are treated very differently under most policies. A documented, methodical trail matters more to a claim's outcome than the repair itself.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Gulfport's sandy, shifting soil and high water table put ongoing stress on slab foundations, which is a leading cause of slab leaks in homes built from the 1960s through the 1980s on copper supply lines. Humidity and heat for much of the year also mean a slow leak can turn into visible mold growth faster here than in drier climates, so the window between a first water stain and a real moisture problem is shorter than homeowners expect. After tropical storms and heavy rain events, wind-driven water intrusion around roof penetrations and window flashing can mimic a plumbing leak, which is part of why thermal imaging and moisture mapping matter more than a visual inspection alone.
