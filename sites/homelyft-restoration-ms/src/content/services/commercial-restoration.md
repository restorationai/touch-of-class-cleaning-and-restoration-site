## What commercial restoration actually involves

Commercial water damage, fire damage, and storm damage all show up differently in a business setting than in a home. A slab leak under a retail floor can travel under multiple suites before anyone notices the smell. A kitchen fire in a strip mall tenant space can push smoke through a shared HVAC return into units that never saw flame. A roof failure on a multi-tenant office building can drop ceiling tiles over server racks or point-of-sale systems. The equipment is industrial-scale: commercial dehumidifiers and air movers sized to square footage rather than room count, HEPA air scrubbers for smoke or mold containment, and negative-air setups when a loss affects occupied adjacent units. Work often happens in phases, after hours or overnight, specifically to keep part of a business open while the affected section is mitigated. Documentation is heavier too, built around what a commercial carrier, a property manager, or a general contractor on a larger rebuild will ask to see.

## Our process

1. **Site assessment and scope walk-through.** We document pre-loss conditions, affected square footage, and any life-safety or code issues (exposed wiring, compromised structural elements) before anything is moved.
2. **Stabilization and mitigation.** Water extraction, structural drying, soot and odor removal, or board-up and tarping, matched to what the loss actually is, with equipment placement planned around what parts of the space need to stay operational.
3. **Containment and monitoring.** Moisture readings and psychrometric logs for water losses, air sampling and containment barriers for smoke or mold exposure in multi-tenant buildings, tracked daily until readings meet dry or clear standards.
4. **Coordination with stakeholders.** Property managers, tenants, general contractors, and the insurance adjuster often need separate updates on the same timeline; we build the documentation package to serve all of them.
5. **Reconstruction and reopening.** Phased rebuild so occupied or lower-damage sections can reopen while higher-damage areas finish, with a final walkthrough before handoff.

## What separates a good commercial response from a bad one

The most common mistake on commercial jobs is treating them like an oversized residential loss. A shared HVAC system means smoke or moisture can be present in a unit that shows no visible damage, and skipping air sampling there just because the suite "looks fine" is how cross-contamination claims get denied later. Commercial occupancy also triggers different code requirements than a house, ADA clearances, fire-rated assembly requirements, suspended ceiling grid specs, and a reconstruction scope that ignores those gets flagged at permit or final inspection. Adjusters reviewing commercial claims look closely at business interruption documentation: dated photos, moisture logs, and a written scope that ties every line item to the actual loss matter more here than on a typical residential file, because the numbers are usually larger and the coverage disputes more detailed.
