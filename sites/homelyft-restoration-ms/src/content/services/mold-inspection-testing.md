## What mold inspection and testing actually involves

Mold inspection and testing is a diagnostic process, not a cleanup job. It answers two questions: is there mold growth in the home, and if so, where, what type, and how extensive. A proper inspection uses moisture meters and thermal imaging to find damp building materials behind finished surfaces, paired with air sampling (spore traps) and, where visible growth exists, surface sampling (tape lifts or swabs). Samples go to a third-party accredited laboratory for analysis, not an in-house guess. Turnaround on lab results typically runs a few business days. The output is a written report: spore counts compared against an outdoor baseline, species identified where relevant, and a clear recommendation on whether remediation is warranted and at what scope.

## Our process

1. **Visual inspection and moisture mapping.** We walk the property with moisture meters and thermal imaging to locate damp framing, subfloor, or drywall that visual inspection alone would miss, paying close attention to areas with a history of leaks, slab moisture, or AC condensation.
2. **Air sampling with an outdoor control.** We pull indoor spore trap samples alongside an outdoor baseline sample, because spore counts only mean something when compared against what's naturally in the outside air that day.
3. **Surface sampling where growth is visible.** Tape lifts or swab samples identify the species present on visible staining or growth, which matters for remediation protocol and, occasionally, for a homeowner's own health questions to their physician.
4. **Independent lab analysis.** Samples are sent to a third-party accredited laboratory, not analyzed in-house, so the results hold up if an insurance adjuster or a real estate transaction needs documentation.
5. **Written report and recommendation.** You get a report outlining findings, affected square footage if applicable, and whether IICRC S520-level remediation is warranted, with a scope of containment if so.

## What separates a good mold inspection response from a bad one

The most common shortcut is skipping the outdoor control sample, which makes indoor spore counts impossible to interpret correctly. A close second is an inspector who stops at the visible stain and never opens a moisture meter on the surrounding wall cavity, missing growth that's spread further than what's showing on the surface. HVAC systems get overlooked often: a return duct pulling air across a damp coil or insulation can seed spores through the whole house, and a visual-only inspection won't catch it.

The bigger conflict of interest is a company that inspects and remediates the same job. An inspection firm that profits from finding a remediation problem has an incentive to find one. ## Seasonal & regional considerations

Gulfport's humidity sits high for most of the year, and that alone keeps indoor relative humidity elevated in homes with underpowered or aging HVAC systems, a common setup in older slab-on-grade ranches and pier-and-beam cottages throughout the Gulf Coast. Hurricane season brings a second pattern: wind-driven rain intrusion around roof flashing and window penetrations that doesn't always show up until weeks later, once trapped moisture has had time to feed growth behind drywall. Spring and early summer, when outdoor humidity climbs fastest, is when we see the most calls about a musty smell that wasn't there over the winter.
