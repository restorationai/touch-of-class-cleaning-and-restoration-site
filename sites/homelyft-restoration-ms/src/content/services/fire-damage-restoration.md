The fire is out — but the damage isn't done. In the hours after a house fire, soot particles are still migrating through your ductwork, acidic smoke residue is etching metal fixtures and appliance finishes, and the protein film from a kitchen fire is bonding to every painted surface in the room. What you do — and what you don't do — in the first 24 to 72 hours determines how much of your home can be saved and how much has to be replaced. Fire damage restoration is a race against secondary damage, and it requires a different skill set than any other cleanup job.

## What fire damage restoration actually involves

Most people picture fire restoration as hauling out charred debris and repainting. The real work is more technical than that. Soot comes in several chemically distinct types — wet soot from slow, smoldering fires behaves completely differently from the dry, powdery residue left by fast-burning synthetic materials. Protein soot from a kitchen fire is nearly invisible but produces an overwhelming odor and requires enzymatic or chemical action to break down. Identifying what burned and how it burned determines which cleaning agents, methods, and equipment the crew deploys.

Beyond the visible char, smoke infiltrates wall cavities, attic insulation, HVAC systems, and the contents of closed cabinets. Structural materials — drywall, framing lumber, subfloor — absorb odor compounds that off-gas for months if not treated properly. Restoration crews certified in fire and smoke restoration (IICRC FSRT) are trained to trace smoke migration paths, not just clean what's obviously black.

Timeline matters: within hours, soot begins permanently staining porous surfaces. Within days, acidic residue pits chrome and tarnishes copper. Within weeks, odor compounds polymerize into surfaces and become exponentially harder to remove. A proper fire restoration response is measured in hours, not days.

## Our process

1. **Emergency stabilization and board-up.** Before any cleaning begins, the structure needs to be secured. That means boarding broken windows and doors, tarping any roof damage from fire department ventilation cuts, and shutting off utilities if needed. This step protects the property from weather intrusion and unauthorized entry — and it protects the contents from additional damage while the scope of work is being developed.

2. **Soot characterization and scope development.** A technician walks every affected room — including areas that didn't burn — to identify soot types, map smoke migration, and document the loss with photographs and moisture readings. This documentation becomes the foundation of your insurance claim. Knowing whether you're dealing with dry soot, wet soot, or protein residue changes the entire cleaning approach.

3. **Contents pack-out and inventory.** Salvageable belongings — furniture, clothing, documents, electronics — are inventoried, packed, and transported to a climate-controlled facility for cleaning and storage. Leaving contents on-site during structural cleaning exposes them to additional chemical exposure and cross-contamination. A detailed inventory protects you if there are disputes about total loss items later.

4. **Structural cleaning, thermal fogging, and odor treatment.** Surfaces are cleaned using the appropriate method for each soot type — dry sponging for light dry soot, chemical sponges or alkaline cleaners for wet soot, enzymatic treatments for protein residue. After surface cleaning, thermal fogging or hydroxyl generation is used to penetrate wall cavities and porous materials where odor compounds have migrated. In severe cases, ozone treatment in an unoccupied structure reaches areas that no surface cleaning can access.

5. **HVAC cleaning and clearance.** Smoke travels through return air ducts and deposits soot on coil surfaces, in plenums, and throughout duct runs. Skipping duct cleaning means the first time the system runs, it redistributes soot and odor through every room. After duct cleaning, a final walkthrough confirms that odor levels are acceptable and surfaces are ready for reconstruction.

## What separates a good fire response from a bad one

The most common mistake in fire restoration is treating all soot the same way. Dry-brushing wet soot drives it deeper into drywall. Using alkaline cleaners on protein soot without enzymatic pre-treatment leaves a residue that smells worse after cleaning than before. These aren't edge cases — they're the errors that lead to callbacks, failed odor treatments, and disputes with insurance adjusters.

Insurance adjusters look for a documented scope that matches the visible damage. That means photographs taken before any cleaning begins, a written inventory of affected materials, and moisture readings that justify structural drying if water was used in firefighting. Adjusters also scrutinize contents claims — a line-item pack-out inventory with condition notes is far stronger than a general estimate.

A less-experienced crew may clean visible surfaces and call the job done. A properly certified team follows smoke migration into the attic, inside wall cavities, and through the HVAC system — because that's where odor complaints come from six months after reconstruction is finished.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Along the Mississippi Gulf Coast, fire damage restoration carries some region-specific wrinkles. High ambient humidity — routinely above 70% from late spring through early fall — slows the drying of any water introduced during firefighting and accelerates mold colonization in smoke-damaged materials. A structure that sat closed for even 48 hours after a fire in July can show mold growth on wet drywall before the restoration crew has finished soot cleaning. Crews working in Gulfport and the surrounding coastal communities need to monitor moisture aggressively and may need to run dehumidification alongside smoke remediation — not sequentially.

Winter months bring a different risk: space heaters and fireplace use spike between November and February, and older housing stock in neighborhoods like North Gulfport and Long Beach often has aging wiring or unlined chimneys that make these fires more common. Older homes also tend to have more plaster, hardwood, and natural-fiber materials that absorb odor compounds deeply and require longer treatment cycles.
