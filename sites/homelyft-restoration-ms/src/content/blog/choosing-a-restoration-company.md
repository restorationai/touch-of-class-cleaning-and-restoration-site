Choosing a restoration company after a flood, fire, or mold discovery is one of the most consequential decisions you'll make as a property owner — and you're usually making it while stressed, wet, or smelling smoke. The short answer: verify credentials before you sign anything, get the scope of work in writing, and never let a company start work without confirming your insurance carrier will accept their documentation. The rest of this guide walks you through exactly how to do that, what warning signs to watch for, and what questions to ask before a crew ever sets foot in your home.

## Why the Restoration Industry Attracts Bad Actors

Restoration work sits at an uncomfortable intersection: emergency conditions, insurance money, and homeowners who just want the problem gone. That combination draws in fly-by-night operators — particularly after Gulf Coast weather events, when out-of-state crews flood the market following a named storm.

In Mississippi, the contractor licensing rules for restoration work can be murky, and insurance payouts create an incentive for inflated scopes. That doesn't mean most companies are dishonest — it means you need to do a quick 10-minute check before signing anything.

Here's what that check looks like:

1. **Search the Mississippi State Board of Contractors** (msboc.state.ms.us) and confirm the company holds an active license for the type of work they're doing.
2. **Ask for proof of general liability and workers' comp insurance.** A legitimate company will email you a certificate of insurance within minutes of being asked.
3. **Look up their IICRC certification.** The Institute of Inspection, Cleaning and Restoration Certification maintains a public directory. An IICRC-certified firm has technicians trained to industry standards for water, fire, and mold work — not just whoever showed up in a truck.
4. **Check Google and the Better Business Bureau** — but read the negative reviews, not just the star count. Look for patterns: disputes over billing, work left incomplete, or insurance documentation problems.
5. **Confirm they have a physical address in or near Gulfport.** A local presence means accountability. A P.O. box and a cell number is a red flag.

## What to Look for in the Estimate and Scope of Work

A trustworthy restoration company will give you a written scope of work before any equipment goes in. If someone asks you to sign a broad "authorization to proceed" without itemizing what they plan to do, slow down.

The estimate should specify:

- **The affected area in square footage** — not just "the basement" or "the kitchen."
- **Equipment being deployed** (number of dehumidifiers, air movers, negative air machines) and how long it's expected to run.
- **The drying standard they're working toward.** Reputable water damage companies dry to the IICRC S500 standard, which means reaching specific moisture content targets in structural materials — not just running fans until things feel dry.
- **What materials will be removed vs. dried in place.** Drywall, insulation, and flooring often need to come out. If a company tells you they can dry everything in place without moisture mapping, ask why.
- **A timeline.** Water damage mitigation typically runs 3–5 days. Mold remediation timelines vary based on affected area and material type. Fire and smoke restoration can take weeks depending on how deep the odor has penetrated.

For mold remediation specifically, ask whether they'll provide a post-remediation verification (PRV) test — an independent air quality test after the work is done. A company that resists independent verification is a company worth walking away from.

## Red Flags That Should Stop You Cold

Some warning signs are obvious in hindsight but easy to miss when you're panicked. Watch for these:

- **They knocked on your door unsolicited after a storm.** Storm chasers follow disaster declarations. That doesn't make them criminals, but it does mean you should verify everything more carefully.
- **They ask you to sign over your insurance benefits (an Assignment of Benefits or AOB).** Mississippi has restrictions on AOB agreements, and signing one can complicate your claim significantly. Talk to your adjuster before signing.
- **They want payment in full before work begins.** A deposit for materials is reasonable. Full payment upfront is not.
- **They can't tell you what drying standard they use.** If a technician looks blank when you ask about moisture targets or the IICRC S500, that's meaningful.
- **They discourage you from calling your insurance company.** A legitimate company works with your carrier. They don't replace them.
- **The estimate is verbal only.** Get everything in writing. Every time.

## Questions to Ask Before You Hire Anyone

You don't need to interrogate a restoration company — just ask these five questions and listen to how they answer:

1. **"Are your technicians IICRC certified?"** They should be able to name the specific certification (WRT for water, FSRT for fire, AMRT for mold) and offer to show documentation.
2. **"Will you bill my insurance company directly?"** Most established restoration companies handle direct billing and can submit documentation in the format your carrier requires.
3. **"Who will be on-site doing the work?"** Some companies subcontract everything. That's not automatically bad, but you should know who's in your home and whether those subs carry their own insurance.
4. **"What does your moisture documentation look like?"** For water damage, you want to see daily moisture readings logged by room and material — the kind of documentation an insurance adjuster will accept without dispute.
5. **"What happens if drying takes longer than expected?"** Scope changes happen. A good company communicates them in writing and gets your approval before adding equipment or extending timelines.

## The Longer Recovery: What Comes After Mitigation

Mitigation — stopping the damage and stabilizing the structure — is only the first phase. What comes after depends on the type of loss:

**After water damage:** Once the structure is dry and moisture readings are documented, reconstruction begins. This might mean replacing drywall, insulation, flooring, and cabinetry. Some restoration companies handle both mitigation and reconstruction under one roof, which simplifies the insurance claim. Others hand off to a separate contractor.

**After fire damage:** Smoke residue is acidic and continues to damage surfaces long after the flames are out. The smell you notice in the first days is the least of it — smoke particles embed in drywall, ductwork, and soft goods. Proper fire restoration involves HEPA vacuuming, chemical sponge cleaning, thermal fogging or ozone treatment, and often duct cleaning. If a company just paints over smoke-stained walls, the odor will return.

**After mold remediation:** The mold itself can be removed, but the moisture source that caused it must be fixed or it will return. Make sure your restoration company identifies and documents the source — whether that's a slow pipe leak, a roof penetration, or chronic high humidity from inadequate ventilation. In coastal Mississippi, where summer humidity regularly tops 90%, that last category is more common than people expect.

Throughout all of it, keep copies of every document: the estimate, the daily moisture logs, the certificate of completion, the PRV test results. Your insurance company will want them, and so will a future buyer if you ever sell the property.

## Making the Call

If you're in the middle of a water, fire, or mold situation right now, the most important thing is to move quickly — but not so quickly that you skip the five-minute credential check. A company that can't answer basic questions about their certifications or documentation process will cost you more in the long run than the hour you spent vetting them.
