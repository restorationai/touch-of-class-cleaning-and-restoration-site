Fire damage rarely stops at the burn marks. By the time the flames are out and the fire department clears the scene, a second wave of damage is already underway — smoke residue is settling into walls and ductwork, soot is reacting with moisture in the air, and the water used to fight the fire is soaking into subfloors and insulation. Understanding the restoration process from start to finish helps you ask the right questions, make faster decisions, and avoid costly mistakes while you're still in shock.

## What Happens in the First 24 Hours

Once the fire marshal releases your property, the clock starts on a narrow window where fast action matters most. Smoke odor compounds — primarily aldehydes, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, and acidic soot particles — begin bonding to porous surfaces within hours. On a warm, humid Gulf Coast day in Gulfport, that process accelerates. Porous materials like drywall, upholstered furniture, and wood trim can absorb odor molecules so deeply that surface cleaning alone won't remove them later.

A professional restoration crew arriving in this window will typically do three things before any cleaning begins:

1. **Board up and tarp the structure.** Broken windows, holes in the roof, and compromised doors need to be secured against weather and unauthorized entry. Mississippi's coastal humidity means an open structure can develop secondary moisture damage — and mold — within 24 to 48 hours.
2. **Document everything.** Every room, every damaged item, every wall cavity gets photographed and catalogued. This documentation is the backbone of your insurance claim. Skipping it — or letting well-meaning family members haul debris before it's recorded — can reduce your payout significantly.
3. **Assess the structural integrity.** Not all fire damage is visible. Heat can compromise load-bearing members, warp door frames, and weaken floor joists without charring them. A trained eye checks these before anyone walks through the structure.

## The Smoke and Soot Removal Phase

This is the step most homeowners underestimate. Smoke doesn't just coat surfaces — it travels. It follows air currents into closets two rooms away from the fire, settles inside HVAC systems, and infiltrates attic insulation. Soot from synthetic materials (carpet, foam furniture, plastic fixtures) is oily and acidic, and it etches metal fixtures and glass within days if left in place.

Professional smoke and soot removal involves several distinct techniques applied based on the material being cleaned:

- **Dry sponge wiping** for loose, powdery soot on walls before any wet cleaning — wetting soot first drives it deeper into drywall.
- **Chemical sponges and alkaline cleaners** to neutralize the acidic residue on hard surfaces.
- **HEPA vacuuming** of ductwork, registers, and air handlers to prevent the HVAC system from redistributing smoke particles every time the heat or AC runs.
- **Thermal fogging or hydroxyl generation** to deodorize structural cavities — spaces that can't be physically wiped down.

This is also the phase where contents — furniture, clothing, documents, electronics — are evaluated. Some items are cleaned on-site; others are packed out to a climate-controlled facility for specialized treatment. Electronics exposed to smoke carry conductive soot residue that can cause short circuits when powered on, so they're typically inspected before you plug anything back in.

## Water Damage from Firefighting Efforts

It sounds counterintuitive, but water damage is one of the most common secondary problems after a structure fire. A single fire hose can discharge 150 to 250 gallons per minute. That water saturates flooring, wicks up wall cavities, and pools in crawlspaces. In a slab-on-grade home common in coastal Mississippi, it has nowhere to drain.

If this water isn't extracted and dried within 48 to 72 hours, mold colonization becomes a near certainty — and mold remediation adds weeks and cost to an already difficult recovery.

Restoration crews use the same equipment for fire-related water damage that they use for flood and burst-pipe jobs: truck-mounted extractors, desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers, and axial air movers positioned to create a drying envelope around wet materials. Moisture readings are logged daily until materials reach their target dryness levels, which are defined by the IICRC S500 standard.

## What Not to Do After a House Fire

Some of the most damaging mistakes happen in the first few hours, before a restoration company arrives.

**Don't turn on the HVAC system.** If smoke has entered the ductwork — and it almost always has — running the system distributes soot and odor throughout every room in the house, including areas the fire never touched.

**Don't wipe soot with a wet cloth.** Moisture turns dry soot into a paste that penetrates deeper into drywall and fabric. Dry sponges or no sponges until a professional assesses the surface type.

**Don't throw away damaged items before your adjuster visits.** Your insurance adjuster needs to see the damage in place. Disposing of items early — even items that are clearly ruined — can complicate your claim. Take photos of everything first, and ideally wait for written authorization.

**Don't assume the structure is safe to enter.** Even after the fire department clears the scene, hidden structural damage, compromised electrical systems, and airborne soot particles are real hazards. Limit entry until a restoration professional has done a walkthrough.

## The Reconstruction Phase

Once the structure is dry, deodorized, and cleared of damaged materials, reconstruction begins. Depending on the severity of the fire, this can range from repainting a single room to rebuilding entire sections of the home.

A full restoration typically moves in this order:

1. **Demolition of unsalvageable materials** — charred drywall, burned framing, melted insulation.
2. **Structural repairs** — sistering damaged joists, replacing compromised roof decking, reframing burned wall sections.
3. **Mechanical rough-in** — replacing electrical wiring, plumbing, and HVAC components that were damaged or exposed to heat.
4. **Insulation and drywall** — closing up wall cavities after mechanical inspections pass.
5. **Finish work** — painting, flooring, cabinetry, trim, and fixture installation.

For larger losses, reconstruction can take weeks to months. Staying in close contact with your insurance adjuster throughout this phase — and keeping records of every invoice and change order — protects you if disputes arise over scope or cost.

## When to Call a Restoration Professional

If the fire was contained to a small area — a stovetop grease fire that was extinguished quickly with no structural damage — thorough cleaning with appropriate products may be something you can manage. But if the fire department responded, if smoke spread beyond one room, or if there's any water damage from firefighting, professional restoration isn't optional. It's the difference between a home that's genuinely safe and one that looks clean but still off-gasses smoke compounds into the air you breathe.

Fire damage and smoke damage restoration are related but distinct disciplines. A contractor who handles one should handle both — otherwise you risk gaps in the scope that leave odor or residue behind.
