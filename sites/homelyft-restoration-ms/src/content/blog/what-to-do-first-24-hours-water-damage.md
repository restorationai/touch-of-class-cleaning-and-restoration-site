If water is actively spreading through your home right now, stop reading and do three things first: shut off the water supply, cut power to any flooded rooms at the breaker box, and move out of standing water. Once you've done that, come back — because what happens in the next 24 hours will determine how much damage you're dealing with, how much your insurance pays, and whether mold becomes your next problem.

## Stop the Source and Make the Space Safe

Every minute water runs unchecked, it travels farther than you'd expect. Water from a burst pipe under a bathroom sink can migrate through the subfloor and show up as a wet spot in the ceiling of the room below within 20 minutes. Your first job is to cut off the supply.

- **Burst or leaking pipe:** Turn the shutoff valve closest to the leak. If you can't find it, go straight to the main shutoff — usually near your water meter, which in Gulfport-area homes is often at the curb or on the side of the house.
- **Overflowing appliance (washing machine, dishwasher):** Pull the machine away from the wall and turn the braided supply lines off by hand.
- **Roof leak or storm intrusion:** You can't stop rain, but you can reduce what enters — move valuables, place buckets, and use towels to dam doorways.
- **Sewage backup:** Do not touch the water. Raw sewage contains pathogens that require protective gear. Leave the area and call a professional before doing anything else.

Once the source is controlled, go to your breaker panel and switch off power to any room with standing water. Don't walk through the water to reach an outlet or switch — electricity and water is a life-threatening combination. If the panel itself is in a flooded area, call your utility provider (Mississippi Power serves most of the Gulfport area) and ask them to disconnect service at the meter.

## Document Everything Before You Touch Anything

This step feels counterintuitive when you're staring at soaked carpet, but it matters enormously for your insurance claim. Spend 10–15 minutes photographing and videoing the damage before you move a single piece of furniture or pull up a rug.

Walk the perimeter of every affected room. Get wide shots showing the full extent of the water, then close-ups of:

- The waterline on walls (even a faint tide mark)
- Damaged flooring, baseboards, and cabinets
- Any personal property sitting in or near the water
- The source of the damage, if visible (the cracked pipe, the failed supply line, the roof penetration)

If you have a timestamp feature on your phone camera, turn it on. Insurance adjusters use photo metadata to verify when damage occurred relative to when the policy was active. After photographing, call your insurance company's claims line — most have 24-hour lines — and open a claim before you begin any cleanup. Some policies require you to report promptly; waiting even a day can complicate the process.

## What to Remove, What to Leave, and What Not to Do

There's a narrow window — roughly 24 to 48 hours — before wet materials begin to support mold growth. In the Mississippi Gulf Coast's humid climate, that window can be even shorter in summer months when indoor humidity is already elevated. Moving fast on the right things matters.

**Do these things:**

1. Remove standing water with a wet/dry shop vac if you have one. Even removing an inch of water significantly slows the spread into subfloor layers.
2. Pull up wet area rugs and move them outside to dry — or discard them if they were sitting in sewage-contaminated water.
3. Open cabinets and closet doors in affected rooms to allow air circulation.
4. Place aluminum foil or wood blocks under furniture legs to prevent rust and tannin stains from transferring to wet carpet.
5. Run air conditioning if the weather allows — your AC acts as a dehumidifier and slows the conditions that mold needs.

**Do not do these things:**

- **Don't use a standard household fan to dry a room with wet drywall.** Fans move air, but without dehumidification, you're just spreading moisture-laden air into adjacent spaces and potentially pushing it into wall cavities where it can't escape.
- **Don't pull up carpet padding yourself** unless you're certain the subfloor is solid wood or concrete. Wet padding hides the true moisture level in the subfloor beneath it, and a professional needs to read those moisture readings before deciding what stays and what goes.
- **Don't use a gas-powered generator indoors** for pumps or fans — carbon monoxide poisoning is a real risk in enclosed, already-stressed situations.
- **Don't discard damaged materials** until your insurance adjuster or a restoration contractor has documented them. Throwing away a ruined couch before it's photographed and listed means losing that claim item.

## When the Damage Is Beyond DIY

A slow drip caught early in a bathroom with tile floors is one thing. Most water damage situations that bring people to this page are something else. Here are the signs that professional water damage restoration is the right call:

- **The water came from a contaminated source** — a toilet overflow, a backed-up floor drain, or floodwater from outside. Category 2 and Category 3 water (industry classifications for gray and black water) require protective equipment, antimicrobial treatment, and specific disposal procedures.
- **The water was present for more than a few hours before you noticed it.** If you came home to a flooded room, the water has already had time to wick up drywall and into insulation. Moisture meters and thermal imaging cameras — tools a restoration crew brings — are the only way to know how far it actually traveled.
- **The affected area is larger than roughly 10 square feet of flooring.** At that scale, consumer-grade equipment won't dry the structure fast enough to prevent mold.
- **You can smell something musty within 24–48 hours.** That earthy, damp smell is microbial activity starting. It doesn't mean mold is fully colonized yet, but it means conditions are right.
- **The water reached wall cavities, insulation, or a crawl space.** These spaces trap moisture and are invisible to the naked eye. Without proper drying equipment positioned inside the wall or under the floor, they stay wet for weeks.

Professional restoration crews use industrial air movers, refrigerant dehumidifiers, and moisture mapping to dry a structure to its pre-loss moisture content — not just until it looks dry on the surface. That's the standard that prevents a mold remediation bill from arriving 30 days later.

## The Days After: What Recovery Actually Looks Like

Once emergency drying begins — whether you've started it yourself or a crew is on-site — the process takes longer than most homeowners expect. A properly dried structure typically takes 3–5 days of continuous equipment operation, with moisture readings taken daily to confirm progress. Rushing this step, or pulling equipment early because the floor "feels dry," is the most common reason people end up with mold problems weeks after a water event.

After drying is confirmed:

- Damaged drywall sections are cut out and replaced
- Flooring that couldn't be saved is removed and new material is installed
- Any affected insulation is replaced
- The space is treated with antimicrobial agents as needed
- A final clearance inspection confirms moisture levels are back to baseline

If your home is older — and much of the housing stock in coastal Mississippi dates to the mid-20th century — there's also the possibility of encountering lead paint or asbestos-containing materials during demolition. A qualified restoration contractor will test before cutting into walls or disturbing floor tiles.
