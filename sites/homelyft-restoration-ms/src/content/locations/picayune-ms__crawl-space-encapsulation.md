Picayune sits in Pearl River County at the edge of Mississippi's coastal plain, where the humidity rarely lets up and the soil holds moisture like a sponge after every Gulf-driven rain event. That combination — high ambient humidity, clay-heavy ground that swells and contracts with the seasons, and a housing stock that includes plenty of pier-and-beam construction built before modern vapor barriers were standard — makes crawl spaces here unusually vulnerable to standing moisture, wood rot, and mold colonization. If you've noticed musty air drifting up through your floors or seen soft spots developing near load-bearing beams, the crawl space is almost certainly where the problem started.

## Why Picayune Properties See Crawl Space Moisture Problems

Pearl River County averages well over 60 inches of rainfall per year, and much of it doesn't drain quickly. The relatively flat terrain around Picayune means water lingers near foundations longer than it would in hillier parts of the state. Older homes — particularly those built in the 1950s through 1970s — were often constructed with open, unvented crawl spaces that relied on cross-ventilation to manage humidity. That approach simply doesn't work in a climate where outdoor air is itself saturated for months at a time. Bringing humid outside air into a crawl space during a Mississippi summer doesn't dry anything out; it feeds the moisture cycle.

Beyond the climate, the region's soils contribute directly to the problem. Clay-rich ground around Picayune expands when wet and shrinks when dry, creating subtle but repeated movement beneath pier footings. That movement can open small gaps in rim joists and sill plates, giving ground moisture another entry point. In homes where the original vapor barrier — if there was one — has degraded into brittle plastic shreds, the crawl space floor is essentially an open evaporation pan.

## Our Crawl Space Encapsulation Process in Picayune

Encapsulation here isn't a one-size-fits-all job. Before any material goes down, the crawl space gets a thorough inspection: moisture readings at multiple points across the subfloor, a check for active wood decay or mold growth on joists and beams, and an assessment of existing drainage and grading around the foundation perimeter. If there's standing water or saturated soil, that has to be addressed before sealing anything in.

Once the space is clean and dry, we install a reinforced polyethylene barrier — typically a 20-mil liner — across the entire crawl space floor and up the foundation walls, sealed at every seam and termination point. Seams that lift or gap over time are one of the most common failure points in cheaper encapsulation jobs, so we use a combination of tape rated for the application and mechanical fasteners at the walls. A sealed crawl space also needs a way to manage any residual moisture that accumulates inside the envelope, which is why we assess whether a crawl space dehumidifier or a connection to the home's conditioned air system is appropriate for each specific property.

For homes with older wood framing that shows early signs of moisture damage but not yet structural compromise, we document the condition carefully — both for the homeowner's records and for any insurance or disclosure purposes.

## Equipment and Methods We Use for Crawl Space Encapsulation

The liner material matters more than most homeowners realize. A 6-mil poly sheet — the kind sold at hardware stores — punctures easily during installation and degrades within a few years in a humid Mississippi crawl space. We use reinforced liners with a higher mil rating specifically because crawl spaces in this region see repeated thermal cycling and occasional pest intrusion. The liner is only as effective as its weakest seam, so every overlap gets a minimum 12-inch seam sealed with compatible tape before any fastening.

For moisture control inside the sealed envelope, we size dehumidifiers to the actual cubic footage of the space, not to a generic square-footage estimate. An undersized unit runs constantly without achieving the target humidity range; an oversized unit short-cycles and doesn't adequately dehumidify. We aim for interior crawl space relative humidity below 55% — the threshold above which mold growth becomes likely on wood substrates, per IICRC AMRT guidelines our team is trained to.
