## Why Laurel Properties Face Elevated Asbestos Exposure Risk

Mississippi's Gulf Coast climate gets most of the humidity headlines, but Laurel sits in Jones County with its own pattern of heat cycling, occasional severe storms, and the kind of slow moisture intrusion that degrades building materials over decades. When insulation around ductwork or plumbing gets wet and dries repeatedly, it becomes brittle. When a popcorn ceiling absorbs humidity year after year, the texture coat can begin to flake. Both scenarios can release asbestos fibers into living space without any renovation work at all.

Laurel's downtown commercial corridor and surrounding residential blocks include a significant number of structures built between 1930 and 1975 — the precise window when chrysotile and amosite asbestos were used most heavily in American construction. Floor tiles in 9×9-inch squares (a near-certain indicator of asbestos-containing adhesive or tile body), corrugated pipe wrap on steam radiators, and spray-applied fireproofing on steel beams are all common findings in buildings of that era. Before any demolition, renovation, or even aggressive cleaning of these materials, Mississippi Department of Environmental Quality (MDEQ) regulations require that suspect materials be tested and, if necessary, abated by a licensed contractor.

## Our Asbestos Abatement Process in Laurel

Every project starts with an asbestos inspection — a systematic visual survey and bulk sampling of suspect materials by a trained technician. Samples go to an accredited laboratory for polarized light microscopy (PLM) analysis, which identifies fiber type and concentration. Results typically return within a few business days, and we walk you through the report in plain language before any abatement work begins.

When abatement is required, the work area is isolated using 6-mil polyethylene sheeting and negative air pressure machines fitted with HEPA filtration. This containment prevents fibers from migrating into adjacent rooms or HVAC systems. Regulated asbestos-containing material (RACM) is wetted to suppress fiber release, carefully removed, double-bagged in labeled disposal bags, and transported to a licensed disposal facility — all in compliance with MDEQ and EPA National Emission Standards for Hazardous Air Pollutants (NESHAP) requirements.

For popcorn ceiling asbestos removal specifically, we score and wet the texture coat in manageable sections rather than dry-scraping, which is the method most likely to release fibers. Once the ceiling is clear, a final air clearance test using phase contrast microscopy (PCM) confirms that fiber counts in the work area have returned to background levels before containment is removed.
