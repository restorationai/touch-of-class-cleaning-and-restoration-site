## Why Laurel Properties See Water Damage Issues

Jones County averages over 55 inches of rainfall per year — well above the national average — and Laurel's position along the Tallahala Creek watershed means that heavy rain events can saturate soil quickly, pushing water toward foundations and into crawl spaces before the storm has even passed. Older neighborhoods in Laurel feature a significant share of homes built before 1970, many with pier-and-beam foundations that leave crawl spaces exposed to ground moisture. These crawl spaces trap humidity year-round, and when a plumbing failure or roof leak adds liquid water to an already damp environment, the damage spreads laterally through subfloor assemblies in ways that aren't always visible from above.

Beyond storm events, aging supply lines and galvanized steel pipes common in mid-century Laurel homes are prone to pinhole leaks and sudden failures. A slow drip inside a wall cavity can go undetected for weeks, silently wetting insulation and framing until a homeowner notices a soft spot in the floor or a musty odor that won't go away. That combination — older housing stock, high ambient humidity, and heavy seasonal rain — makes water damage one of the most common property emergencies in this part of Mississippi.

## Our Water Damage Restoration Process in Laurel

Every job begins with a thorough moisture assessment using thermal imaging cameras and calibrated moisture meters. In Laurel homes with pier-and-beam construction, that assessment always includes the crawl space — water that enters through a foundation vent or a broken drain line migrates upward into subfloor sheathing and floor joists before it ever reaches the living space above. Identifying the full extent of saturation before extraction begins prevents callbacks and incomplete drying.

Once the scope is mapped, we deploy truck-mounted and portable extraction units to remove standing water, followed by commercial-grade air movers and refrigerant or desiccant dehumidifiers sized to the affected area. Structural drying in Laurel requires equipment calibrated for high ambient humidity — running a residential dehumidifier in a Jones County summer is like trying to bail a boat with a coffee cup. Our drying plans follow the IICRC S500 standard, with daily moisture readings logged until all affected materials reach documented dry standard. If lead-containing materials are disturbed during the process — a real consideration in pre-1978 Laurel homes — our EPA Lead-Safe certification covers the work.

## Laurel Insurance Coordination

Most standard homeowners insurance policies cover sudden and accidental water damage — a burst pipe, an appliance failure, an ice maker line that lets go overnight. What they typically do not cover is damage that developed gradually from a slow leak the insurer can argue was a maintenance issue. When we document a loss in Laurel, we photograph affected materials systematically, record moisture readings at each measurement point, and prepare a detailed scope of work that gives your adjuster the information needed to process the claim accurately. ## Local Note

In Laurel's older neighborhoods, original hardwood floors — often heart pine or oak — are common, and homeowners understandably want to save them. Heart pine in particular is extremely dense and slow to release moisture, which means drying timelines for these floors run significantly longer than for modern engineered wood or laminate. We use floor mat drying systems that draw moisture upward through the wood rather than relying solely on ambient air movement, which reduces the risk of cupping and warping. It takes patience, but saving a century-old heart pine floor is almost always worth the extra drying days compared to a full replacement.
