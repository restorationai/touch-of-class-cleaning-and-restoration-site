## Why Pass Christian Properties See Sewage Backup Issues

The combination of coastal elevation, aging municipal infrastructure, and the area's history with major storm events creates conditions that make sewage problems more common here than in inland Mississippi communities. Many homes along the beachside corridors of Pass Christian were rebuilt or substantially renovated after Hurricane Katrina, but the sewer laterals connecting those homes to the city main were not always replaced at the same time. A lateral that's fifteen or twenty years older than the house above it can root-intrude, offset at joints, or simply collapse under the weight of saturated soil after a heavy Gulf rain.

Septic systems are also common on the western and northern edges of Pass Christian where city sewer service doesn't reach. Those systems are particularly vulnerable during the wet season — roughly November through March — when the water table rises and the drain field loses its ability to absorb effluent. An overloaded septic tank can push sewage back through the lowest fixture in the house before the homeowner realizes the system is stressed.

Older slab-on-grade construction, which is widespread in Pass Christian's post-storm rebuilds, means there's often no crawl space to inspect. Sewage that seeps under a slab can go undetected for days, wicking into concrete and the base of interior walls before the smell becomes obvious.

## Our Sewage Cleanup and Sanitization Process in Pass Christian

Every sewage call starts with a containment assessment before any equipment moves in. We identify the source — municipal backup, failed lateral, septic overflow — and confirm it's been addressed or isolated before cleanup begins. Working on an active sewage source wastes time and re-contaminates cleaned areas.

Once the source is controlled, the process moves through these stages:

- **Extraction and removal**: Standing sewage and saturated materials are extracted using truck-mounted and portable units. Flooring, drywall, and insulation that have absorbed Category 3 water are removed rather than dried in place — porous materials cannot be reliably sanitized after sewage contact, and attempting to dry them creates ongoing microbial risk.
- **Sanitization**: Hard surfaces — concrete, tile, studs, joists — are treated with EPA-registered disinfectants formulated for Category 3 contamination. We follow IICRC S500 protocols for contact time and surface coverage.
- **Structural drying**: After sanitization, affected structural cavities are dried using desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers and directed airflow. In Pass Christian's humid Gulf Coast climate, ambient humidity can exceed 80% on summer afternoons, which means drying equipment has to work against the outdoor air. We monitor moisture readings daily until the structure reaches regional equilibrium moisture content.
- **Clearance documentation**: Before reconstruction begins, we provide written moisture readings and a photo-documented scope of the remediated area — useful both for your insurance adjuster and for any required permit inspections.

## Local Note

One thing that comes up specifically in Pass Christian's post-Katrina rebuilds: many of those homes used concrete board backer and tile in areas that previously had vinyl or hardwood, specifically because those materials seemed more water-resistant. In a sewage event, that's partially true — tile itself doesn't absorb contamination the way wood does. We probe beneath tile in sewage-affected areas rather than assuming the hard surface is a barrier, because leaving contaminated mortar in place under a "clean" tile floor is a mold and odor problem waiting to resurface.
