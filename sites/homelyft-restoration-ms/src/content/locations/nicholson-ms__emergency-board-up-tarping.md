## Why Nicholson Properties Face Distinct Board-Up and Tarping Needs

Pearl River County's climate is the primary driver. Nicholson receives some of the highest annual rainfall totals in Mississippi — frequently exceeding 65 inches — and sits in a corridor that funnels tropical moisture northward from the Gulf even in non-hurricane seasons. A roof opening that would stay dry for two days in a drier climate can take on significant water intrusion within hours here during a summer thunderstorm cycle.

The local housing stock adds another layer. Much of Nicholson's residential development consists of wood-frame construction built between the 1960s and 1990s, with roof systems that use dimensional lumber rather than engineered trusses. When a section of that framing is fire-damaged or storm-fractured, it loses load-bearing capacity quickly, and tarping has to account for that instability — anchoring points need to be chosen carefully to avoid pulling on compromised rafters. Older pier-and-beam foundations common in this part of Pearl River County also mean that ground-level access for staging ladders and equipment sometimes requires working around soft soil conditions after rain.

## Our Board-Up and Tarping Process in Nicholson

The first step on any Nicholson job is a rapid structural read — walking the perimeter to identify which openings are actively exposing the interior to weather or entry, and which structural members are sound enough to anchor to. For fire-damaged structures, our IICRC FSRT-certified technicians assess whether the framing around a burned opening can hold fasteners before any board is cut.

For window and door openings, we use minimum ¾-inch CDX plywood, secured with structural screws rather than nails, which resist the wind uplift that Pearl River County storms generate. Boards are cut to overlap the frame by at least two inches on each side and are sealed at the edges with foam backer to reduce wind-driven rain infiltration.

For roof tarping, we use heavy-duty poly tarps rated at no less than 6 mils — often 10-mil or reinforced woven poly on larger spans — and we anchor with cap nails and wood battens rather than relying on tarp grommets alone, which fail in sustained wind. The tarp is run past the ridge line when possible so that rain sheds to both sides rather than pooling at the peak. On roofs where the decking itself is compromised, we lay the batten boards across intact sheathing sections to distribute load.
