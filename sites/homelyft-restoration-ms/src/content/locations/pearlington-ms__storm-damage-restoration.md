When a hurricane or severe thunderstorm rolls through Hancock County, Pearlington doesn't just get wind — it gets storm surge, saturated soils that can't absorb another drop, and debris loads from the dense bottomland hardwoods that line the riverbanks. ## Why Pearlington Properties See Severe Storm Damage

Pearlington's geography is the defining factor. The community sits at or near sea level along the Pearl River, and the surrounding wetlands and tidal flats offer almost no natural barrier to surge from the Gulf. Katrina erased much of the town in 2005, and the homes rebuilt in the years after range from FEMA-elevated slabs to older pier-and-beam structures that predate modern wind-load requirements. That mix matters for restoration: an elevated slab home may escape flooding but suffer catastrophic roof damage from wind-driven debris, while a lower pier-and-beam structure can take on several inches of surge before the wind even becomes the main concern.

The dense tree canopy — live oaks, water oaks, and loblolly pines — that shades most residential lots in this part of Hancock County becomes a liability in a named storm. Root systems in the saturated alluvial soil near the river hold poorly under sustained winds, and large trees coming down on roofs or across driveways are among the most common calls we handle here after a significant weather event.

## Our Storm Damage Restoration Process in Pearlington

The first priority after any storm is stopping further damage. That means emergency tarping of breached roofs, boarding openings where windows or doors have failed, and — critically in this low-lying area — identifying whether any standing water inside the structure is rainwater intrusion or surge-related, because the two require different remediation paths under IICRC S500 standards.

Once the structure is stabilized, the assessment phase documents every affected material: roofing, sheathing, wall cavities, insulation, flooring systems, and any structural members that took impact from fallen trees or airborne debris. Because many Pearlington homes were rebuilt post-Katrina with insurance oversight, there is often a clear pre-loss record to work from, which helps when building a scope for the carrier.

Drying and structural repair follow in sequence. Our IICRC-certified team uses commercial desiccant and refrigerant drying equipment calibrated to the high ambient humidity typical of coastal Mississippi — conditions where standard residential dehumidifiers simply can't pull moisture fast enough to prevent secondary mold growth, which can begin colonizing wet framing within 48 to 72 hours in summer temperatures.

## Pearlington Insurance & Storm Claim Coordination

Hancock County properties in the Pearl River floodplain frequently carry both homeowners wind coverage and a separate NFIP flood policy, and the line between what each policy covers is one of the most contested points in a storm claim. Wind-driven rain entering through a breached roof is typically a wind claim; water entering at grade from surge is typically a flood claim. We document the damage in a way that distinguishes the two — photographing tide lines, debris patterns, and entry points — so the adjuster has a clear record rather than a disputed one.

For properties where tree removal is part of the scope, we coordinate with licensed tree services and document the tree's point of impact on the structure, which is relevant to both the property claim and any potential liability question if the tree originated on a neighboring lot.

## Local Note

One thing that catches crews off guard in Pearlington if they haven't worked this corridor before: the post-Katrina rebuilds along the river used a lot of ICF (insulated concrete form) construction because of its wind resistance, and ICF walls respond very differently to moisture intrusion than wood-frame walls do. The foam core can trap water at the interface between the concrete and interior finish, and standard moisture meters read the concrete rather than the foam. We use both pin and pinless meters at multiple depths on any ICF wall that shows signs of water entry — a step that's easy to skip and expensive to miss.
