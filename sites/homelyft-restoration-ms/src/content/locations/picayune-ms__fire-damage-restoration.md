Picayune sits in Pearl River County at the edge of Mississippi's piney woods, where the humidity rarely lets up and older wood-frame homes line neighborhoods that have seen decades of Gulf Coast weather. When fire moves through one of these structures — whether it starts in a kitchen, a utility room, or from a lightning strike during a summer storm — the damage compounds fast. Smoke and soot don't just stain surfaces; in Picayune's humid climate, they bond to walls, ductwork, and insulation within hours, and the moisture already present in the air accelerates the corrosion of metals and the warping of wood framing before cleanup even begins.

## Why Picayune Properties Are Particularly Vulnerable After a Fire

Pearl River County's housing stock skews older, and many Picayune homes were built with pine framing, tongue-and-groove subfloors, and plaster-over-lath wall systems that predate modern fire-resistant construction standards. These materials burn differently than contemporary OSB or drywall — they char more deeply, and the porous nature of old-growth pine holds smoke odor molecules in a way that surface cleaning alone cannot address. The region's subtropical humidity, which regularly pushes above 80% in summer, means that char residue on exposed framing begins absorbing ambient moisture almost immediately after a fire is extinguished, creating conditions where secondary damage — rust, mildew, and structural swelling — can develop within 24 to 48 hours if the structure isn't stabilized.

Picayune also relies on above-ground utility runs in many older sections of the city, and fire events frequently damage exterior meter boxes and service entrance cables. Coordinating with Entergy Mississippi to safely disconnect and restore power is a routine part of post-fire site work here — something that affects the sequencing of our structural assessment and board-up process.

## Our Fire Damage Restoration Process in Picayune

Every fire loss is different, but the sequence of work follows a disciplined framework. After an initial safety walkthrough and documentation — photographs, moisture readings, and a structural stability check — the crew moves into emergency services: boarding windows and doors, tarping compromised roof sections, and removing standing water left by firefighting efforts. That water matters more than most homeowners expect; it saturates insulation and subfloors and, in Picayune's climate, becomes a mold risk within two to three days.

Once the structure is secured and dry, the restoration work shifts to smoke and soot removal. Our IICRC FSRT-certified technicians use dry chemical sponges on flat surfaces, thermal fogging and hydroxyl generation to neutralize odor molecules embedded in framing cavities, and HEPA-filtered negative air machines to capture fine particulate. In homes with older plaster walls — common in Picayune's mid-century neighborhoods — soot penetrates the finish coat and sometimes requires skim-coat resurfacing rather than simple cleaning. Ductwork is inspected and cleaned separately, because smoke travels the HVAC system and will re-contaminate a cleaned space if the ducts are overlooked.

Because many Picayune properties contain pre-1978 painted surfaces, our EPA Lead-Safe Certified team follows RRP protocols during any demolition or abrasive cleaning of older substrates — a requirement that affects both the timeline and the disposal process for debris.
