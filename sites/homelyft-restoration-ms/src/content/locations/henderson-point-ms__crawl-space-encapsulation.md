Henderson Point sits on a narrow peninsula between the Bay of St. Louis and the open Gulf, and that geography does something particular to the ground beneath older homes here: the soil stays damp almost year-round, humidity rarely drops below uncomfortable, and crawl spaces that were never sealed become slow-motion moisture traps. Crawl space encapsulation in Henderson Point isn't a luxury upgrade — for many homes along this stretch of the Mississippi Gulf Coast, it's the difference between structural integrity and a floor system quietly rotting from below.

## Why Henderson Point Properties Are Especially Vulnerable to Crawl Space Moisture

The peninsula's position means prevailing Gulf winds push warm, saturated air inland even on clear days. Ground-level relative humidity in Hancock and Harrison County regularly sits above 80% for months at a stretch, and an open or vented crawl space acts like a sponge — pulling that moisture up through floor joists, subfloor sheathing, and eventually into living areas. Homes built before the 1980s, which make up a significant share of the housing stock in this part of coastal Mississippi, were typically constructed with minimal vapor barriers or none at all. A thin sheet of 6-mil poly laid directly on bare soil — common in older Gulf Coast construction — degrades quickly in the heat and provides almost no real protection against rising ground moisture.

After major storm events, the problem compounds. Floodwater that recedes leaves behind saturated soil that off-gasses moisture for weeks. Homeowners often address visible water damage upstairs while the crawl space continues wicking humidity into the structure long after the carpets are dry.

## Our Crawl Space Encapsulation Process in Henderson Point

Every encapsulation project starts with a thorough crawl space inspection — checking existing vapor barriers (if any), measuring moisture content in the wood framing, looking for standing water, mold colonies, or pest evidence, and noting any compromised vents or foundation cracks. In coastal Mississippi, we frequently find that fiberglass insulation between floor joists has absorbed enough moisture to become useless or even to hold mold growth against the subfloor. That material comes out before encapsulation begins.

The encapsulation itself involves:

- **Ground cover installation** — a reinforced, puncture-resistant polyethylene liner (typically 20-mil) sealed to the foundation walls and around piers, eliminating the soil-to-air moisture pathway entirely.
- **Wall liner application** — extending the barrier up the interior foundation walls and mechanically fastening it above the flood zone line where applicable.
- **Seam and penetration sealing** — every pipe, wire, and column penetration is taped and sealed; gaps are where moisture finds its way back in.
- **Drainage and dehumidification** — in Henderson Point's climate, a sealed crawl space still needs active moisture management. We assess whether a crawl space dehumidifier, a sump pit, or both are appropriate given the site's drainage characteristics.
- **Vent sealing** — traditional foundation vents are closed and sealed; the encapsulated space is conditioned rather than ventilated, which is the current building science standard for humid climates like coastal Mississippi.

The IICRC AMRT certification our team carries means we're trained to identify and address mold before sealing the space — encapsulating over active mold growth simply traps the problem inside.

## Local Note: What the Storm History Here Tells Us About Crawl Spaces

One thing that becomes clear working in homes along this part of the Gulf Coast is how much Katrina-era repairs shaped the current condition of crawl spaces. Many homes were partially rebuilt or elevated after 2005, but the crawl space work was often done quickly and under pressure — vapor barriers were installed but not properly sealed at seams, dehumidifiers were added but never sized correctly for the space, or the original bare-soil floor was simply left untouched beneath a new floor system. If your home was repaired or rebuilt in the 2005–2010 window, it's worth having the crawl space inspected even if it was "done" at the time. What passed inspection during a rushed rebuilding period may not be performing adequately now, nearly two decades later.
