## Why Soso Properties See Asbestos Concerns

Jones County's building boom from the 1950s through the late 1970s left a lasting footprint. During those decades, asbestos was woven into virtually every category of building material because it was cheap, fire-resistant, and readily available. Rural communities like Soso often saw construction continue with older material stocks even into the early 1980s, meaning the cutoff date isn't as clean here as it might be in a larger metro area.

The region's heat and humidity accelerate the aging of older building materials. Asbestos-containing materials that were once stable can become friable — meaning they crumble and release fibers — when they've been subjected to decades of Mississippi summers, roof leaks, or the kind of slow moisture intrusion that's common in pier-and-beam homes. A popcorn ceiling that looks intact may already be shedding microscopic fibers if the substrate has been wet even once.

## Our Asbestos Abatement Process in Soso

Every project starts with a formal asbestos inspection and bulk sampling before any removal work begins. Samples are sent to an accredited laboratory, and we don't proceed to abatement until results are confirmed — this protects you, your family, and our crew.

Once testing confirms the presence of asbestos-containing materials, the affected area is isolated using negative air pressure containment with HEPA filtration. This is especially important in older Soso homes where HVAC ductwork may run through multiple zones — cross-contamination through a shared air system is a real risk that we account for in the containment plan. Wet methods are used throughout removal to suppress fiber release, materials are double-bagged and labeled per Mississippi DEQ and EPA requirements, and waste is transported to a licensed disposal facility.

Final air clearance testing is conducted after abatement and before containment is removed, giving you a documented record that the space is safe — a record that matters if you ever sell the property or file an insurance claim.

## Local Note

One thing that comes up specifically in older Jones County homes is the combination of asbestos floor tile and the adhesive — called "black mastic" — used to bond it to the subfloor. In many Soso-area homes built in the 1960s and 1970s, both the tile and the mastic contain asbestos. Removing just the tile and leaving the mastic is sometimes an acceptable approach under Mississippi DEQ guidelines if the mastic is in good condition and will be covered, but that decision requires a licensed assessment — not a judgment call made during a DIY renovation. We've seen homeowners unknowingly grind through mastic with a floor sander, which is one of the highest-exposure scenarios in residential abatement work.
