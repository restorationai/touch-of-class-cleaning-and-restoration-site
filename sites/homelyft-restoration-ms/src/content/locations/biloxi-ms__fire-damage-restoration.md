## Why Biloxi Properties Face Distinct Fire Damage Challenges

Biloxi's building stock tells a complicated story. The city has a mix of post-Katrina rebuilds — many constructed quickly between 2006 and 2010 — alongside older wood-frame cottages and elevated pier-and-beam homes that predate modern fire codes. The post-storm rebuilds often used engineered lumber and composite materials that char and off-gas differently than old-growth pine framing. Smoke penetrates engineered OSB subflooring faster than solid wood, which means odor and residue can reach structural cavities before the fire is fully out.

The coastal climate compounds this. Biloxi averages over 60 inches of rain per year, and relative humidity regularly exceeds 80 percent in summer months. When firefighters knock down a blaze with water, that moisture combines with existing ambient humidity to create ideal conditions for secondary mold growth — sometimes within 24 to 48 hours of the fire event. A restoration plan here has to address fire, smoke, and moisture simultaneously, not sequentially.
