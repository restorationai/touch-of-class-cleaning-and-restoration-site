Vancleave sits in a part of Jackson County where pine-heavy lots, older wood-frame construction, and the kind of dry summer heat that turns pine straw into tinder create real fire risk for homeowners. When a fire moves through a house here — whether it started in a kitchen, a carport, or an outbuilding — the damage rarely stops at the charred walls. Smoke and soot travel through every gap in an older wood-frame structure, and the humid Gulf Coast air that rolls in after a fire is extinguished begins working against recovery almost immediately.

## Why Vancleave Properties Face Particular Fire Damage Challenges

Much of the residential building stock in Vancleave consists of wood-frame homes built on pier-and-beam or slab foundations, many with crawl spaces and attic spaces that are largely open to each other through wall cavities. When fire burns in one part of the structure, superheated smoke and toxic combustion byproducts migrate through those cavities and deposit soot on surfaces far from the origin point — inside wall voids, on attic insulation, along HVAC ductwork. You can repaint a ceiling and still smell smoke six months later if the ductwork and wall cavities weren't addressed.

Jackson County's climate adds a second layer of complexity. The region averages over 60 inches of rainfall annually, and relative humidity regularly climbs above 80 percent during summer months. Once a fire is out and the structure is open to the elements — windows broken, roof compromised — moisture infiltration begins within hours. Soot and char absorb that moisture, accelerating corrosion on metal fixtures, warping hardwood floors, and creating conditions where secondary mold growth can begin within 24 to 48 hours. The fire damage and the moisture damage become a single, compounding problem.

## Our Fire Damage Restoration Process in Vancleave

The first priority after the fire marshal clears a property is stabilization — boarding windows, tarping roof penetrations, and securing the structure so weather can't compound the loss. From there, the process follows the IICRC FSRT framework our team is certified under:

**Scope and documentation.** Every affected surface is photographed, measured, and catalogued before anything is removed. This documentation is the foundation of the insurance claim and the restoration plan.

**Controlled demolition of non-salvageable material.** Charred framing, saturated insulation, and fire-damaged drywall come out in a controlled sequence. We separate salvageable structural lumber from material that needs replacement and flag anything that may trigger a permit requirement with Jackson County.

**Soot and smoke residue removal.** Dry soot from fast-burning pine fires behaves differently than the oily, wet smoke residue left by synthetic materials or slow smoldering. Our IICRC FSRT-certified technicians use the appropriate chemistry and method for each surface — dry sponge cleaning for flat soot, alkaline cleaners for protein residue, thermal fogging or hydroxyl generation for odor in wall cavities and ductwork.

**Structural drying.** Because Vancleave's humidity is a constant factor, we deploy commercial dehumidifiers and air movers immediately after cleaning. Moisture readings are logged daily until materials reach regional equilibrium moisture content.

**Reconstruction.** Once the structure is clean, dry, and documented, reconstruction begins — framing, insulation, drywall, paint, and finish work coordinated to match existing materials wherever possible.

Older wood-frame homes in the Vancleave area — particularly those built in the 1970s and 1980s — frequently used fiberglass batt insulation in walls and attics that was installed without a continuous vapor barrier.
