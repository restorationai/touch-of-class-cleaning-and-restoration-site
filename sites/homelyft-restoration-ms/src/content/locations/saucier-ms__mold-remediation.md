## Why Saucier Properties See Mold Problems

The geography here works against homeowners in a few specific ways. Saucier's tree canopy is dense — the area's proximity to the DeSoto National Forest means many properties sit under a near-continuous shade cover that slows evaporation from rooflines, siding, and crawl spaces. Shaded exterior walls stay damp longer after rain, and crawl spaces under older pier-and-beam homes in this area rarely dry out fully between storm seasons. That persistent ground-level moisture is one of the most common starting points we see for mold colonies that eventually migrate up into subfloor framing and insulation.

Harrison County's clay-dominant soil also drains slowly, which means water from heavy rain events pools against foundation walls and slab edges longer than it would in sandier coastal soils closer to the beach. Homes that have experienced any settling or minor foundation movement — not uncommon in this region — can develop small gaps where that pooled water finds its way inside.

Contaminated materials — drywall, insulation, wood framing where necessary — are removed, bagged, and disposed of according to Mississippi Department of Environmental Quality guidelines.
