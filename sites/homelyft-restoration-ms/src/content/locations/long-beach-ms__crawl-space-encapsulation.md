Long Beach sits in one of the most humidity-saturated corridors on the Gulf Coast, where summer air regularly holds moisture levels that would be considered extreme almost anywhere else in the country. That persistent humidity doesn't just affect the living spaces of homes here — it migrates directly into crawl spaces, where it condenses on joists, insulation, and soil, quietly setting the stage for wood rot, mold colonization, and structural softening that homeowners often don't notice until floors begin to flex underfoot. Crawl space encapsulation is the most effective long-term answer to that cycle, and doing it right in Long Beach means accounting for conditions that are genuinely different from inland Mississippi.

## Why Long Beach Homes Are Especially Vulnerable Below the Floor

Harrison County's coastal climate delivers a combination that crawl spaces handle poorly: high annual rainfall, salt-laden air from the Gulf, and summer relative humidity that frequently exceeds 80 percent even on days without rain. Homes built on pier-and-beam foundations — common throughout Long Beach's older residential neighborhoods — were often constructed with open or minimally vented crawl spaces that made sense in an era before modern vapor barrier science. Today those same crawl spaces act as collection points for ground moisture that evaporates upward through the soil, condenses on cooler structural surfaces, and feeds the biological processes that degrade wood and foster mold growth.

The soil composition along this stretch of the Gulf Coast compounds the problem. The sandy, porous ground common in coastal Harrison County drains quickly after rain but also allows moisture to wick upward readily during dry spells as capillary action pulls subsurface water toward the surface. A crawl space without a sealed vapor barrier is essentially in direct communication with that moisture reservoir year-round.

## Our Crawl Space Encapsulation Process in Long Beach

Every encapsulation project begins with a thorough inspection of the crawl space — not a quick visual scan, but a physical assessment of the soil condition, existing insulation, joist and beam integrity, any standing water or evidence of prior flooding, and the current ventilation setup. In Long Beach, we frequently find fiberglass batt insulation hanging between joists that has absorbed enough moisture to lose most of its R-value; that material is removed before any barrier work begins.

Once the space is cleared and any damaged structural components are documented, we install a heavy-duty polyethylene vapor barrier — typically 20-mil reinforced liner — across the entire ground surface, running it up the foundation walls and sealing it at the perimeter with adhesive and mechanical fasteners. Seams are overlapped and taped with purpose-made encapsulation tape rated for long-term adhesion in humid environments. In crawl spaces where moisture intrusion is more aggressive, we pair the barrier with a drainage mat layer beneath it to redirect any water that does find its way in toward a sump point rather than allowing it to pool.

Ventilation is addressed on a case-by-case basis. Some Long Beach homes benefit from closing existing foundation vents and conditioning the crawl space as part of the home's thermal envelope; others are better served by controlled mechanical ventilation. We assess which approach fits the specific structure and the homeowner's HVAC configuration before making a recommendation.

The project is documented with before-and-after photography and moisture readings so homeowners have a clear record — useful both for their own reference and for any future insurance or real estate disclosure purposes.

## Equipment and Methods We Use

The liner material matters significantly in a coastal climate. Thin, single-ply barriers degrade faster under the UV exposure that occurs near open vents and are more susceptible to puncture during the life of the installation. We use reinforced, multi-layer polyethylene rated for long-term performance in high-humidity environments. For spaces with active moisture issues, we deploy commercial-grade dehumidification equipment calibrated to the cubic footage of the crawl space, and we verify final moisture readings before considering the job complete. Our team holds IICRC AMRT (mold) and IICRC WRT (water) certifications, which means the people assessing your crawl space understand the full moisture and microbial picture — not just the barrier installation piece.

## Local Note

One pattern we see consistently in Long Beach is that homes located closer to the bayous and tidal areas in the southern part of the city tend to have crawl spaces where the soil never fully dries between rain events — the water table simply sits too close to grade for passive drying to occur. In those situations, a vapor barrier alone is sometimes insufficient without also addressing drainage. Homeowners in those areas who have had encapsulation done elsewhere and are still seeing moisture readings above 55 percent relative humidity inside the crawl space should have the drainage situation evaluated before assuming the barrier failed.
