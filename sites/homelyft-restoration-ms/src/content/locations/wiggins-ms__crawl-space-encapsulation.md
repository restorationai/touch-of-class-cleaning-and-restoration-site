Stone County's humidity doesn't stay outside — it works its way under your house. In Wiggins, where warm Gulf air pushes inland through the pine belt and summer ground moisture levels stay elevated for months at a stretch, an open or poorly vented crawl space is less a minor inconvenience and more a slow structural problem. ## Why Wiggins Properties See Crawl Space Moisture Problems

Stone County sits in a transition zone where sandy loam topsoil gives way to clay-heavy subsoil layers that hold water long after a rain event passes. That clay doesn't drain — it perches moisture against your foundation walls and releases it slowly upward as vapor. Wiggins homes built before the 1990s were typically constructed with open crawl space vents, a design standard that made sense in theory but in practice pulls humid outdoor air directly into the space during summer, where it condenses on cooler joist surfaces.

The region also sees significant rainfall — Stone County regularly records 60-plus inches annually — and even a modest slope toward the house can channel runoff into the crawl space after a heavy storm. Once relative humidity in an enclosed crawl space climbs above 60 percent and stays there, mold colonization can begin within 24 to 48 hours on untreated wood. By the time a homeowner notices a musty smell or soft spots in the floor, the damage is often already weeks old.

## Our Crawl Space Encapsulation Process in Wiggins

Every encapsulation project starts with a full inspection of the crawl space — not a quick look from the access hatch, but a physical crawl through the space to assess joist condition, existing insulation, standing water, vapor barrier remnants, and signs of mold or pest activity. We document what we find before a single material goes in.

From there, the process follows a defined sequence:

1. **Remove and dispose of old insulation and debris.** Fallen batt insulation holds moisture against wood and has to come out before any sealing begins.
2. **Address any standing water or drainage issues.** If the space has a low point that collects water, we install an interior drain channel and sump system before laying barrier material.
3. **Install a reinforced polyethylene vapor barrier.** We use a minimum 12-mil liner across the floor and up the foundation walls, sealed at seams and termination points. Mississippi's ground moisture levels warrant a heavier liner than the 6-mil minimum some contractors use.
4. **Seal foundation vents.** In a fully encapsulated crawl space, traditional vents are closed and sealed — outside air is no longer welcome in the space.
5. **Install a dehumidifier or condition the space with conditioned air.** A sealed crawl space needs active moisture management. We size the unit to the square footage of the space and the moisture load typical for Stone County summers.
6. **Final inspection and documentation.** We photograph the completed installation and provide a written summary you can share with your insurance carrier or a future home buyer.

## Equipment and Materials We Use

The liner material matters more than most homeowners realize. A 6-mil poly sheet — the kind sold in big-box stores — punctures easily during installation and degrades faster under the alkaline conditions common in Mississippi soil. We use a reinforced, antimicrobial-treated liner that holds up to foot traffic during future inspections and resists the mold growth that can occur on the barrier surface itself if conditions are not properly controlled.

For dehumidification, we use commercial-grade units rated for crawl space environments — not repurposed basement dehumidifiers. These units have drain lines that route condensate out of the space automatically, so there's no bucket to empty and no overflow risk during a wet Stone County summer.

Our team holds IICRC AMRT (Applied Microbial Remediation) and IICRC WRT certifications, which means the moisture science behind encapsulation — vapor pressure, dew point, relative humidity targets — is part of how we design every installation, not an afterthought.
