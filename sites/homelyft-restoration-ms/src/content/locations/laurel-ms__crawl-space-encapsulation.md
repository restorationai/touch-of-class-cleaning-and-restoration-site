Laurel sits in the heart of Jones County, where the Piney Woods climate delivers something crawl spaces dread: long stretches of high humidity punctuated by heavy Gulf-moisture rain events that push ground saturation well above what most vapor barriers can handle alone. Homes across Laurel — many built during the mid-century timber-industry boom when pier-and-beam construction was standard — sit low to the ground with open or poorly vented crawl spaces that have been quietly accumulating moisture, wood rot, and mold growth for decades. ## Why Laurel Properties See Crawl Space Moisture Problems

Jones County averages over 55 inches of rainfall per year, and the clay-heavy soils common throughout Laurel drain slowly. After a significant rain event, that water does not disappear — it migrates laterally through the soil and evaporates upward through the ground beneath your home. An open or vented crawl space acts like a chimney in reverse: warm, humid air from the ground rises into the cooler crawl space, condenses on floor joists and insulation, and begins feeding wood-decay fungi within 24 to 48 hours of saturation.

Older pier-and-beam homes — a construction style that dominated residential building in Laurel through the 1970s — are especially vulnerable. The original fiberglass batt insulation stapled between joists in these homes is not a vapor retarder. It absorbs moisture, loses its R-value, sags, and eventually falls away entirely, leaving floor joists exposed to a continuously humid environment. By the time a homeowner notices soft spots in the floor or a musty smell drifting up through the subfloor, the structural damage is often already underway.

## Our Crawl Space Encapsulation Process in Laurel

Every encapsulation project starts with a thorough crawl space inspection — not a quick flashlight scan, but a documented assessment of moisture readings, joist condition, existing insulation, vapor barrier status, and any signs of mold colonization or pest intrusion. In Laurel's climate, we frequently find standing water or saturated soil even when the homeowner has not reported a plumbing event; ground seepage alone is enough.

From there, the process follows a specific sequence:

1. **Remove and dispose of degraded insulation and any existing failed vapor barriers.** Old 6-mil poly sheeting common in Laurel homes from the 1980s and 1990s tears easily, pools water on top rather than blocking it, and is not code-compliant for a sealed crawl space system.
2. **Address active moisture sources.** If there is standing water, we extract it. If there are foundation vents allowing humid outside air to enter, we seal them as part of the conditioned crawl space design.
3. **Install a reinforced, puncture-resistant encapsulation membrane** — typically a 20-mil polyethylene liner — across the ground and up the foundation walls, sealed at seams and penetrations.
4. **Install a crawl space dehumidifier** sized to the square footage and calibrated to maintain relative humidity below 55%, the threshold at which mold growth stalls.
5. **Final moisture verification.** We take post-installation readings and document them before closing out the job.

The IICRC AMRT (Applied Microbial Remediation Technician) certification our team holds matters here: if we find active mold during the inspection, we remediate it correctly before sealing the space — encapsulating over active mold growth only traps the problem.
