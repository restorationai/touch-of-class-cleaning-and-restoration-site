Long Beach sits squarely in the crosshairs of Gulf Coast storm season, and when a fast-moving squall punches out a window or peels back a section of roofing on a home near the beachfront, the window between the damage and the next rain band can be measured in hours — not days. ## Why Long Beach Properties Face Elevated Board-Up and Tarping Needs

The Harrison County coastline takes a beating that most Mississippi communities don't. Long Beach's housing stock includes a substantial number of older wood-frame homes built before modern hurricane strapping requirements, and those structures are more vulnerable to partial roof failures and blown-in windows during named storms and even the unnamed tropical systems that roll through the Gulf between June and November. Salt air corrodes fasteners and degrades window glazing over time, which means a moderate-speed gust can dislodge frames that would hold in a drier climate. Add the fact that Long Beach sees measurable rainfall on roughly 60 days per year, and an unsecured opening doesn't stay dry for long.

Fire damage compounds the problem differently here. When a structure fire vents through a roof or blows out windows, the Gulf breeze that feels pleasant on the beach becomes an accelerant for secondary damage — pushing rain, humidity, and airborne debris into the exposed interior. Prompt board-up after a fire isn't just about deterring trespassers; it's about stopping the moisture intrusion that turns a fire-damaged home into a mold-colonization site within 24 to 48 hours.

## Our Emergency Board-Up and Tarping Process in Long Beach

When a call comes in from Long Beach, the crew dispatches from our Gulfport base and moves down U.S. 90 or Jeff Davis Avenue depending on where the property sits relative to the coast. On arrival, the first step is a rapid exterior assessment — we identify every breach point, not just the obvious one, because wind events routinely open secondary gaps at soffit lines and gable vents that are easy to miss in a first pass.

For broken window board-up, we use minimum ½-inch plywood cut to overlap the frame by at least two inches on each side, secured with structural screws rather than nails, which hold better in the repeated wind cycles that follow a storm. For roof tarping, we use heavy-duty polyethylene tarps rated for high-wind conditions, anchored with lumber cap strips screwed into the decking — not just draped and weighted, which is a temporary fix that fails in the next gust. Every penetration point is sealed to prevent wind uplift. The crew photographs each step, which matters when an insurance adjuster needs to see the pre-repair condition of the structure.
