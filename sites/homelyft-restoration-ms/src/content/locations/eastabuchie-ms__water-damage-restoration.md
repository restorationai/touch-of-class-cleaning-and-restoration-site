## Why Eastabuchie Properties See Water Damage Issues

Jones County averages more than 55 inches of rain per year — well above the national average — and Eastabuchie's position in the county puts many homes on lots where the clay-heavy soil sheds water slowly rather than absorbing it. That means even a moderate rainstorm can overwhelm crawl-space drainage and push groundwater against pier-and-beam foundations, which are common in this part of rural Mississippi. Older homes throughout the area were often built with wood subflooring directly over unventilated crawl spaces, a construction pattern that accelerates moisture damage once water finds its way inside. Add in the region's aging residential plumbing — galvanized supply lines in homes built before the 1980s are prone to pinhole leaks that go undetected for weeks — and you have a combination of factors that makes water damage a recurring problem rather than a rare event.

## Our Water Damage Restoration Process in Eastabuchie

Every job starts with a thorough moisture assessment using thermal imaging cameras and calibrated moisture meters. From there, the process follows the IICRC S500 standard for water damage restoration:

- **Water extraction**: Truck-mounted and portable extractors remove standing water from flooring, carpet, and hard-to-reach cavities.
- **Structural drying**: High-capacity desiccant and refrigerant dehumidifiers, combined with axial air movers, create the airflow needed to drive moisture out of wall cavities and subfloor assemblies.
- **Monitoring**: Technicians return daily to log moisture readings and adjust equipment placement until all affected materials reach their dry standard.
- **Documentation**: Every reading, photograph, and equipment placement is logged — the same documentation package your insurance adjuster will need to process the claim.

## Local Note

One pattern we see repeatedly in Eastabuchie and the surrounding Jones County area: homes built in the 1960s and 1970s often have original heart-pine subfloor planks beneath vinyl or carpet. Heart pine is extraordinarily dense and holds moisture differently than modern OSB or plywood sheathing — it resists warping longer, which can make a water loss look less severe than it actually is. By the time visible cupping or buckling appears, the wood has typically been holding elevated moisture for days.
