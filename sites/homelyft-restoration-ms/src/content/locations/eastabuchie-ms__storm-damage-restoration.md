## Why Eastabuchie Properties See Severe Storm Damage

The landscape around Eastabuchie is defined by dense stands of longleaf and loblolly pine — beautiful, but a serious hazard when sustained winds exceed 50 mph. Root systems in the sandy, well-drained soils common to this part of Jones County don't anchor trees as deeply as clay-heavy soils farther north, which means mature pines topple more readily onto rooflines, carports, and power lines. The housing stock here skews older, with a meaningful share of homes built before modern wind-load requirements were codified in Mississippi's residential building code. That combination — tall trees, shallow roots, and pre-code framing — produces roof decking failures, broken rafters, and compromised wall sheathing that a surface inspection alone won't fully reveal.

Hurricane remnants are a particular concern. Even after a Gulf storm weakens to a tropical depression, it can still push 6 to 10 inches of rain across Jones County in 12 hours. That volume overwhelms soffit vents, saturates attic insulation, and drives water behind exterior trim in ways that don't show up as a ceiling stain until days later.

## Our Storm Damage Restoration Process in Eastabuchie

Every job begins with a systematic exterior and interior assessment before any debris is moved. We photograph and document the full loss — roof punctures, broken windows, displaced flashing, water intrusion points — because that documentation is the foundation of your insurance claim. From there, the process moves in a defined sequence:

**Emergency stabilization** comes first: tarping compromised roof sections, boarding broken windows, and removing hanging limbs or structural debris that pose an immediate safety risk. We use reinforced poly tarps rated for Mississippi wind conditions, not the lightweight sheeting that fails in the next rain.

**Moisture mapping** follows. Using thermal imaging and calibrated moisture meters, we trace water migration through wall cavities, subfloor assemblies, and insulation bays. In older Eastabuchie homes with board sheathing rather than OSB, water travels differently — it wicks along individual boards and can travel several feet from the original entry point before showing on a surface reading.

**Structural drying** is then set up per the IICRC S500 standard, with commercial desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers and high-velocity air movers positioned based on the moisture map, not guesswork. Daily moisture readings track drying progress until materials reach target values.

**Debris removal and repairs** close out the project — from replacing damaged decking and roofing materials to reframing sections where wind load exceeded the original structure's capacity.
