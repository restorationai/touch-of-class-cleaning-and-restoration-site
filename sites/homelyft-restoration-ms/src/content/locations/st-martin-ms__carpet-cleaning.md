## Why St. Martin Carpets Take a Harder Hit

Jackson County's average annual humidity rarely dips below 70%, and St. Martin's proximity to the Back Bay watershed means that figure climbs even higher during the late spring and fall rainy seasons. High ambient humidity slows the evaporation that carpet fibers need to release soil, which means a standard consumer steam cleaner or a quick-dry service often leaves backing damp for 24–36 hours. That's enough time for mold spores — always present in Gulf Coast air — to begin colonizing the padding underneath.

The housing stock in the St. Martin area adds another layer of complexity. Many homes built in the 1970s and 1980s were fitted with wall-to-wall carpet over concrete slab foundations, a common construction pattern in coastal Mississippi. Slabs in this region wick ground moisture upward, especially after heavy rainfall. When that moisture meets a carpet that wasn't fully dried after cleaning, you end up with a delamination problem in the backing — the glue that bonds the secondary backing to the primary layer breaks down, and the carpet begins to bubble and ripple. Identifying that risk before cleaning, not after, is part of what a certified technician does on the initial walk-through.

## Our Carpet Cleaning Process in St. Martin

The process starts with a moisture and pH assessment. Before any water or cleaning solution touches the carpet, technicians measure ambient humidity and check the slab or subfloor for elevated moisture readings using calibrated meters. This step is especially important in St. Martin because cleaning into a wet subfloor environment can make conditions worse, not better.

Once conditions are confirmed safe to proceed, we pre-treat high-traffic lanes and stain zones with targeted spotting agents matched to the soil type — oil-based stains from garage tracking, tannin stains from Mississippi red clay, or protein-based stains from pet accidents each respond to different chemistry. We then use truck-mounted hot-water extraction, which pulls water back out of the pile at a rate that portable units can't match. Extraction pressure and water temperature are dialed to the carpet's fiber type — synthetic olefin common in 1980s construction handles heat differently than modern nylon or wool blends.

After extraction, high-velocity air movers are positioned to bring drying time down to 6–12 hours rather than the 24+ hours that low-powered equipment leaves behind. In St. Martin's climate, cutting drying time in half is not a convenience — it's a mold-prevention measure.
