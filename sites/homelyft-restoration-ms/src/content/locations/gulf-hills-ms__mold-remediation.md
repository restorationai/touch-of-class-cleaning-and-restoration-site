## Why Gulf Hills Properties See Mold Problems

Gulf Hills developed largely as a residential enclave along the bluffs and bayou-adjacent lots east of Biloxi, and that geography creates conditions that accelerate mold growth in ways that inland Mississippi communities don't face at the same rate. Homes here often sit on crawl space foundations — common in the area's mid-century and post-war building stock — where ground moisture wicks upward year-round. When outdoor humidity regularly exceeds 80 percent from late spring through early fall, vapor barriers degrade faster, floor joists stay damp, and mold colonizes the underside of subfloors before a homeowner notices anything from inside the house.

Post-storm moisture is the other major driver. Jackson County properties that took on water during past hurricane seasons sometimes had remediation work done quickly but incompletely — drywall cavities dried on the surface while moisture persisted deeper in the assembly. Years later, that latent moisture source can feed a mold colony that finally becomes visible or begins affecting indoor air quality.

Older homes in Gulf Hills with original wood-framed walls and minimal insulation also tend to experience more condensation on interior surfaces during the months when air conditioning runs constantly against high outdoor humidity. That condensation, if it occurs repeatedly near a window frame or exterior wall penetration, is enough to sustain mold growth without any single dramatic water event.

## Our Mold Remediation Process in Gulf Hills

Every mold remediation job starts with a thorough assessment — not a visual scan, but a moisture mapping exercise using thermal imaging and pin-type moisture meters to find where water is actually hiding. In Gulf Hills homes with crawl spaces, that means getting under the structure to check joists, rim boards, and vapor barriers before any containment is set up inside the living space.

Once the moisture source and the full extent of affected material are identified, the work follows a structured sequence:

1. **Containment** — Negative air pressure chambers with HEPA-filtered air scrubbers are established to prevent spores from migrating to unaffected areas of the home during removal work.
2. **Removal of colonized material** — Porous materials that cannot be effectively cleaned (drywall, insulation, compromised wood framing) are bagged and removed per EPA guidelines. Non-porous surfaces are HEPA-vacuumed and treated with EPA-registered antimicrobial agents.
3. **Structural drying** — Because mold remediation without addressing the underlying moisture is a temporary fix, the team uses industrial desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers and high-velocity air movers to bring structural moisture content to acceptable levels before any rebuilding begins. This step is calibrated to coastal Mississippi's ambient conditions, not a generic drying protocol.
4. **Clearance verification** — Before containment comes down, moisture readings are retaken across the affected area to confirm the structure is within acceptable parameters.

The team holds IICRC AMRT (Applied Microbial Remediation Technician) certification, which means the process follows a defined industry standard rather than improvised judgment calls.

## Local Note

One pattern that comes up repeatedly in Gulf Hills homes built in the 1960s and 1970s is the use of original tongue-and-groove wood subfloors beneath later vinyl or tile finishes. When moisture gets under the finish layer, the wood subfloor absorbs it and holds it far longer than modern OSB or plywood would — and the finish layer acts as a vapor barrier that traps the moisture in place. Thermal imaging often catches this when a visual inspection would miss it entirely.
