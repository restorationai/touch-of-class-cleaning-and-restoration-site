## Why Claremont Properties See Leak Detection Issues

Claremont's identity is built around its tree-lined streets and the college corridor, but those mature trees, many of them decades old, send roots deep into the clay-heavy soil common to the San Gabriel Valley foothills. Clay expands when it absorbs moisture and contracts sharply during the dry season, and that repeated movement puts lateral stress on buried supply lines and sewer laterals alike. A pipe joint that holds fine for years can begin weeping after one particularly wet winter followed by a long dry stretch.

The city's housing stock adds another layer of complexity. Neighborhoods closer to the Claremont Colleges include homes built in the 1920s through the 1950s, many with original galvanized steel supply lines that have been corroding from the inside out for decades. Galvanized pipe doesn't fail all at once, it pinhole-leaks, and those pinholes can sit inside a wall cavity or beneath a slab for months before the water bill or a soft spot in the drywall gives them away.

Slab construction is common throughout Claremont, which means many leaks occur in copper lines embedded in concrete. A slab leak often announces itself as a warm patch on a tile floor, the sound of running water when every fixture is off, or a water meter that keeps spinning at 2 a.m. These signs deserve immediate attention, water migrating under a slab can undermine the foundation and saturate subfloor materials faster than the surface suggests.

## Our Leak Detection Process in Claremont

The goal of leak detection is to find the exact location of a leak before any demolition happens. Tearing into walls or breaking up concrete based on a guess wastes time, money, and material. The process starts at the meter, isolating sections of the system to confirm whether the leak is on the supply side, inside the structure, or in the irrigation circuit that many Claremont properties rely on to maintain landscaping through the dry season.

From there, electronic listening equipment picks up the acoustic signature of water escaping under pressure. Pressurized lines produce a distinct frequency as water forces through a breach, and trained technicians can triangulate that signal through concrete, stucco, and soil. For lines that don't respond clearly to acoustic methods, particularly older galvanized sections that dampen sound, thermal imaging can reveal temperature differentials caused by water migrating through a structure.

Once the leak is pinpointed, the findings are documented with photos and a written location report before any repair work begins. That documentation matters if you're filing a homeowner's insurance claim or need to demonstrate the scope of damage to an HOA board.
