Riverside sits in one of Southern California's most challenging air basins — the South Coast Air Quality Management District consistently ranks the Inland Empire among the region's highest for particulate matter and ozone days. That outdoor pollution doesn't stay outside. It infiltrates through gaps around windows, HVAC returns, and attic bypasses, and once it's inside a Riverside home, it mixes with whatever the house itself is generating: dust mite allergens in older carpet, volatile organic compounds from recent renovations, and the dry, recirculated air that results from running central AC through a long Inland summer. RT Olson Plumbing, Heating and Air Conditioning helps homeowners across Riverside diagnose what's actually in their air and install the equipment to clean it up.

## Why Riverside Homes See Persistent Air Quality Problems

Riverside's climate does most of the damage before you even open a window. Summer temperatures routinely push past 100°F, which means residents keep homes sealed and HVAC systems running for months at a stretch. That extended runtime circulates whatever is already in the duct system — accumulated dust, mold spores from any moisture event, and fine particulates that a standard 1-inch fiberglass filter was never designed to capture.

The city's housing stock adds another layer. Riverside has a significant number of homes built between the 1950s and 1980s, many of which were fitted with original duct systems that were never sealed or insulated to modern standards. Leaky ducts in unconditioned attic spaces pull in attic air — insulation fibers, rodent dander, and concentrated heat — and deliver it directly to living spaces. Homes in the older residential corridors near downtown Riverside and the University Avenue corridor frequently have this pattern, and it's one reason whole-house filtration upgrades make such a measurable difference in those neighborhoods.

Santa Ana wind events compound the problem seasonally. When strong offshore winds push through the passes, outdoor PM2.5 spikes sharply, and anything that can infiltrate a home does. Whole-home air purifiers and upgraded MERV-rated filtration become especially valuable in the weeks surrounding those events.

## Our Indoor Air Quality Services Process in Riverside

Every engagement starts with an honest assessment rather than a product pitch. A technician walks the home, inspects the existing HVAC filtration, checks for obvious duct leakage, and discusses what the occupants are noticing — whether that's allergy symptoms that worsen in certain rooms, a musty smell when the system first kicks on, or visible dust settling quickly after cleaning.

From there, the path forward depends on what the home actually needs:

- **HVAC filtration upgrades** — swapping a builder-grade filter rack for a properly sealed, high-MERV media cabinet that captures fine particles without starving the system of airflow
- **UV air purifier installation for HVAC** — germicidal UV-C lamps installed in the air handler to neutralize biological contaminants passing through the coil
- **Whole-house air purifier integration** — electronic air cleaners or polarized-media units that work in tandem with the existing system
- **Whole-home humidifier installation** — Riverside winters are dry enough that static electricity and cracked woodwork are common complaints; a bypass or fan-powered humidifier tied to the furnace corrects that without adding a fleet of portable units
- **Indoor air quality testing** — particulate, VOC, and CO2 readings that give a baseline before any equipment goes in and a verification point afterward

All equipment is sized to the actual airflow of the existing system. Oversizing a media filter in a system with an undersized blower motor creates static pressure problems that shorten equipment life — a mistake that shows up often when homeowners buy filtration upgrades at a big-box store and install them without checking system specs.

## Local Note

Riverside's older residential areas — particularly blocks developed in the post-war decades — were built with wall and ceiling cavities that were never insulated or air-sealed. When those homes had central HVAC added later, installers often ran supply and return ducts through those open cavities rather than through sealed duct board.
