## Why Chino Hills Properties See More Hidden Leaks Than You'd Expect

The geology here works against plumbing longevity in a specific way. The clay-heavy soils that give Chino Hills its characteristic hillside topography expand when the winter rains arrive and contract again through the long dry summers. That repeated movement puts lateral stress on underground supply lines and the sleeve fittings where pipes pass through slab foundations. Homes built in the late 1980s and through the 1990s, which make up a large share of the city's housing stock, were typically plumbed with copper that is now old enough to show pinhole corrosion, especially where the pipe runs through concrete or contacts soil directly. Polybutylene pipe, installed in some homes of that same era before it was phased out, is another known failure point. A leak in any of these systems can run silently for months: the water migrates along the path of least resistance under the slab, surfaces somewhere unexpected, and by the time a homeowner sees it, the damage is well underway.

## Our Leak Detection Process in Chino Hills

Finding a hidden leak accurately means not guessing, and not opening walls or trenching a yard until we know exactly where the break is. The process starts with a pressure test to confirm a leak exists and to isolate which line is losing water. From there, electronic listening equipment picks up the acoustic signature of water escaping under pressure; the sound travels through the slab or soil and registers differently depending on depth and pipe material, which is why the same tool needs to be calibrated to the conditions on a given property. Thermal imaging adds a second layer of confirmation on slab leaks, where a temperature differential at the floor surface can pinpoint the leak location to within inches before any concrete is touched. For irrigation and underground service lines, ground microphones and correlation equipment let us trace the line without excavation. We document the findings with photos and a written report, useful both for the repair decision and for any insurance or HOA claim that follows.

A slab leak or a long-running hidden leak often qualifies as a covered loss under a standard homeowner's policy, but insurers want documentation before they authorize repair or remediation.
