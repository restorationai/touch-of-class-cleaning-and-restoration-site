Rialto sits in the heart of the Inland Empire, where summer temperatures regularly push past 100°F and the soil, a mix of alluvial sand and clay deposited by the Santa Ana River over centuries, expands and contracts with every wet and dry season. That ground movement puts constant stress on water supply lines and slab foundations, and it's one of the main reasons homeowners here often notice a mysteriously climbing water bill long before they ever spot a wet spot on the floor. When a hidden leak is somewhere beneath a concrete slab or behind a stucco wall, finding it fast matters, both for your water bill and for the structural integrity of your home.

## Why Rialto Properties See Leak Detection Issues

The Inland Empire's climate is hard on plumbing in ways that aren't always obvious. Rialto experiences wide temperature swings between summer highs and winter nights that can dip near freezing, enough to stress pipe joints and accelerate micro-cracking in older copper and galvanized lines. Much of Rialto's residential housing stock was built during the postwar and 1960s–1980s tract-home booms, when copper supply lines were standard. Copper is durable, but after 40 or 50 years in reactive soil, pinhole leaks are a predictable outcome rather than a surprise.

Slab construction is nearly universal in this part of San Bernardino County. That means when a supply line or drain line develops a leak, the water has nowhere to go but laterally through the slab or upward through flooring, often staying invisible until the damage is already significant. A warm patch on a tile floor, a faint sulfur or musty odor near a baseboard, or a water meter that keeps spinning after every fixture is shut off are the early signals worth taking seriously.

## Our Leak Detection Process in Rialto

Because most Rialto homes are slab-on-grade, the detection process has to be non-invasive first. Cutting into a slab without confirming the leak location is expensive and disruptive, a mistake that costs more to fix than the leak itself. The approach here starts with a pressure test on the supply side to confirm a leak is present and isolate which line is losing pressure. From there, electronic amplification equipment picks up the acoustic signature of water escaping under pressure, a faint hissing or rushing sound that travels through concrete and is inaudible to the human ear without the right tools.

Thermal imaging adds a second layer of confirmation. A pressurized leak changes the temperature profile of the slab above it, and an infrared camera makes that temperature gradient visible. Combining acoustic detection with thermal imaging narrows the location to within inches before any concrete is touched. That precision matters especially in Rialto, where slab thickness and aggregate composition can vary between neighborhoods built in different decades, older slabs sometimes transmit sound differently than poured concrete from the 1990s onward.

## Equipment & Methods We Use for Leak Detection

The core toolkit for slab and underground leak detection includes electronic ground microphones, acoustic correlators, and thermal imaging cameras. For suspected underground line leaks, irrigation laterals, main service lines running from the meter to the house, tracer gas detection is an option when acoustic methods produce ambiguous results. Tracer gas (a safe, inert hydrogen-nitrogen mixture) is introduced into the line and detected at the surface with a sensitive probe; it escapes through the smallest breach and rises through soil, pinpointing the leak without excavation.

For suspected drain line leaks, a sewer camera inspection runs alongside the acoustic work, drain lines in Rialto's older housing stock are often cast iron or Orangeburg, both of which degrade in ways that acoustic detection alone won't fully characterize.
