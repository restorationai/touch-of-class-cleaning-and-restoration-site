## Why Riverside Homes Go Through Water Heaters Faster Than You'd Expect

Riverside's water supply consistently tests high in total dissolved solids, which means scale accumulates on heating elements and tank liners faster than in softer-water markets. A standard tank that might last twelve years in coastal Southern California can show serious degradation in eight or nine years here. That sediment layer doesn't just reduce efficiency — it forces the burner to run longer to reach the set temperature, which drives up your Southern California Gas bill and stresses the tank wall.

The city's housing stock adds another layer of complexity. Riverside has a substantial inventory of mid-century homes — many built in the 1950s and 1960s — where original gas lines were sized for appliances of that era. When upgrading from a 40-gallon tank to a 50-gallon unit, or switching to a tankless system, the existing gas supply line sometimes needs upsizing to meet the higher BTU demand. That's a detail that matters during the estimate, not after the new unit is already on the wall.

## Tank vs. Tankless: What Makes Sense in Riverside

The tankless vs. tank decision comes up on nearly every replacement call, and in Riverside the answer depends on a few factors that are genuinely local. Tankless water heaters deliver hot water on demand without storing a reservoir, which makes them attractive for households trying to cut standby energy loss. However, tankless units are more sensitive to incoming water hardness — without a water softener or descaling system in place, the heat exchanger in a tankless unit can scale up faster than a traditional tank's element, requiring annual flushing to maintain warranty coverage.

For larger households — three or more bathrooms, a washer, and simultaneous demand — a 50-gallon tank or a high-output tankless unit both work well in Riverside's climate. The region's mild winters mean the incoming cold-water temperature doesn't drop as dramatically as it does in mountain communities, so a single tankless unit can typically handle whole-home demand without needing a second unit in parallel. That said, homes with a detached ADU or a converted garage unit may benefit from a dedicated point-of-use heater rather than stretching one tankless system across a long pipe run.

## Our Installation and Replacement Process in Riverside

Every installation starts with a site assessment — checking the existing gas or electrical supply, the flue or venting path, the seismic strapping requirements (California code requires water heaters to be strapped to resist earthquake movement), and the available space. We pull the appropriate permit through the City of Riverside Building and Safety Division when the scope of work requires one, so the installation is inspected and on record.

Once the old unit is drained and disconnected, we haul it away. The new unit goes in with proper seismic strapping, updated flex connectors if the originals are corroded, and a tested pressure-relief valve discharge line that terminates correctly — not just draped over the side of the pan. For tankless installations, we flush the system before sign-off and walk through the maintenance schedule with you, including how often to descale given Riverside's water hardness.

One thing that catches homeowners off guard in Riverside: the City's building code requires a drip leg (a sediment trap) on the gas supply line to a water heater, and inspectors here enforce it consistently. On older installations — particularly in homes built before the 1980s — that drip leg is often missing or was never installed.
