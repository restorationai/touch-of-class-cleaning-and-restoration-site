## Why Ontario Properties See Hidden Leak Problems

The soil profile across much of Ontario is the core issue. Expansive clay — common throughout the San Bernardino Valley floor — moves measurably between wet and dry seasons. That movement stresses pipe joints and slab penetrations year after year, and copper or galvanized lines installed in the tract homes built through the 1960s, 70s, and 80s were not designed with that kind of cumulative flex in mind. Pinhole leaks in copper are especially common in this region because the water chemistry, combined with decades of thermal cycling, gradually thins pipe walls from the inside out.

Ontario also sits within the service territory of the Inland Empire Utilities Agency and the City of Ontario's own water system, depending on the neighborhood. Either way, tiered water pricing means even a modest hidden leak — say, a slow drip at a slab penetration — can push a household into a higher rate tier and add meaningfully to the bill before the wet spot ever appears on a floor or wall.

## Our Leak Detection Process in Ontario

Finding a leak without tearing up a slab or opening walls requires layering methods. The process typically starts with a meter test: we isolate the property at the main shut-off and watch the meter dial for movement. If the meter confirms active loss, we pressurize the line and use electronic amplification equipment — ground microphones and acoustic correlators — to listen for the specific frequency signature of water escaping under pressure. On slab foundations, which dominate Ontario's residential stock, we also use thermal imaging to identify temperature differentials in the concrete that indicate moisture migration below.

For irrigation and pool-adjacent lines, where leaks often travel laterally before surfacing, tracer gas detection gives us a non-destructive way to pinpoint the exit point at grade. The combination of acoustic and thermal tools means we can typically mark the leak location within inches before any excavation or saw-cutting begins — limiting the repair footprint and the restoration work that follows.

## Equipment & Methods We Use for Leak Detection

The equipment matters because Ontario's slab construction leaves little margin for guesswork. Cutting or jackhammering the wrong spot adds cost and extends the project significantly. The tools we rely on:

- **Acoustic ground microphones** — amplify the sound of pressurized water escaping through a pipe wall or joint, effective through concrete and compacted soil
- **Electronic correlators** — compare signal timing between two sensor points to calculate leak location mathematically
- **Infrared thermal cameras** — map surface temperature anomalies on slabs and walls that indicate subsurface moisture
- **Tracer gas (nitrogen/hydrogen mix)** — non-toxic, lighter than air, rises through soil to the surface directly above the leak point
- **Video pipe inspection** — for accessible drain lines where visual confirmation of a crack or root intrusion is faster than acoustic methods

No single method works in every situation, which is why we arrive equipped to switch approaches based on what the property's construction and the meter test tell us.

## Local Note

One pattern that comes up repeatedly in Ontario's older residential tracts — particularly homes built on post-tension slabs common from the late 1970s onward — is that the leak location and the wet surface spot are often not directly above each other. Water migrates along the path of least resistance through the sand bed beneath the slab, sometimes traveling several feet before wicking up through a grout line or expansion joint. Homeowners who spot a damp area near a hallway bathroom may actually have a leak originating under the kitchen. Experienced technicians account for this migration when interpreting acoustic and thermal data, and they mark a search zone rather than a single point before any cutting begins.
