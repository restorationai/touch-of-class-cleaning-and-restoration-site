## Why Ontario Properties See Leak Detection Issues

Ontario sits on the western edge of the San Bernardino Valley, where expansive clay soils shift with seasonal moisture changes. That movement puts stress on buried copper and galvanized supply lines, particularly in homes built during the post-war residential boom of the 1950s through the 1970s. Galvanized pipe from that era corrodes from the inside out, and the first sign of trouble is often a pinhole leak hidden inside a wall cavity or beneath a slab — not a visible drip at a fixture.

The region also has hard water. High mineral content accelerates scale buildup inside copper lines, thinning pipe walls over time and creating weak spots that fail under normal operating pressure. If your water meter is spinning when every fixture in the house is off, or if one section of flooring feels noticeably warmer than the rest, those are classic signs of a slab leak that warrants electronic detection rather than exploratory demolition.

Ontario's rapid commercial and residential development over the past two decades has also produced a mix of irrigation systems, shared utility easements, and HOA-governed common areas where underground leaks can cross property lines — complicating both the diagnosis and the repair authorization process.

## Our Leak Detection Process in Ontario

Electronic leak detection removes the guesswork that used to mean tearing up tile or opening walls across a wide search area. The process begins with a pressure test on the supply system to confirm a leak exists and to isolate which branch is affected. From there, acoustic listening equipment amplifies the sound signature of water escaping under pressure — a frequency that travels through concrete, soil, and pipe material differently depending on depth and ground composition.

In Ontario's clay-heavy soils, acoustic readings require calibration because clay dampens sound transmission more than sandy or loamy ground. Thermal imaging adds a second data layer: a slab leak warms the concrete above it, and an infrared camera maps that heat gradient precisely, narrowing the target area before any concrete is touched. Combining acoustic and thermal methods means the crew marks a specific location — not a three-foot search zone — before any saw or jackhammer is involved.

For underground irrigation or service line leaks in yards or common areas, ground microphones and tracer gas detection allow us to pinpoint breaks without trenching the full line length.

## Local Note

Ontario has a significant stock of tract homes built in the 1960s and early 1970s that used copper supply lines with soldered joints. In the Inland Empire's hard-water environment, those joints are a known failure point — the solder corrodes at a different rate than the surrounding copper, and leaks tend to develop at the joint itself rather than mid-pipe. When we're detecting leaks in homes from that era, we pay particular attention to joint locations near manifolds and under slab transitions, because a single failed joint often signals that neighboring joints are under similar stress.
