## Why Chino Properties See Hidden Leak Problems

Chino sits on alluvial soils deposited by the Santa Ana River watershed — ground that behaves differently depending on the season and how recently it has rained. When those soils shift, they place lateral stress on water mains, slab-penetrating supply lines, and older cast-iron or galvanized pipes. Homes built during Chino's rapid residential expansion of the 1970s through the 1990s often have original copper or galvanized steel supply lines that are now at or past their expected service life. Pinhole leaks in copper are especially common in this region because Southern California municipal water tends to run on the harder side, and mineral deposits gradually thin pipe walls from the inside out.

Irrigation systems add another layer of risk. Chino's warm, dry climate means most properties run sprinkler systems for much of the year, and underground lateral lines crack under root pressure or soil movement without producing any visible surface sign — just a slow, invisible draw on the meter.

## Our Leak Detection Process in Chino

The first step is always the meter. We isolate the home from all fixtures and irrigation zones and observe the meter dial. If it moves, water is leaving the system somewhere it shouldn't. That tells us whether we are chasing a pressurized supply leak or a drain and waste issue — two very different searches.

For supply leaks, we use electronic listening equipment and thermal imaging to narrow the location before any concrete is cut or drywall is opened. Acoustic sensors pick up the frequency signature of pressurized water escaping a pipe — a sound that travels through slab and soil differently depending on depth and pipe material. Thermal cameras reveal temperature differentials on slab surfaces and interior walls that indicate moisture migration. Together, these tools let us mark a precise target area, which keeps demolition to a minimum.

For pressurized lines under the slab, we may also introduce tracer gas — a safe, inert helium-nitrogen mixture — into the pipe and use a surface probe to detect where it escapes. This method is particularly effective on Chino slabs where the leak point is small and the acoustic signal is weak.

## Equipment & Methods We Use for Leak Detection

Not every leak detection job calls for the same tool. We match the method to the situation:

- **Acoustic listening devices** — effective for pressurized supply leaks in quiet environments, particularly useful on single-story slabs common in Chino's tract-home neighborhoods.
- **Thermal imaging cameras** — surface-level temperature mapping that reveals moisture migration behind stucco, drywall, or tile without contact.
- **Tracer gas detection** — used when acoustic signals are ambiguous or when the suspected leak is deep under a thick slab pour.
- **Video pipe inspection** — for drain and waste lines where we need to see interior pipe condition and confirm the location of a crack or joint failure.

Using the right combination means we locate the leak accurately the first time and avoid unnecessary exploratory cuts.

Chino's older residential tracts — particularly those developed in the late 1970s and early 1980s — were frequently built on post-tension slab foundations, a construction method that uses tensioned steel cables embedded in the concrete. Cutting into a post-tension slab without first identifying cable locations can sever a cable and compromise the structural integrity of the entire slab. Before any saw cut on a Chino slab repair, we use a cable locator to map the tension grid.
