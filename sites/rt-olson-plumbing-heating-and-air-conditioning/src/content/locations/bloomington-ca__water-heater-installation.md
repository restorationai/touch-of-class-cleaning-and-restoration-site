## Why Bloomington Homes Are Hard on Water Heaters

The Inland Empire's water is notoriously hard. That dissolved calcium and magnesium doesn't just leave spots on your shower door, it settles as sediment at the bottom of a tank water heater, forming a thick layer that forces the burner to work harder to heat water through it. You'll often hear the result before you see it: a low rumbling or popping sound coming from the utility closet, especially during the first draw of hot water in the morning. Left alone, that sediment layer shortens the tank's lifespan significantly and drives up your gas or electric bill in the meantime.

Bloomington's climate adds another layer of stress. The San Bernardino Valley's summer heat means outdoor-adjacent utility rooms, common in the area's single-story ranch-style homes, can reach temperatures that push tank units beyond their rated operating range. Then winter nights drop sharply, and that thermal cycling accelerates wear on the tank lining, the anode rod, and the pressure relief valve. Most tank water heaters in this region don't reach the manufacturer's rated lifespan.

## Tank vs. Tankless: What Makes Sense in Bloomington

For many Bloomington households, a standard 40- or 50-gallon natural gas tank remains the most practical and cost-effective replacement. Parts are widely available, installation is straightforward in homes already plumbed for it, and recovery time is predictable. If your current unit is leaking, making noise, or producing lukewarm water and it's more than ten years old, a direct tank-for-tank replacement is often the fastest path back to reliable hot water.

Tankless water heaters are a strong option when the math supports it, particularly in larger households where back-to-back showers are draining a tank, or in situations where the utility room space is limited. That said, tankless installation in Bloomington-area homes sometimes requires a gas line upgrade to handle the higher BTU demand of an on-demand unit, and the hard local water means a water softener or at minimum a scale filter is worth serious consideration to protect the heat exchanger. We'll walk through both options with you before any work begins so you're choosing based on your actual usage and home setup, not a sales pitch.

## Local Note

Homes in the older sections of Bloomington, particularly those built in the 1960s and 1970s, were often constructed with water heaters installed in interior closets with minimal clearance and original galvanized steel supply lines still in service. Galvanized pipe corrodes from the inside out, and by the time it's visibly failing at a fitting, the interior of the line may already be significantly restricted. When we're replacing a water heater in one of these older installations, we routinely check the condition of the supply lines and recommend replacing galvanized sections with copper or flexible stainless connectors. It's a small additional cost that prevents a much larger problem down the road.
