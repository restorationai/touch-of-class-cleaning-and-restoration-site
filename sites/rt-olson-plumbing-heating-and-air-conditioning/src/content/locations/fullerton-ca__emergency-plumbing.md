## Why Fullerton Properties Experience Plumbing Emergencies

Fullerton's housing stock spans nearly a century of construction, and that range matters when something goes wrong. Homes built in the postwar boom of the late 1940s and 1950s — many of them still standing in the older residential corridors near downtown — were plumbed with galvanized steel pipe that has long since reached the end of its service life. Galvanized pipe corrodes from the inside out, narrowing over decades until pressure spikes cause pinhole leaks or outright failures. You might not see the problem until water is already soaking into a wall cavity.

Fullerton also sits in a region where expansive clay soil shifts with the wet-dry cycle of Southern California winters and dry summers. That ground movement puts lateral stress on buried supply lines and sewer laterals, gradually working joints loose. A slow slab leak — the kind you notice first as a warm patch underfoot or an unexplained spike on your Southern California Gas or Golden State Water bill — is often the result. Left alone, slab leaks migrate moisture under the foundation and into the slab itself, compounding the repair cost significantly.

Older cast-iron drain stacks, common in Fullerton homes from the 1960s and earlier, develop hairline cracks as the iron oxidizes. Tree roots — particularly from the mature ficus and liquid amber trees common in established Fullerton neighborhoods — exploit those cracks aggressively, turning a slow drain into a full sewer backup with little warning.
