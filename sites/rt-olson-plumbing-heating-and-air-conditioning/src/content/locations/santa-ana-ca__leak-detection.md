## Why Santa Ana Properties See More Hidden Leaks

Santa Ana is one of Orange County's most densely built cities, and much of its residential and commercial stock dates to the mid-20th century or earlier. Galvanized steel supply lines from that era corrode from the inside out — they can hold pressure for years while the interior diameter quietly narrows, then fail suddenly when a pressure surge hits. The expansive clay soils common across the Santa Ana basin shift seasonally with Southern California's wet-dry cycles, and that movement stresses underground copper and PVC lines at joints and elbows. Slab-on-grade construction — the dominant foundation type throughout the city — means leaks under the floor have nowhere to drain visibly; water migrates laterally until it surfaces at a baseboard or a doorframe, sometimes weeks after the break began.

The city's water is delivered through the Mesa Water District and the Santa Ana system, both of which maintain aging distribution mains in older corridors. High static pressure from the municipal supply is a known contributor to pinhole leaks in copper repipes installed in the 1980s and 1990s — a common era of renovation for Santa Ana homes that were originally built with galvanized plumbing.

## Our Leak Detection Process in Santa Ana

Finding a hidden leak without tearing out walls or jackhammering a slab requires layering multiple detection methods. The process typically moves through these stages:

**Pressure testing and isolation.** We isolate the supply system zone by zone, holding pressure on each segment to identify where the loss is occurring. This narrows the search area before any equipment touches the structure.

**Electronic amplification listening.** Ground microphones and acoustic listening rods pick up the distinctive frequency signature of water escaping under pressure — a sound that travels through concrete and soil but is inaudible to the human ear without amplification. This method is especially effective on the slab foundations common throughout Santa Ana's residential neighborhoods.

**Thermal imaging.** An infrared camera reads temperature differentials across floor surfaces and wall cavities. Evaporating water cools the surrounding material, producing a cold signature that shows up clearly on camera even when the surface looks and feels dry.

**Tracer gas detection.** For stubborn cases — particularly underground irrigation lines or service laterals buried beneath hardscape — we introduce a safe hydrogen-nitrogen tracer gas into the line. The gas rises through soil and pavement and is detected at the surface with a sensitive probe, pinpointing the leak location to within inches.

Once the leak is located, we mark the exact spot so repair work — whether a slab penetration, a wall opening, or a trench — is as small as possible.

## Local Note

Santa Ana's older neighborhoods — particularly the blocks of bungalows and Spanish Colonial homes built between the 1920s and 1950s — were frequently replumbed during the 1970s and 1980s using polybutylene pipe, a gray flexible plastic that was later found to degrade from chlorine contact over time. If your home was built before 1960 and hasn't had a full plumbing inspection since a remodel, there's a reasonable chance polybutylene is still present in sections of the supply system. It doesn't always fail dramatically — it tends to weep at fittings first, which is exactly the kind of slow, hidden loss that drives a water bill up by 20 to 30 dollars a month before anyone notices wet drywall.
