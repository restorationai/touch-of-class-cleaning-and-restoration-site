## Why Rialto Homes See More Disposal Problems Than You'd Expect

The San Bernardino Valley's water supply carries some of the highest mineral content in Southern California. Over months and years, calcium and magnesium deposits accumulate inside disposal housings, on impeller blades, and around the grinding ring. The result is a unit that struggles to spin under load, that low, labored hum you hear when you flip the switch is often the motor fighting through scale buildup, not a jammed bone or a stuck impeller.

Rialto's housing stock adds another layer. A significant share of homes in the city were built during the post-war and 1960s–1980s suburban expansion, when kitchens were plumbed with narrower drain lines and lower-capacity disposals rated for light residential use. Those units were never designed for today's household volume, and when one fails, the replacement often needs to step up to a higher horsepower model to match how the kitchen actually gets used. That's a different job than a straight swap, it may involve upgrading the drain connection, checking the P-trap configuration, and confirming the electrical outlet under the sink is properly grounded.

## What the Repair and Installation Process Looks Like

When a technician arrives, the first step is diagnosis, not assumption. A disposal that hums but won't spin is almost always a tripped overload or a seized turntable, and the fix is fast. A disposal that leaks from the bottom collar has a failed internal seal and typically needs replacement rather than repair. A unit that drains slowly but grinds normally usually points to a partial blockage in the drain line downstream, not the disposal itself.

For installations, whether replacing a failed unit or upgrading an existing one, the process runs in a consistent order: shut off power at the breaker, disconnect the drain and dishwasher inlet if present, unmount the old unit, inspect the sink flange and mounting ring for corrosion (common in older Rialto kitchens where the original cast-iron flanges have been sitting in hard water for decades), replace the flange if needed, mount and wire the new unit, reconnect drains, and run a full test cycle before leaving. No shortcuts on the electrical check, a disposal that trips the breaker every few weeks usually has a wiring issue, not a motor issue.

## Local Note: Hard Water and Disposal Longevity in the Inland Empire

One thing that comes up repeatedly in Rialto kitchens: homeowners replace a disposal, and within a few years the new unit starts showing the same symptoms as the old one, slow grinding, intermittent humming, reduced throughput. The culprit is almost never the disposal brand. It's the water. Running cold water through the disposal for 15–20 seconds after grinding is finished helps flush the drain line, but it doesn't address mineral accumulation inside the unit itself. A periodic flush with a disposal-safe descaler, the kind designed for hard-water regions, can meaningfully extend the service life of a new unit in this part of San Bernardino County. It's a small habit that makes a real difference when your incoming water is as mineral-heavy as Rialto's typically is.
