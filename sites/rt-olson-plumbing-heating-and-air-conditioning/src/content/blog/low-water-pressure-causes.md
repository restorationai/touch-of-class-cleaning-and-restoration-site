Low water pressure in your house usually comes down to one of a handful of causes: a partially closed shutoff valve, a failing pressure regulator, mineral buildup inside your pipes, or a leak somewhere in the system. Most of these are diagnosable without special tools, and some are a quick fix. A few point to something bigger — like corroded galvanized pipes or a hidden leak that's been quietly losing water for months. Here's how to work through the list, starting with the easiest checks.

## Start With the Simple Stuff Before You Panic

Before you call anyone, run through these in order.

**Check whether it's one fixture or the whole house.** Turn on a faucet in the kitchen, then one in a bathroom on a different part of the house. If pressure is low everywhere, the problem is upstream — at the meter, the main shutoff, or the pressure regulator. If it's only one faucet or one shower, the issue is local: a clogged aerator, a failing cartridge, or a shutoff valve under that sink that got bumped.

**Look at your main shutoff valve.** It's usually on the side of the house facing the street, in a utility closet, or near the water heater. If it's a gate valve (round wheel handle), it should be turned all the way counterclockwise. If it's a ball valve (lever handle), the lever should be parallel to the pipe. A valve that's even partially closed can cut your pressure noticeably.

**Check the meter shutoff too.** In Corona and most of Riverside County, the water meter is in a small concrete box near the curb. There's a shutoff on the street side (controlled by the water utility) and one on your side. If your side was recently closed — after a repair, or by accident — and not opened all the way back, that's your answer.

**Clean the aerators.** Unscrew the tip of a low-pressure faucet, and you'll often find a small screen caked with white mineral deposits. Southern California water is hard — calcium and magnesium are a fact of life here — and aerators collect that buildup over time. Soak the aerator in white vinegar for 20–30 minutes and reinstall it. If pressure comes back, you've solved it for free.

## The Pressure Regulator: The Culprit Nobody Thinks About

Most homes in Corona built in the last few decades have a pressure reducing valve (PRV) installed where the main supply enters the house. Its job is to step down the street pressure — which can run well above what household plumbing is designed for — to a safe working range, typically somewhere between 50 and 80 PSI.

PRVs don't last forever. When one starts to fail, it can do one of two things: let pressure run too high (which stresses your pipes and fixtures) or let it drop too low (which is what you're feeling). A failing PRV often goes gradually, so you might not notice until the pressure is noticeably weak.

You can check your home's water pressure with an inexpensive gauge that threads onto an outdoor hose bib. If you're reading below 40 PSI at the hose bib with no other fixtures running, and your shutoff valves are fully open, the PRV is a likely suspect. Replacing one is a straightforward job for a plumber and usually resolves the problem the same day.

## Mineral Buildup and Old Galvanized Pipes

If your home was built before the mid-1980s and still has its original plumbing, there's a good chance it has galvanized steel supply pipes. Galvanized pipe corrodes from the inside out. Over decades, rust and mineral scale build up on the interior walls, gradually narrowing the passage water flows through. The result is steadily worsening pressure — often so gradual that homeowners don't notice until it's quite bad.

You can sometimes spot the signs without opening a wall: reddish or brownish tint to the water when you first run a tap, visible rust staining in the toilet tank, or fittings and exposed sections of pipe that look orange or flaky on the outside.

There's no cleaning fix for heavily corroded galvanized pipe. If this is what's happening in your home, the long-term answer is repiping — replacing the old steel supply lines with copper or PEX. It's a significant project, but it's also a one-time fix that typically improves pressure throughout the house immediately and eliminates the rust issue entirely.

## Low Pressure That Points to a Leak

A drop in water pressure that appeared suddenly — not a gradual decline over years, but a noticeable change over days or weeks — can mean there's a leak somewhere in your supply system. The water going somewhere it shouldn't means less of it arrives at your faucets.

Here's how to do a basic leak check at home:

1. Make sure no water is running anywhere in the house — no appliances, no irrigation, no ice makers.
2. Find your water meter and note the reading, including the small dial or digital flow indicator.
3. Wait 15–30 minutes without using any water.
4. Check the meter again. If the reading changed or the flow indicator moved, water is leaving the system somewhere.

If the leak is behind drywall, you may see bubbling paint, a soft spot in the wall, or a faint musty smell before you see any actual water. Slab leaks — leaks in pipes running under the concrete foundation — are common in Riverside County's older housing stock and can be especially hard to detect. You might notice warm spots on the floor, the sound of running water when everything is off, or an unexplained spike in your water bill.

This is where professional leak detection earns its value. Acoustic detection equipment and thermal imaging can locate a leak inside a wall or under a slab without opening every wall in the house — saving time, money, and a lot of drywall.

## When to Stop Troubleshooting and Make the Call

Do your own checks first — they're worth doing. But call a plumber when:

- Pressure is low throughout the entire house and your shutoff valves are fully open
- You've confirmed pressure below 40 PSI at a hose bib
- Your home has galvanized pipes and pressure has been declining for years
- Your water meter shows movement when nothing is running
- You hear water running inside walls or under the floor with no obvious source
- Your water bill jumped without a clear explanation

None of these are emergencies in the way a burst pipe is, but they don't get better on their own. A leak left unaddressed can cause structural damage and mold growth. Corroded pipes will keep narrowing. A failing PRV can swing the other direction and send pressure too high, stressing your fixtures and supply lines.
