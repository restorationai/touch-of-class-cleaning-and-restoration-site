You searched "best plumber near me" because something needs fixing and you don't want to get burned. Maybe a slow drain turned into a standing-water situation. Maybe you're planning a water heater replacement and want to do this right. Either way, the next 10 minutes spent on this checklist will save you from the most common hiring mistakes homeowners make with plumbing contractors.

## How Do You Know If a Plumber Is Actually Qualified?

A qualified plumber holds a valid state contractor's license, carries general liability insurance, and can hand you proof of both before the job starts. In California, plumbing contractors are licensed through the Contractors State License Board (CSLB) and must hold a C-36 classification. You can verify any contractor's license status at [cslb.ca.gov](https://www.cslb.ca.gov/Consumers/Hire_A_Contractor/) in about 60 seconds.

Beyond the license, look for these signals:

- **A physical business address.** Not a P.O. box, not just a cell number. A real company has a real location.
- **Workers' compensation coverage.** If an uninsured worker is injured on your property, you can be held liable. Ask specifically about workers' comp, not just general liability.
- **Time in business.** A contractor who has operated under the same license for several years has a track record you can research.
- **Verifiable reviews.** Google, Yelp, and the BBB all show review history. Look for patterns in the negatives, not just the star average.

This post deliberately skips the deep dive on license verification because that topic gets its own full treatment. Here the focus is on everything that happens after you confirm the license checks out.

## How Many Plumbing Quotes Should You Get?

For jobs under $200 (a straightforward toilet flapper, a supply line swap), one reputable company is usually enough. For anything over $300, get at least two written quotes. For major work like a water heater installation, a slab leak repair, or a whole-house repipe, three quotes is a reasonable standard.

The reason isn't just price comparison. Getting multiple quotes teaches you the vocabulary of the job. When two plumbers both mention a specific part or step and a third does not, that's a signal worth asking about. "The other quotes included a drip pan and expansion tank -- why isn't that in yours?" is a question that reveals a lot about how a contractor operates.

Do plumbers give free estimates? Some do, some charge a diagnostic or trip fee, especially for emergency calls or complex leak detection. A trip fee is not a red flag on its own -- it covers a technician's time and fuel. What matters is that the fee is disclosed upfront and credited toward the job if you proceed.

## What Should a Plumbing Estimate Actually Include?

A written itemized estimate is the single most important document in any plumbing job. It should break out parts, labor, the call-out or trip fee, permit costs if applicable, and disposal of old equipment. Before you sign anything, ask this exact question: "Is this the total installed price, including parts, labor, permits, disposal, and the call-out fee?"

If the answer is anything other than a clear yes, ask them to revise the estimate until it is. Vague estimates are how jobs balloon from $800 to $1,400 before the truck leaves your driveway.

Here is what a complete estimate covers:

1. **Itemized parts list** with model numbers or specifications where relevant ("50-gallon Bradford White electric, model RE250T6-1NCWW" beats "water heater").
2. **Labor hours or a flat-rate total** with a clear scope of work (what is included, what is not).
3. **Permit line item** -- many jurisdictions require permits for water heater replacements, gas line work, and repiping. A contractor who skips permits to save you money is saving themselves paperwork at your expense.
4. **Disposal fee** for old equipment haul-away.
5. **Call-out or trip fee** -- confirm whether it is included in the total or added on top.
6. **Warranty terms** -- both on parts (manufacturer warranty) and labor (contractor warranty). One year on labor is common for installation work.

## Flat Rate vs Hourly Plumbing Pricing: Which Is Better?

Flat-rate pricing means you pay a fixed price for a defined job regardless of how long it takes. Hourly pricing means you pay for time and materials, with the final bill depending on how long the work runs. Neither model is inherently better -- what matters is transparency.

Flat rate works well for standard jobs with predictable scope: water heater replacements, toilet installations, drain cleaning with a drum auger. You know the number before work starts, and an efficient technician doesn't cost you extra.

Hourly can make sense for diagnostic work or jobs where the scope is genuinely unknown (chasing an intermittent leak, opening a wall to inspect old galvanized pipe). The risk is scope creep. If you go hourly, ask for a not-to-exceed cap in writing before work begins.

One thing to watch: a very low flat rate on a complex job sometimes signals that the estimate doesn't include everything. That's where the "total installed price" question earns its keep.

## What Are the Red Flags That Tell You to Walk Away?

Several warning signs consistently appear before a bad plumbing hire. Recognizing them early saves you from a much larger problem later.

**Walk away if:**

- They demand full payment in cash before starting work. A deposit of 10-33% is normal for larger jobs; full payment upfront is not.
- They can't or won't provide proof of license and insurance before starting.
- The estimate is verbal only. If it isn't in writing, it doesn't exist.
- They pressure you to decide immediately, especially by claiming the price is only good "right now."
- They suggest skipping permits to save money. Unpermitted work can void your homeowner's insurance and create problems at resale.
- They have no verifiable business history -- no reviews, no address, no license you can look up.
- They quote dramatically lower than every other estimate without explaining why.

That last point deserves its own paragraph. A bid that comes in 40% below the others is not a deal -- it's a question. Either the scope is incomplete, the materials are substandard, the labor is unlicensed, or the contractor plans to find reasons to charge more once work is underway. Ask what the low bidder is leaving out. The answer tells you everything.

## Should You Always Take the Lowest Plumbing Bid?

No. The lowest bid is rarely the best value once you account for the full cost of the job. A plumber who installs a water heater without a permit, skips the expansion tank required by code, or uses a builder-grade unit with a 6-year warranty instead of a 12-year unit may charge $300 less today and cost you $900 more in three years.

The right question isn't "who is cheapest?" It's "who is giving me the most complete job at a fair price?" Compare estimates line by line. If one includes a permit and the others don't, add the permit cost to the others before comparing totals. If one includes haul-away and the others don't, add that too.

Middle bids from established local contractors with verifiable reviews tend to represent the best value. They're priced to cover real costs without the markup of a national franchise or the corner-cutting of the lowest bidder. ## What Questions Should You Ask Before Hiring a Plumber?

Keep this short list ready before any plumbing call:

1. Can you provide your license number and proof of insurance before starting?
2. Will I receive a written itemized estimate, and is that the total installed price?
3. Does this job require a permit, and is pulling that permit included in your estimate?
4. What warranty do you offer on parts and on your labor?
5. How long have you been operating under this license?
6. If you find additional problems once the job is open, what is your process for communicating scope changes and getting my approval before proceeding?

That last question is underused. On jobs that involve opening walls, accessing a slab, or diagnosing a leak, surprises happen. A good contractor has a clear process for stopping, calling you, and getting written authorization before the scope expands. A contractor who just keeps working and presents a larger bill at the end is a contractor to avoid.

For jobs involving [leak detection](/services/leak-detection/) or drain issues, also ask whether they use a camera to document conditions before and after -- that footage protects both of you if there's ever a dispute about what was found.
