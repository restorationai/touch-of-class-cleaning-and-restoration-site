The short answer: a drain snake is the right tool for a simple clog — hair, soap scum, a wad of grease near the drain opening. Hydro jetting is the right tool when buildup has narrowed the pipe over months or years, when roots have threaded into a sewer line, or when snaking has already been tried and the slow drain came back within weeks. Knowing which one you actually need saves you from paying for the more aggressive service when you don't need it — or from repeatedly snaking a line that will never stay clear without a thorough cleaning.

## How Each Method Works

A drain snake — also called a drain auger — is a flexible metal cable that a plumber feeds into the drain opening and rotates until the tip breaks through whatever is blocking the pipe. Think of it as punching a hole through the clog. The blockage gets dislodged or pulled back out, flow is restored, and the job is done in under an hour in most cases. Snakes come in different sizes for sink drains, toilets, and main sewer lines, but the principle is the same.

Hydro jetting uses pressurized water — typically in the range of 3,000 to 4,000 PSI for residential lines — delivered through a specialized nozzle that sprays in multiple directions simultaneously. Instead of punching through a clog, the water scours the interior walls of the pipe, blasting away grease, mineral scale, and organic buildup all the way to the pipe wall. The debris is then flushed downstream and out of the system. Because the nozzle travels the full length of the pipe, hydro jetting cleans the entire run, not just the spot where the blockage was worst.

## When a Snake Is the Right Call

Snaking makes sense when the problem is a discrete, localized clog — the kind that develops over a few days rather than over months. Common scenarios:

- A bathroom sink drains slowly after a haircut or a product-heavy styling session.
- A kitchen drain backs up after a holiday meal where grease went down the drain.
- A toilet won't flush because something — a wad of tissue, a small object — is lodged in the trap.
- A single fixture is affected while every other drain in the house runs normally.

In these situations, the pipe itself is probably in fine shape. The clog is near the fixture, the pipe walls are relatively clear, and a snake will resolve it quickly and affordably. There's no reason to bring in high-pressure water equipment for a problem that a cable auger handles in 20 minutes.

Snaking is also the safer first step in older homes where pipes may already be weakened. High-pressure water in a corroded cast-iron or clay sewer line can cause more harm than good — which is exactly why a reputable plumber will typically run a camera inspection before recommending hydro jetting on an aging system.

## When Hydro Jetting Is Worth the Investment

Hydro jetting earns its higher cost in situations where buildup is the problem, not just a single obstruction.

**Recurring slow drains.** If a drain has been snaked two or three times in the past year and keeps slowing down, the cable is clearing a path through accumulated grease or scale, but the walls of the pipe are still coated. The buildup closes back in. Hydro jetting removes what's on the walls, not just what's in the middle.

**Grease-heavy kitchen lines.** In Southern California's climate, cooking grease that goes down a drain doesn't solidify the way it does in colder regions — but it does accumulate in layers along pipe walls over time, especially in longer horizontal runs. A snake passes through it. A hydro jet removes it.

**Root intrusion.** Tree and shrub roots find their way into sewer lines through joints and small cracks, especially in older clay or Orangeburg pipe. A snake can cut through fine root tendrils, but it won't clear a pipe that's become heavily infiltrated. Hydro jetting with a root-cutting nozzle can clear the line thoroughly enough to restore flow — though if roots have entered, a camera inspection afterward is important to assess whether the pipe itself needs repair. That's where [sewer line repair](/sewer-line-repair) becomes part of the conversation.

**Pre-sale or pre-rental cleaning.** Hydro jetting a main sewer line before listing a home or turning over a rental property gives you a clean baseline and can surface problems (like root intrusion or pipe offsets) before they become a buyer's or tenant's emergency.

## What a Camera Inspection Changes

Neither snaking nor hydro jetting tells you what's actually going on inside the pipe. A drain camera does. A plumber inserts a flexible camera line into the drain and watches live video of the pipe interior — seeing grease buildup, root intrusion, cracked pipe sections, bellied sections where the pipe has settled and holds standing water, or offset joints where the ground has shifted.

In Corona and the surrounding Inland Empire, the expansive clay soils common throughout Riverside County can cause pipe movement over time, particularly in homes built in the 1960s through 1980s when clay sewer pipe was standard. A camera inspection before hydro jetting isn't just a precaution — it's how a plumber confirms the pipe can handle the pressure and identifies whether a recurring drain problem is actually a [drain cleaning](/drain-cleaning) issue or a structural one that requires repair.

If a camera shows a collapsed section, a severe belly, or significant root damage, hydro jetting will clean what's there but won't fix the underlying problem. You'd be back to the same slow drain within months.

## Comparing the Two Side by Side

**Snaking is typically the better choice when:**
- The clog is recent and localized to one fixture
- The pipe is older and its condition is unknown
- Budget is a primary concern and the drain was running well before this incident
- A camera inspection hasn't been done yet

**Hydro jetting is typically the better choice when:**
- The drain has been snaked before and the problem returned
- Multiple drains in the house are running slowly
- There's known grease buildup or root intrusion
- The pipe has been inspected and confirmed to be in good structural condition
- You want a thorough cleaning rather than a temporary fix

## Making the Call

For most homeowners, the decision starts with a phone call to a licensed plumber who can ask a few questions — how long has the drain been slow, has it been snaked before, which fixtures are affected — and recommend the right starting point. Sometimes that's a snake. Sometimes it's a camera first, then a decision. Rarely should hydro jetting be the first step without knowing what's in the pipe.
