Most tank water heaters last **8 to 12 years**. Tankless units typically run **15 to 20 years** before they need replacing. Those ranges assume the unit has been flushed periodically, the anode rod has been replaced when needed, and the water supply isn't unusually hard or corrosive. If your water heater is inside that window and acting up, repair is often worth it. If it's past it — or if the repair cost is climbing toward half the price of a new unit — replacement usually makes more financial sense. Here's how to think through the decision.

## How to Tell Where Your Water Heater Stands

Start with the age. The serial number on the data plate tells you the manufacture date, though the format varies by brand. A common encoding puts the year in the first two digits after a letter prefix — so a serial starting with **F14** was made in 2014. If you can't decode it, the manufacturer's website usually has a lookup tool, or you can call the model number in.

Once you know the age, factor in what you're seeing:

- **Rust-colored or metallic-tasting water** from the hot tap points to corrosion inside the tank — a sign the anode rod has been depleted and the steel lining is being attacked.
- **A rumbling or popping sound** when the burner fires is sediment — mineral scale that has settled on the tank floor and is now being superheated. It shortens the life of the element and the tank itself.
- **Water pooling around the base** may be a loose drain valve or a failing pressure-relief valve, both repairable. But a seam leak or a pinhole in the tank wall is not — once the tank itself is breached, replacement is the only fix.
- **Inconsistent hot water** — you're running out faster than you used to, or the temperature swings — can mean a failing heating element (on electric units), a thermocouple or gas valve issue (on gas units), or simply a tank that's undersized for a household that has grown.

In Southern California, the water coming out of the tap is notoriously hard. Corona sits in a region where mineral content is high enough that sediment buildup happens faster than the national average, which is one reason local water heaters often trend toward the shorter end of that 8–12 year window without regular maintenance.

## The Repair-vs-Replace Decision, Made Practical

A useful rule of thumb: **multiply the repair estimate by the age of the unit**. If that number exceeds the cost of a new water heater, replace it. A $400 repair on a 10-year-old tank — a unit that may have 2 years of useful life left — rarely pencils out the way the same repair does on a 4-year-old unit.

Repairs that are generally worth doing:

- Replacing a thermocouple or igniter on a gas unit (relatively low cost, extends life significantly)
- Swapping a heating element or thermostat on an electric unit
- Replacing a pressure-relief valve or drain valve
- Flushing sediment and replacing the anode rod on a unit under 8 years old

Situations that almost always point to replacement:

- Any leak from the tank body itself (not a fitting, not a valve — the tank)
- Persistent rust in the hot water after the anode rod has already been replaced
- A unit older than 12 years needing a repair that costs more than $300–400
- A tank that's been repaired twice in the past two years

If you're on the fence, a plumber can do a quick inspection and give you a realistic read on the tank's remaining life. That conversation costs far less than a surprise failure.

## What Happens When a Water Heater Fails Without Warning

Not every water heater sends warning signs. Some fail suddenly — a tank seam gives way, a fitting cracks, or the pressure-relief valve discharges and doesn't reseat. When that happens:

1. **Turn off the cold water supply** to the heater. There's a shutoff valve on the cold inlet line at the top of the tank. Turning it off stops water from continuing to flow into (and out of) the unit.
2. **Cut power or gas to the unit.** For electric heaters, flip the dedicated breaker. For gas, turn the gas valve on the unit to the pilot position or off entirely.
3. **Open a hot-water faucet somewhere in the house** to relieve pressure in the system and slow the flow from any remaining water in the tank.
4. **Attach a garden hose to the drain valve** at the base of the tank and run it to a floor drain or outside — this lets you empty the tank without flooding the space further.
5. **Call a plumber.** A failed tank in a garage or utility closet can put 40 to 80 gallons of water into a space quickly. If the area around the heater is already wet, document it with photos before cleanup begins.

If you're in a home with a water heater in a finished space — an interior closet, a hallway, a second-floor laundry — a slow tank failure can cause significant damage before it's noticed. A drip pan with a drain line (or a leak sensor connected to an auto-shutoff) is cheap insurance.

## Choosing a Replacement: Tank vs. Tankless

If you're replacing rather than repairing, you'll face this choice. The short version:

**Standard tank water heaters** cost less upfront, are simpler to install (especially as a like-for-like swap), and are easier to service. The tradeoff is standby heat loss — the energy spent keeping 40 or 50 gallons hot around the clock.

**Tankless (on-demand) water heaters** heat water only when you need it, which cuts standby energy use substantially. They last longer and take up less space. The tradeoffs: higher upfront cost, more complex installation (gas units often need a larger gas line; electric units may require a panel upgrade), and a slight delay before hot water reaches the tap.

**Heat pump water heaters** are worth mentioning in California specifically. They use ambient air to heat water rather than burning gas or running resistance coils, making them significantly more efficient than either standard option. California's incentive programs have made them increasingly cost-competitive, and they perform well in the mild climate of the Inland Empire where ambient temperatures stay moderate most of the year.

The right choice depends on your home's gas and electrical capacity, the size of your household, and how long you plan to stay in the home. A plumber who does both installations can walk you through the tradeoffs without steering you toward one product over another.

## Signs You Should Call a Plumber Today, Not Next Week

Some water heater problems are inconvenient. Others are urgent. Call the same day if you notice:

- Water actively pooling or dripping from the tank body
- A rotten-egg smell near a gas water heater (this can indicate a gas supply issue — if the smell is strong, leave the area and call your gas utility before calling a plumber)
- The pressure-relief valve discharging repeatedly or not reseating after it opens
- No hot water at all combined with a gas smell or a tripped breaker that keeps tripping

For anything less urgent — sediment noise, inconsistent temperature, water that's taking longer to heat — scheduling within a few days is usually fine, but don't let it sit for weeks. A stressed water heater that's ignored tends to fail at the least convenient moment.
