A slab leak is a break or pinhole in the water lines running beneath your home's concrete foundation. In Southern California's Inland Empire, where older copper supply lines meet reactive soil and seasonal ground movement, these leaks are more common than most homeowners expect — and they rarely announce themselves loudly. The warning signs are subtle at first: a warm patch on the floor, a water bill that climbed for no reason, the faint sound of running water in a silent house. Catch them early and you're looking at a targeted repair. Miss them and you're looking at foundation damage, mold, and a significantly larger bill.

## The 7 Warning Signs to Know

**1. Your water bill jumped without explanation.**
A slab leak can bleed dozens of gallons a day into the soil beneath your foundation. If your usage habits haven't changed but your bill from your water provider has spiked, that water is going somewhere. Pull two or three consecutive bills and compare them.

**2. You can hear water running when everything is off.**
Turn off every faucet, appliance, and irrigation zone in the house. Stand in a quiet room — often a hallway or near the center of the home — and listen. A faint hiss, trickle, or rushing sound with no obvious source is a strong indicator of pressurized water escaping underground.

**3. Warm or hot spots on your floors.**
This one is specific to hot-water line leaks. If a section of your tile, hardwood, or slab-on-grade flooring feels noticeably warmer than the surrounding area — especially first thing in the morning before the sun has heated the house — the heat is likely radiating up from a leaking hot-water pipe below.

**4. Cracks in your flooring or baseboards.**
Water saturating the soil beneath a concrete slab causes it to shift. That movement shows up as new cracks in tile grout, separation between baseboards and the floor, or hairline fractures in the slab itself. A single crack isn't a diagnosis, but cracks appearing alongside other signs on this list should raise your concern level.

**5. Damp or wet carpet with no surface explanation.**
If a section of carpet feels spongy, smells musty, or shows a discolored wet patch and there's no plumbing fixture directly above it, moisture may be wicking up through the slab. Press a dry towel firmly to the area — if it pulls moisture, the source is below, not above.

**6. Low water pressure throughout the house.**
A significant leak underground reduces the pressure available to your fixtures. If multiple faucets and showerheads seem weak at the same time and nothing has changed with your service line or pressure regulator, a slab leak is one of the causes worth ruling out.

**7. Mold, mildew, or a persistent musty smell at floor level.**
Chronic moisture beneath a slab creates ideal conditions for mold to colonize the underside of flooring and the lower portions of walls — often before you see visible growth. If a room smells damp and earthy despite no visible spills or flooding, and the smell is strongest near the floor, take it seriously. In Corona's warm climate, mold can establish itself within 24 to 48 hours of sustained moisture exposure.

## What To Do Right Now

If you recognize two or more of the signs above, here's how to respond before a professional arrives.

1. **Do the meter test.** Locate your water meter — typically near the curb in a concrete box. Write down the reading, then make sure no water is used in the house for 30 minutes. Check the meter again. If the dial has moved, you have an active leak somewhere in your system.
2. **Check the meter's leak indicator.** Most modern meters have a small triangular or star-shaped dial that spins even with very low flow. If it's moving with all fixtures off, that confirms active water loss.
3. **Locate your main shutoff.** Know where it is before you need it. In most Corona-area homes built on slab foundations, the main shutoff is near the front of the house at the meter or at a valve on the exterior wall. If you suspect a major or fast-moving leak, shutting off the main stops the damage from compounding while you arrange professional leak detection.
4. **Document what you're seeing.** Take photos of wet spots, cracks, stained flooring, and your meter readings. This documentation helps your plumber pinpoint the location faster and supports any insurance claim you may need to file.
5. **Don't ignore it.** A slow slab leak won't seal itself. The longer pressurized water contacts your foundation soil, the more the ground shifts, and the more expensive the repair becomes.

## What NOT To Do

- **Don't jackhammer or dig on your own.** You won't know where the pipe actually runs, and breaking into the slab in the wrong place adds cost and concrete repair to an already expensive job.
- **Don't use pipe-sealing additives.** Products marketed to seal leaks from the inside are not designed for pressurized copper or PEX supply lines under a slab. They can clog fixtures and don't address the root cause.
- **Don't assume the leak is small because you can't see it.** A pinhole in a supply line under pressure loses water continuously. The visible damage at the surface is almost always less than what's happening below it.
- **Don't delay calling your insurance company if you have a homeowner's policy.** Many policies cover sudden and accidental slab leaks but exclude damage from leaks that were known and ignored. The sooner you report it, the cleaner your claim.

## When To Call a Professional — and What They'll Do

If the meter test confirms active water loss and you can't identify a visible source, it's time for professional leak detection. Electronic leak detection uses acoustic listening equipment and pressure testing to pinpoint the break without opening up the slab unnecessarily. Thermal imaging can also identify temperature anomalies consistent with a hot-water line leak.

Once the location is confirmed, repair options typically include:

- **Direct-access repair:** Opening the slab at the specific leak point, replacing the damaged section of pipe, and patching the concrete. This is appropriate for isolated breaks.
- **Pipe rerouting:** Running a new line through the walls or attic, bypassing the damaged underground pipe entirely. This avoids the slab altogether and is often preferred when the existing pipe has corroded in multiple places.
- **Epoxy pipe lining:** In some cases, a resin liner can be applied to the interior of the pipe to seal pinholes. Suitability depends on pipe diameter, material, and the extent of deterioration.

## The Longer Picture: Foundation and Water Damage

Even after the pipe is repaired, the moisture that soaked into your slab and subfloor doesn't disappear on its own. Depending on how long the leak was active, you may need drying equipment run for several days to bring moisture readings down to acceptable levels. Flooring that has absorbed standing water — particularly hardwood, laminate, or carpet padding — often needs to be removed before the slab can dry properly. Mold assessment is worth considering if the leak was slow and long-running.

This is worth knowing before you get the repair estimate: the plumbing fix and the water damage remediation are two separate scopes of work, and both matter for the long-term health of your home.
