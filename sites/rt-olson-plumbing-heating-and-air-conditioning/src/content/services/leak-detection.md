Your water bill jumped $80 last month and nothing obvious has changed. No dripping faucet, no wet ceiling, no puddle under the sink — just a meter that keeps spinning even when every fixture in the house is off. That gap between what you can see and what the meter knows is exactly where leak detection starts. Finding that water before it saturates a slab, rots out a wall cavity, or feeds a mold colony is the entire job.

## What leak detection actually involves

Leak detection is a diagnostic service, not a repair — though RT Olson Plumbing, Heating and Air Conditioning handles both. The goal is to locate the precise point of failure without opening walls or jackhammering concrete until you know exactly where to cut. That means using equipment that listens to, pressurizes, or traces your plumbing system rather than guessing.

Electronic leak detection equipment picks up the acoustic signature of water escaping under pressure — a frequency range inaudible to the human ear but distinct enough for a trained technician to pinpoint within inches on a concrete slab. Thermal imaging cameras reveal temperature differentials behind drywall where a slow leak has cooled the framing. Pressure decay testing isolates which branch of your system is losing pressure so the search zone narrows before any equipment touches the floor. For underground water leak detection on supply lines, tracer gas (typically a nitrogen-hydrogen blend) can be introduced into the line and detected at the surface with a gas sensor, even through several inches of soil or concrete.

The timeline depends on the complexity of the system and how long the leak has been active. A slab leak on a single-story home with accessible cleanouts can often be located in two to three hours. A multi-zone commercial property with aging copper and no as-built drawings takes longer. ## Our process

1. **Meter and isolation check.** Before any equipment comes off the truck, a technician reads the water meter with all fixtures off, then systematically isolates zones — hot, cold, irrigation — to determine which branch is losing water. This narrows the search area before any specialized equipment is deployed.

2. **Pressure decay testing.** The suspect line is pressurized and monitored over a set interval. A measurable pressure drop confirms an active leak in that segment and gives the technician a baseline for how significant the breach is — a slow weep versus a full-bore failure behaves very differently under pressure.

3. **Acoustic and thermal scanning.** Electronic listening equipment is walked along the slab, wall cavities, or ceiling plane above the suspect line. Thermal imaging runs concurrently where wall access exists. The two methods cross-confirm each other: acoustics finds the noise source, thermal finds the moisture footprint.

4. **Pinpoint marking and documentation.** Once the leak location is confirmed, the technician marks the surface and documents findings with photographs and written notes — the format insurance carriers and adjusters expect when a claim is involved.

5. **Repair scope and handoff.** RT Olson Plumbing, Heating and Air Conditioning provides a written repair scope on-site. Because detection and repair are handled by the same licensed team (CA License #997337), there is no handoff delay between finding the leak and fixing it.

## What separates a good leak detection response from a bad one

The most common mistake is skipping the isolation step and going straight to acoustic scanning on the full system. Without isolating branches first, background noise from irrigation timers, water heaters, and neighboring units creates interference that leads technicians to the wrong location — and the wrong cut in your slab.

A second failure point is relying on a single detection method. Acoustic equipment is highly effective on pressurized supply lines but will not find a drain leak, a slow slab seep below the water table, or a leak that has already equalized pressure. Experienced operators layer methods: acoustic plus thermal plus pressure testing, not acoustic alone.

For insurance purposes, documentation matters as much as location accuracy. Adjusters need photographs of the meter test, the isolation sequence, the scan path, and the pinpoint mark — not just a verbal report. Carriers will sometimes deny a claim or reduce the payout when the detection record is incomplete, because they cannot confirm the leak was active (versus pre-existing damage) without a time-stamped diagnostic trail.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Corona and the surrounding Inland Empire sit in a semi-arid climate, but the soil conditions here create a specific slab-leak risk that catches homeowners off guard. The expansive clay soils common throughout Riverside County shift seasonally — swelling with winter rains and contracting through the dry summer months. That movement stresses copper supply lines embedded in slabs, particularly in homes built in the 1970s through 1990s when thin-wall copper was standard. A high water bill that appears in late spring, after the soil has dried and contracted, is a recognizable pattern in this region. Older neighborhoods in Corona, Norco, and Eastvale see this cycle repeatedly.

Irrigation systems add another variable. Warm-season watering schedules push underground lateral lines hard from May through October, and a slow drip at a fitting buried 12 inches down can go unnoticed until the meter tells the story.
