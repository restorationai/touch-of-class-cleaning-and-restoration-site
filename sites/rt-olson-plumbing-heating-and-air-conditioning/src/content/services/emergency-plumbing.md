## What emergency plumbing actually involves

Emergency plumbing isn't just a faster version of a scheduled service call. The work begins with locating the failure — which isn't always where the water is visible. A slab leak, for example, can surface several feet from the actual pipe breach. A failed supply line inside a wall may not announce itself until water wicks through drywall and shows up as a stain on the ceiling below.

Once the source is confirmed, the repair scope depends on what broke and where. Common emergency calls involve burst or frozen supply lines, failed water heater connections, sewer line backups, broken shutoff valves, and ruptured washing machine or refrigerator supply hoses. Each requires different tools and different repair strategies — a sewer backup calls for a camera inspection and jetting equipment, while a pinhole leak in copper may need a section cut out and resweat or a press-fit repair.

## Our process

1. **Isolate the water source.** Before anything else, the water supply to the affected area — or the whole house if needed — is shut off to stop active flow. If you haven't already located your main shutoff, the technician can guide you by phone before arrival.

2. **Diagnose the failure point.** Visual inspection alone isn't enough for hidden leaks. Depending on the situation, the technician may use an electronic leak detector, a sewer camera, or pressure testing to pinpoint exactly where the system failed. Guessing costs more time and money than a proper diagnosis.

3. **Make the repair and pressure-test.** The pipe, fitting, valve, or connection is repaired or replaced. Before the water is restored, the repaired section is pressure-tested to confirm the fix holds. A repair that passes visual inspection but fails under normal operating pressure is a callback waiting to happen.

4. **Document the work and affected areas.** The technician notes what was found, what was repaired, and the condition of surrounding materials — especially if water has reached walls, flooring, or cabinetry. This documentation matters if you file a homeowners insurance claim for secondary water damage.

5. **Advise on next steps.** If water has been standing or soaking into structural materials, the plumber will flag that a water damage assessment may be warranted. Plumbing and water mitigation are separate scopes — knowing where one ends and the other begins protects you from gaps in the repair.

## What separates a good emergency plumbing response from a bad one

The most common mistake in an emergency plumbing call is treating the symptom rather than the system. A plumber who patches the visible break without checking line pressure upstream, inspecting the surrounding pipe age, or verifying the repair under load leaves you exposed to a second failure within days.

On sewer backups specifically, snaking the line without a camera inspection is a short-term fix. Roots, offset joints, and collapsed sections will cause the same backup again. A camera run takes an extra 20–30 minutes and tells you whether you have a maintenance issue or a structural one.

For insurance purposes, the documentation step is where many contractors fall short. Adjusters want to see the failure point photographed before repair, the repair method noted, and any water migration to adjacent materials recorded. Without that, a claim for secondary damage — warped flooring, wet insulation, damaged cabinetry — becomes much harder to support.
