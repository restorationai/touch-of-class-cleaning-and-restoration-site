## Why Boulder City Properties See Distinct Storm Damage Patterns

Boulder City's building stock reflects its unusual origin: it was a planned federal construction camp built to house Hoover Dam workers in the early 1930s and was later developed under strict city controls that limited growth for decades. That history means the city has a high proportion of older masonry and stucco construction alongside mid-century ranch homes, many of which were built before modern wind-load and flashing standards. Stucco that has micro-cracked from decades of thermal cycling can look intact until a microburst drives rain horizontally against it, at which point water infiltrates the wall cavity and can reach framing before the storm even ends.

Boulder City also sits at roughly 2,500 feet elevation, higher than the Las Vegas Valley floor. That elevation difference means storm cells that have already dropped rain on Henderson can still carry significant moisture and wind energy by the time they reach Boulder City. Flash flooding is a real secondary concern: the city's desert soil sheds water quickly, and drainage channels can overwhelm during a heavy cell, pushing debris and sediment toward structures on lower-lying lots.

Hail events, while not annual, do occur and tend to damage composition shingles, HVAC condensing units, and skylights. Because Boulder City has a small, tight-knit contractor community, demand for emergency restoration services spikes sharply after any significant storm, making early contact critical.

## Our Storm Damage Restoration Process in Boulder City

When a storm call comes in, the first priority is a safe exterior assessment before any crew member enters an affected structure. Wind-driven damage can compromise roof decking, soffits, and gable vents in ways that are not visible from the ground. The crew uses moisture meters and thermal imaging to map water intrusion behind walls and under flooring, because surface readings in stucco construction routinely understate how far moisture has traveled.

Once the scope is mapped, the process moves through controlled phases: emergency tarping or board-up to stop further water entry, extraction of any standing water, placement of industrial drying equipment calibrated to the interior volume and material types, and documentation of all affected materials for the insurance claim. Structural drying follows the IICRC S500 standard, with daily moisture readings logged until materials reach regional equilibrium moisture content. Final steps include debris removal, tree-limb cleanup from affected areas, and coordination with any trade specialists needed for roofing or structural repairs.
