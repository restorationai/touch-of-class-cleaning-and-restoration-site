## Why Henderson Properties See Water Damage Differently

Henderson sits in one of the fastest-growing corridors in Nevada, which means the housing stock spans several very different construction eras. Planned master communities built in the late 1990s and early 2000s feature concrete block construction with stucco exteriors, a combination that traps water inside wall cavities with almost no outward sign. Newer construction in the city's southern and eastern edges often uses engineered wood framing and composite sheathing that absorbs moisture faster than traditional lumber and begins to lose structural integrity sooner.

The valley's monsoon season, typically July through September, adds a layer of risk that surprises homeowners who moved here from wetter climates. Henderson's hardpan caliche soil sheds water rather than absorbing it, so even a moderate storm can push runoff through garage slabs, window wells, and low-lying entry points faster than interior drains can handle. That same caliche layer also means that water that does penetrate a foundation has nowhere to go, pooling against footings until it finds a crack or a seam.

Indoor plumbing failures are the other major driver. Water heaters in Henderson homes work harder than in cooler climates because of high mineral content in Southern Nevada water, which accelerates sediment buildup and corrosion. A failed water heater in a garage or utility closet can release forty to eighty gallons before a homeowner notices, and in a single-story home with slab-on-grade construction, that water has nowhere to go but under the flooring.

## Our Emergency Water Cleanup Process in Henderson

The first step on every job is moisture mapping, not extraction. Before a single pump runs, technicians use thermal imaging cameras and calibrated moisture meters to locate where water has traveled, not just where it is visible. In Henderson's stucco-and-block construction, water frequently migrates along the slab edge and wicks up interior drywall from the bottom plate, appearing dry at the surface while the base of the wall stays saturated.

Once the scope is documented, truck-mounted extraction equipment removes standing water from hard floors and carpet. High-velocity air movers and low-grain refrigerant dehumidifiers are positioned according to the IICRC S500 standard for structural drying, with equipment counts and placement calculated for the actual square footage and material types present, not a generic room count. In Henderson's climate, ambient temperatures outside are high, but interior air-conditioning keeps indoor humidity low, which actually slows evaporation from materials because the moisture gradient between the wet material and the cool indoor air is compressed. Equipment placement accounts for this.

Moisture readings are logged daily. Drying is not declared complete until all affected materials reach documented dry standard, typically measured against an unaffected reference area in the same structure.
