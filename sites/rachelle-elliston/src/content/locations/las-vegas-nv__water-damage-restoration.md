## Why Las Vegas Properties See Water Damage Issues

The Las Vegas valley sits in a basin ringed by mountains, and while annual rainfall is low, the ground beneath most neighborhoods is caliche-heavy, a calcium carbonate hardpan that sheds water rather than absorbing it. During monsoon season, which runs roughly July through September, that soil can push surface water toward foundations and slab edges faster than most homeowners expect. Homes built in the 1980s and 1990s, which make up a large share of the valley's residential stock, commonly used polybutylene or early CPVC supply lines that have aged past their reliable service life. When those lines fail, water spreads quickly across tile and concrete slab floors, wicking into drywall at the base and into cabinetry before the leak is even discovered. Flat and low-slope roofs common on stucco ranch-style homes here also create ponding conditions during heavy summer storms, and even a small roof membrane breach can saturate insulation and the top plates of interior walls.

## Our Water Damage Restoration Process in Las Vegas

The first priority on any water loss is stopping the spread. The crew assesses the source, confirms the water supply is shut off or the intrusion point is controlled, and then begins extraction using truck-mounted and portable extraction units rated for high-volume removal. In slab-on-grade construction, which is the norm across the valley, standing water migrates under baseboards and into wall cavities fast, so thermal imaging cameras are used alongside moisture meters to map the full extent of saturation before any drying equipment is placed.

Once extraction is complete, the drying phase begins. The low relative humidity in Las Vegas is an asset here: ambient conditions often support faster evaporation than in coastal or humid climates. The team calibrates the number and placement of industrial air movers and desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers to the actual moisture readings, not a generic formula. Drying logs are recorded daily and shared with the homeowner and, when applicable, the insurance adjuster. The process follows IICRC S500 standards for water damage restoration, and the crew holds IICRC WRT and ASD credentials, so the documentation stands up to carrier review.

Materials that cannot be dried in place, saturated drywall below the flood cut line, swollen base cabinets, or compromised flooring, are removed and documented photographically before disposal. Antimicrobial treatment is applied to affected framing and subfloor surfaces as part of the standard scope.
