A storm rolls through the Las Vegas Valley fast. Within minutes, wind-driven rain can push water under door thresholds, lift roofing felt off decking, and drop debris through attic vents. What looks like surface damage from the street is often the beginning of a longer problem: water tracking behind stucco, saturating blown-in insulation, and sitting against framing that dries slowly in a sealed attic. The window between the storm passing and secondary damage taking hold is shorter than most homeowners expect.

## What Storm Damage Restoration actually involves

Storm damage is rarely one problem. It is usually three or four problems that arrived at the same time. A single event can combine structural breach (missing shingles, cracked fascia, displaced flashing), water intrusion (ceiling stains, wet drywall, soaked insulation), and debris impact (broken windows, crushed gutters, tree limbs through fencing or rooflines).

Restoration work addresses all of these in sequence, not in isolation. That means tarping and boarding before anything else to stop the weather from continuing to enter, then moisture mapping to find where water has already traveled, then debris removal and structural assessment, and finally the repair and drying work that closes the loss. Equipment on a typical storm job includes penetrating and non-penetrating moisture meters, thermal imaging cameras to find hidden saturation behind intact surfaces, commercial desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers, and high-velocity air movers staged to dry cavities without trapping moisture inside wall assemblies.

Timeline varies with severity. A contained roof breach with minor attic wetting can be stabilized in a day and dried within three to five days. A loss involving structural compromise, multiple water entry points, or debris impact to load-bearing elements takes longer and may require engineering review before reconstruction begins.

## Our process

1. **Emergency stabilization.** The first priority is stopping the loss from growing. That means emergency tarping of roof breaches, boarding broken windows and doors, and clearing debris that poses an immediate safety hazard. Desert Valley Contracting Inc responds 24/7 and documents conditions on arrival with photographs and moisture readings before any material is moved.

2. **Damage mapping and moisture assessment.** Once the structure is stabilized, the team conducts a full moisture survey using thermal imaging and calibrated meters. Water from a roof breach does not stay in the attic. It follows framing, runs down interior wall cavities, and pools at the lowest point it can reach. Every affected area is logged and photographed for the insurance file.

3. **Debris removal and structural triage.** Fallen tree limbs, displaced roofing material, broken glass, and wind-scattered debris are removed and documented. Any structural element that may be compromised (rafters, sheathing, load-bearing walls) is flagged before drying equipment is placed, because equipment staging around an unstable structure creates additional risk.

4. **Controlled drying and dehumidification.** Drying is set up to move air through cavities, not just across surfaces. Insulation that has absorbed water typically cannot be dried in place and must be removed to allow the framing behind it to dry completely. Moisture readings are logged daily until affected assemblies reach target values consistent with regional equilibrium moisture content for the Las Vegas climate.

5. **Scope documentation and handoff.** Once drying is complete and verified, Desert Valley Contracting Inc produces a written scope of the structural damage remaining. This document supports the insurance claim and serves as the basis for any reconstruction work.

## What separates a good storm damage response from a bad one

The most common mistake in storm damage work is treating the visible damage as the full extent of the loss. A roof with three missing shingles and a water stain on the ceiling below it may have six feet of wet insulation and damp top plates that will support mold growth within days if the attic is not opened and dried properly.

A second failure point is inadequate documentation. Insurance adjusters reviewing storm claims look for a clear chain of evidence: pre-mitigation photos, moisture readings with GPS-stamped timestamps, a materials list for anything removed, and a drying log that shows conditions improving to acceptable levels. Gaps in that record give carriers grounds to dispute line items.

A third issue is debris removal that outpaces documentation. Tree limbs and roofing material that caused structural damage need to be photographed in place before they are moved. Once the debris is gone, proving the mechanism of damage becomes much harder.

Finally, in the Las Vegas area, stucco exteriors can mask water intrusion for weeks. Water enters at a crack or failed flashing, wicks into the substrate, and the stucco face dries out while the framing behind it stays wet. Thermal imaging during the initial assessment is the reliable way to find these pockets before they become a mold problem.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

North Las Vegas sits in a desert climate, but the monsoon season (roughly July through September) delivers the most damaging storms the valley sees. Thunderstorms during this period can produce wind gusts above 60 mph, heavy localized rainfall that overwhelms flat or low-slope roofing, and hail that cracks tile and damages HVAC equipment on rooftops. The dry months before monsoon season often leave roofing materials brittle and more vulnerable to wind uplift.

The valley's caliche soil sheds water quickly rather than absorbing it, which means heavy rain that enters a structure has nowhere to drain and tends to pool against foundations and slab edges. Homes built on slab foundations (common throughout the North Las Vegas area) are particularly vulnerable to water tracking under door thresholds and along the slab perimeter.
