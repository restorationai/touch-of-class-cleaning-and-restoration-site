After a fire or a water loss, the structure gets the attention first, but for most families the hardest part is the belongings: the clothing, bedding, rugs, drapery, and upholstery that carry real value and memory. It is easy to assume all of it is gone. In practice, a large share of soft goods that look ruined by smoke, soot, or water can be recovered through specialized textile restoration, and doing so is usually far less costly than replacing everything at once. Textile and fabric restoration is the part of the job that treats those items as recoverable property rather than debris.

## What textile and fabric restoration actually involves

Textiles behave differently from hard surfaces after a loss. Smoke and soot are made of fine, often oily particles that work into fibers and leave both staining and odor; water and humidity add the risk of mold and dye migration if items sit wet; and different fabrics, cotton, wool, synthetics, leather, and delicates, each tolerate different cleaning methods. A shirt and an area rug and a leather jacket damaged in the same fire do not get treated the same way.

The work starts with sorting the salvageable from the non-salvageable and documenting both, because that inventory is what an insurance claim runs on. Salvageable items are cleaned with methods matched to the fabric and the contamination, wet cleaning and controlled laundering for many washables, dry or solvent methods for items that cannot take water, and specialized soft-goods cleaning for heavier soot loads. Where smell lingers after cleaning, the item is deodorized at the molecular level rather than perfumed over, using controlled methods such as ozone or hydroxyl processing. The goal throughout is recovery to a genuinely clean, wearable, usable condition, not a surface improvement that fades.

## Our process

1. **Assessment and inventory.** Each item is examined and sorted into salvageable and non-salvageable, then photographed and logged. This documented inventory is the backbone of the insurance claim and the record of what was recovered.

2. **Pack-out and tracking.** Salvageable textiles are packed out and moved to a controlled cleaning environment, tracked as their own category so nothing is misplaced within a larger contents or structural restoration.

3. **Fabric-matched cleaning.** Items are cleaned by methods appropriate to the material and the type of contamination, so a wool rug, a cotton wardrobe, and a leather piece each get the right treatment rather than a one-size approach that can set stains or damage fibers.

4. **Deodorization.** Any remaining smoke or musty odor is treated at the source through controlled deodorization, and items are confirmed odor-free after airing out rather than judged on the day of cleaning.

5. **Return and documentation.** Recovered items are returned clean and wrapped, with an itemized record of what was treated, what was recovered, and what was assessed as non-salvageable for your claim.

## What separates good textile restoration from bad

The most common failure is treating odor as a fragrance problem. Spraying a scent over smoke-contaminated fabric leaves the residue in the fibers, and the smell comes back within days. Real recovery removes the contamination first and only then deodorizes, and it verifies the result after the item has aired out.

The second is skipping the inventory. When soft goods are cleaned in bulk without an item-by-item record, homeowners lose the documentation their claim depends on, and genuinely non-salvageable items get quietly mixed in with recovered ones or, worse, salvageable items get written off by default. A photographed, itemized inventory protects both the recovery and the claim. The third is applying one cleaning method to everything; delicates, leather, and heirloom pieces need individual handling, and a blanket approach is how salvageable items become losses.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

In the North Las Vegas and greater Las Vegas Valley climate, the dry desert air is an advantage for drying and airing textiles, but it does nothing to remove smoke residue or soot on its own, dry does not mean clean. After a water loss, the same dry heat can accelerate dye migration and set stains if wet items are left sitting, so getting textiles into the restoration process quickly matters. Household dust and fine grit common to the valley also load into rugs and upholstery over time, which is worth addressing during any recovery cleaning.
