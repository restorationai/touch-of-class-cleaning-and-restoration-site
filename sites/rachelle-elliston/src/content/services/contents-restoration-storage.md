After a water loss, fire, or mold event, the structure gets most of the attention, but the belongings inside often tell a different story. Smoke odor embeds itself in upholstered fibers within hours. Soot from synthetic materials leaves a greasy film on electronics that corrodes contacts if it sits. Waterlogged documents, photographs, and heirlooms begin to warp and mold within 24 to 48 hours. Contents restoration is the discipline of recovering those items systematically, not just wiping them down and hoping for the best.

## What Contents Restoration & Storage actually involves

Contents restoration covers every movable item in a home or commercial property: furniture, clothing, electronics, artwork, documents, collectibles, and personal effects. When a loss event makes a space unlivable or unsafe, those items need to be inventoried, packed out, transported, cleaned, deodorized, and stored in a climate-controlled facility until the structure is ready to receive them again.

The work is more technical than it looks. Ultrasonic cleaning tanks use high-frequency sound waves to lift soot and residue from hard surfaces without abrasion. Ozone chambers and hydroxyl generators neutralize odor molecules embedded in soft goods. Document freeze-drying stabilizes wet paper before mold can colonize it. Electronics require specialized cleaning and testing before they can be declared salvageable or a total loss.

Storage matters as much as cleaning. Items packed out of a fire-damaged home cannot go into a standard warehouse where temperature and humidity fluctuate. Climate-controlled storage prevents secondary damage during the weeks or months a reconstruction can take. Every item is logged with photos and a condition report before it leaves the property, creating the documentation trail an insurance adjuster needs to process the claim accurately.

## Our process

1. **Pre-move inventory and condition documentation.** Before a single box is packed, every item in the affected area is photographed and logged. Condition at the time of loss is recorded item by item. This protects the homeowner and creates the evidence base for the insurance claim.

2. **Pack-out and categorization.** Items are sorted into salvageable, questionable, and non-salvageable categories. Fragile items, electronics, and high-value pieces are packed separately with appropriate protective materials. Soft goods, hard goods, and documents travel in separate streams because they require different cleaning methods.

3. **Cleaning and deodorization.** Hard surfaces go through ultrasonic cleaning or hand-cleaning with appropriate chemistry depending on the material. Soft goods are laundered using ozone-injection or hydroxyl treatment to break down odor compounds. Electronics are cleaned and tested. Documents and photographs that sustained water damage are stabilized through freeze-drying when the volume warrants it.

4. **Climate-controlled storage.** Cleaned and processed items are stored in a secure, climate-controlled facility. Inventory is maintained so any item can be located and retrieved during the restoration period. Homeowners can request access to specific items if they need them before the project is complete.

5. **Pack-back and placement.** When the structure is cleared for occupancy, items are returned, unpacked, and placed. A final condition check is completed against the original inventory to confirm nothing was damaged in transit or storage.

## What separates a good contents response from a bad one

The most common failure in contents restoration is the absence of a pre-move inventory. Without item-level documentation at the time of loss, disputes arise over pre-loss condition, value, and whether damage occurred before or during the pack-out. Insurance adjusters rely on that documentation to approve replacement or restoration costs. A crew that packs first and documents later creates problems that are difficult to unwind.

The second common failure is treating all materials the same. Protein soot from a kitchen fire behaves differently than synthetic soot from burning plastics. Protein residue is nearly invisible but deeply penetrating and requires enzymatic chemistry; synthetic soot is oily and requires different surfactants and techniques. A technician who applies the same cleaning method to both will leave one category damaged or still contaminated.

Third: storing items in non-climate-controlled environments during a long reconstruction. A property sitting in remediation for two months in a Las Vegas summer means ambient temperatures that can reach well above 100°F in an unventilated space. Items that survived the original loss can be destroyed by secondary heat and humidity exposure if storage is not controlled.

IICRC-certified technicians are trained to recognize these distinctions. The IICRC FSRT and WRT credentials address the behavior of different loss types and the appropriate response for contents in each scenario.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Las Vegas and the surrounding valley present specific challenges for contents storage. Summer temperatures regularly exceed 110°F outdoors, and an unventilated garage or non-climate-controlled warehouse can reach temperatures that warp wood furniture, delaminate electronics, and accelerate mold growth on items that absorbed any moisture during a water loss. Winter inversions trap particulates and can affect air quality inside storage facilities that rely on outside air exchange. Climate-controlled storage is not a premium add-on in this region, it is a baseline requirement for protecting contents through a restoration project.
