Smoke does not stop at the room where the fire burned. Within hours of a structure fire, soot particles travel through HVAC ducts, settle into wall cavities, and bond to cool surfaces throughout the home. The smell that greets you three days later is not a leftover odor, it is active off-gassing from char residue and synthetic materials that burned. Fire damage restoration is the work of reversing that chemistry before it becomes permanent, and the window to do it well is shorter than most homeowners expect.

## What Fire Damage Restoration actually involves

A fire loss has three overlapping problems: structural damage from heat and flame, surface contamination from soot and char, and airborne and embedded odor from combustion byproducts. Each requires a different approach, and treating only one while ignoring the others is the most common reason a restored home still smells like smoke six months later.

Soot itself is not uniform. Protein soot from kitchen fires is nearly invisible but leaves a greasy, varnish-like film that bonds aggressively to painted surfaces and cabinetry. Synthetic soot from burning plastics, insulation, or modern furnishings is oily, dark, and highly acidic, it etches metal fixtures and pits glass within days if left untreated. Wet smoke residue from a slow-burning, low-oxygen fire smears when wiped incorrectly and requires different chemistry than the dry, powdery soot from a fast, high-heat fire.

Equipment used in a professional fire restoration includes HEPA air scrubbers to capture fine particulate, thermal foggers that push deodorizing agents into the same porous pathways smoke traveled, and ozone generators used in unoccupied spaces to oxidize odor molecules at the molecular level. Structural materials, drywall, framing, subfloor, are assessed for char depth and thermal damage before any finish work begins.

## Our process

1. **Emergency stabilization and safety assessment.** Before any cleaning begins, the structure is evaluated for compromised load-bearing members, broken utilities, and hazardous materials. Board-up and tarping protect the property from weather and unauthorized entry while the scope is developed. Desert Valley Contracting Inc holds IICRC FSRT (Fire and Smoke Restoration Technician) certification, OSHA training, and EPA Lead-Safe certification, relevant because older North Las Vegas homes built before 1978 may have lead paint disturbed by fire and suppression water.

2. **Soot characterization and surface pre-testing.** Technicians identify the soot type present in each area of the structure. This step determines which cleaning agents are appropriate, alkaline cleaners for protein residue, dry chemical sponges for dry soot, solvent-based products for synthetic residue. Applying the wrong chemistry to the wrong soot type can permanently set staining into drywall and wood grain.

3. **Contents pack-out and inventory.** Salvageable contents are documented, packed, and moved to a controlled environment for cleaning and deodorization. This protects personal property from further contamination during structural cleaning and gives adjusters a clear inventory of items requiring replacement versus restoration.

4. **Structural cleaning and deodorization.** Affected surfaces are cleaned in sequence, working from ceiling to floor and from unaffected areas toward the fire origin. HEPA vacuuming precedes wet cleaning to avoid smearing loose soot. Thermal fogging and, where appropriate, ozone treatment follow surface cleaning to address odor molecules embedded in framing, insulation, and subfloor materials.

5. **Clearance verification and documentation.** Before reconstruction begins, affected areas are re-inspected and documented with photographs and moisture readings. Any char-damaged structural members are flagged in the written scope. This documentation package is delivered to the homeowner and their insurance carrier.

## What separates a good fire response from a bad one

The most common technical failure in fire restoration is treating odor as a surface problem. Painting over smoke-stained drywall without sealing with an appropriate shellac-based or pigmented primer traps odor temporarily but does not eliminate it, warmth from sunlight or HVAC cycling will reactivate it. Experienced technicians seal before painting, not instead of it.

A second common failure is skipping soot characterization. A crew that applies a single cleaning protocol across a whole structure will over-wet dry soot (spreading it deeper into porous materials) or under-treat protein soot (leaving the greasy film that causes persistent odor). Insurance adjusters reviewing a supplement or a re-clean claim will look for documentation that different soot types were identified and addressed with appropriate methods.

HVAC systems are frequently under-scoped. Smoke travels the path of least resistance, and duct systems distribute soot throughout the entire structure. A fire in one room can contaminate registers, coils, and air handlers throughout the home. Failing to address the HVAC system is one of the leading causes of odor recurrence after a restoration is declared complete.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

North Las Vegas sits in the Mojave Desert, where summer temperatures regularly exceed 110°F. That heat matters after a fire: soot residue on exterior-facing walls, window frames, and metal fixtures bakes faster in desert sun than in a temperate climate, accelerating the bonding of acidic smoke residue to surfaces. Restoration work that might allow a few days of buffer in a cooler climate may need to begin sooner here to prevent permanent etching on glass and metal.

Dry desert air also means that odor molecules disperse differently than in humid regions. Thermal fogging is particularly effective in low-humidity environments, but ozone treatment timelines may need adjustment because low ambient moisture slows the ozone oxidation process. Technicians familiar with the local climate account for this in their deodorization protocols.
