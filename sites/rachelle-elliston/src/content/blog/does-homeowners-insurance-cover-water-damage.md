## The Short Answer: It Depends on the Source

Homeowners insurance usually covers water damage, but only when the water arrived suddenly and accidentally. A pipe that bursts on a Tuesday afternoon is typically covered. A slow drip behind your bathroom vanity that rotted the subfloor over six months is typically not. The distinction insurers draw is between sudden, accidental loss and gradual damage or neglect. Understanding that line before you file a claim can save you from a denial that damages your claims history without putting a dollar in your pocket.

---

## What Standard Policies Usually Cover

Most HO-3 homeowners policies (the most common form in Nevada) cover water damage that is sudden and internal. That generally includes:

- A supply line that fails on a washing machine, dishwasher, or refrigerator ice maker
- A pipe that freezes and bursts (less common in the Las Vegas Valley, but not unheard of during hard desert winters)
- An accidental overflow from a bathtub, sink, or toilet
- Water that enters through a roof opening caused by a covered peril, such as wind damage from a monsoon storm
- Sudden discharge from a water heater that fails

When the damage qualifies, your policy typically pays to dry and restore the affected structure, replace damaged personal property up to your contents limit, and sometimes cover additional living expenses if the home is uninhabitable during repairs.

One thing many homeowners miss: the policy pays for the water damage to your home, not for repairing the appliance or pipe that failed. The broken supply line itself is usually a maintenance item, not a covered loss.

## What Policies Almost Never Cover

Knowing the exclusions is just as important as knowing the coverage. The following are routinely excluded from standard homeowners policies:

**Flooding from outside the home.** If water enters because a storm drain backed up, a nearby wash overflowed, or surface water pushed through your garage door, that is flood damage. Flood coverage requires a separate policy, typically through the National Flood Insurance Program or a private flood carrier. North Las Vegas sits in a region where desert monsoon events can overwhelm drainage infrastructure quickly, so flood exposure is real even in a dry climate.

**Gradual or long-term leaks.** If a slow drip behind a wall has been seeping for weeks or months, insurers classify that as a maintenance failure. The logic: a homeowner exercising reasonable care would have detected and fixed it. The damage from hidden leaks is often the most expensive kind because mold can colonize wet framing within 24 to 48 hours of sustained moisture, and by the time you smell it or see discoloration, the problem is well established.

**Sewer or drain backup.** Water that pushes back up through a floor drain or toilet because of a blocked or overwhelmed sewer line is excluded from most base policies. Some carriers offer a backup-of-sewers-and-drains endorsement for an additional premium. It is worth asking your agent whether you have it.

**Neglect or deferred maintenance.** A roof that has been deteriorating for years, a water heater well past its service life, or corroded supply lines that were never replaced fall into this category. Insurers can and do inspect after large claims, and evidence of known deterioration can result in a partial or full denial.

## How to Read Your Policy Before a Loss Happens

The declarations page tells you your coverage limits and deductible. The actual coverage language lives in the policy form itself, usually a dense document most people never open until something goes wrong. The sections to locate now, before any loss:

1. **Perils insured against** (Section I, Coverage A and B): Look for whether your policy is open-peril (covers everything not excluded) or named-peril (covers only what is listed). HO-3 policies are typically open-peril for the structure.
2. **Exclusions**: Search for the words "flood," "surface water," "seepage," "gradual," and "neglect." These are the words that appear in denial letters.
3. **Endorsements**: A separate page or pages that add or subtract coverage from the base form. This is where sewer backup coverage or service line coverage would appear if you purchased it.
4. **Loss settlement**: How the policy values your loss. Replacement cost value (RCV) pays what it costs to rebuild or replace. Actual cash value (ACV) deducts depreciation. The difference on a water-damaged floor or ceiling can be substantial.

If the language is unclear, your state's Division of Insurance can help interpret standard policy provisions, and a public adjuster can review your specific situation.

## What to Do the Moment You Discover Water Damage

The steps you take in the first hour affect both the physical outcome and your claim.

1. **Stop the source if you safely can.** For a burst pipe or failed supply line, turn off the main water shutoff. In most North Las Vegas homes built in the last few decades, the shutoff is near the meter at the street or in a utility area. If you do not know where yours is, find it today.
2. **Document everything before touching it.** Walk through with your phone and photograph standing water, wet materials, affected walls, ceilings, and flooring. Take wide shots that show the room context and close shots that show the damage detail. Timestamp matters: the photo metadata establishes when you discovered the loss.
3. **Call your insurance company to report the loss.** Most carriers have a 24-hour claims line. Reporting promptly is usually a policy requirement. Delayed reporting can give an adjuster grounds to question the timeline.
4. **Begin mitigation, but keep records.** You have a duty to mitigate under most policies, meaning you cannot let damage worsen if you can reasonably prevent it. Moving furniture off wet carpet, extracting standing water, and opening windows for airflow are reasonable steps. Save every receipt for anything you spend.
5. **Do not discard damaged materials before the adjuster sees them.** Wet drywall, flooring, and insulation are evidence. If materials must be removed for health reasons before the adjuster arrives, photograph and set them aside rather than hauling them to the curb.

## When the Claim Is Approved: What the Restoration Process Looks Like

Insurance approval is the beginning of the work, not the end of the problem. Water damage restoration follows a defined sequence:

- **Moisture mapping**: Technicians use thermal imaging cameras and moisture meters to find exactly where water traveled, including inside wall cavities and under flooring. Water moves along framing and subfloor in ways that are not visible at the surface.
- **Extraction and drying**: Industrial air movers and dehumidifiers run for several days. The goal is to bring structural moisture readings down to levels that will not support mold growth. This is not a process that a box fan from the hardware store can replicate.
- **Controlled demolition if needed**: Wet drywall and insulation typically cannot be dried in place. Sections are cut out to expose framing, dried, and then rebuilt.
- **Clearance testing**: Before reconstruction begins, moisture readings should confirm the structure is genuinely dry, not just dry at the surface.

If your policy covers the loss, the restoration contractor often works directly with your adjuster, submitting documentation and scope of work that aligns with the claim. That coordination matters: a scope that does not match the adjuster's estimate can delay your project.
