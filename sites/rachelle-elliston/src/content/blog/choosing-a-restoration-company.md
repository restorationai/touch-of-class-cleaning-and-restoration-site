Choosing a restoration company after water damage, fire damage, or a mold discovery is one of the few purchasing decisions you'll make while your home is actively getting worse. The wrong call costs you time, money, and sometimes your insurance claim. The right call gets a trained crew on-site with the right equipment before secondary damage sets in. Here is what to look for, what to avoid, and how to avoid the contractors who profit from your panic.

## Why the First Hour Matters More Than You Think

Water soaks into drywall and insulation within minutes. Smoke residue begins bonding to surfaces within hours. Mold can start colonizing damp material in as little as 24 to 48 hours under the right temperature and humidity conditions. In the Las Vegas Valley, that timeline can compress even further during summer months when indoor humidity spikes after a monsoon intrusion or a burst evaporative cooler line.

This urgency is real, and unfortunately, it is exactly what predatory contractors count on. Storm chasers and unlicensed crews show up at your door before you have had a chance to think clearly. They pressure you to sign an Assignment of Benefits form immediately, which can hand over control of your insurance claim before you understand what you are agreeing to. Knowing what a legitimate restoration company looks like before you need one is the single best protection you have.

## What to Look for in a Legitimate Restoration Company

**Verifiable licensing and insurance.** In Nevada, contractors performing restoration work are required to hold a state contractor's license. Ask for the license number and look it up on the Nevada State Contractors Board website before anyone touches your property. A company that hesitates to provide this information is telling you something important.

**Industry certifications.** The Institute of Inspection, Cleaning and Restoration Certification (IICRC) sets the technical standards for water damage drying (the S500 standard), fire and smoke restoration (the S700), and mold remediation (the S520). These are not marketing badges. They represent specific training in how moisture behaves in building materials, how smoke residue differs by fire type, and how to contain and remove mold without cross-contaminating clean areas. Ask which certifications the company holds and which technicians on your job will be certified.

**A written scope of work before any work begins.** A professional company will assess the damage, explain what they found, and give you a written estimate that itemizes the work. Vague verbal promises and handshake agreements are red flags. The scope should reference industry-standard pricing software (Xactimate is the most common) so your insurance adjuster can review it line by line.

**Direct insurance billing experience.** Navigating a property damage claim is complicated. A restoration company that works regularly with insurance carriers knows how to document losses properly, photograph affected materials systematically, and communicate with adjusters in the language they expect. This does not mean the contractor works for your insurer. It means they know the process well enough to advocate for a complete and accurate claim.

**Local presence and a physical address.** Out-of-state storm chasers disappear after they collect payment. A company with a local office, local employees, and a history in the North Las Vegas area has a reputation to protect. Check Google reviews, the Better Business Bureau, and the Nevada Contractors Board for complaints.

## Red Flags That Should Stop You Cold

Not every warning sign is obvious. Watch for these:

- A contractor who shows up unsolicited at your door after a storm, flood, or fire in your neighborhood.
- Anyone who asks you to sign an Assignment of Benefits form before explaining what it means in plain language.
- Pressure to start demolition or tear-out before your insurance company has been notified or an adjuster has had a chance to inspect.
- No written estimate, or an estimate that lists lump sums rather than itemized line items.
- Cash-only payment demands or requests for full payment upfront.
- A company that cannot name the specific drying standard or remediation protocol they follow.
- Unusually low bids. Proper water damage drying requires calibrated equipment, moisture mapping, and multiple site visits over several days. A company bidding significantly below others is almost certainly cutting steps.

On the mold side specifically, be cautious of any contractor who offers to both test for mold and remediate it. Independent testing and remediation are typically kept separate so there is no financial incentive to find a problem or to declare it resolved prematurely.

## Questions to Ask Before You Hire Anyone

Treat the initial call or site visit like an interview. These questions will separate professionals from opportunists:

1. Are you licensed with the Nevada State Contractors Board, and can you give me your license number?
2. Which IICRC certifications does your company hold, and will a certified technician be on my job?
3. How do you document the damage for insurance purposes?
4. What drying or remediation standard do you follow?
5. Will you provide a written, itemized scope of work before any work begins?
6. Do you bill my insurance carrier directly, or do I pay and seek reimbursement?
7. Who do I contact if I have questions once the job is underway?

A company that answers these questions clearly and without defensiveness is worth continuing the conversation with. One that deflects, rushes you, or cannot answer the technical questions should be removed from your list.

## Understanding the Restoration Process So You Can Evaluate the Work

Knowing roughly what each type of restoration involves helps you recognize whether a contractor is doing the job correctly.

**Water damage restoration** is not just drying out what you can see. Moisture migrates into wall cavities, under flooring, and into structural framing. A proper job involves moisture mapping with meters and thermal imaging, strategic placement of air movers and dehumidifiers, and daily monitoring until readings confirm the structure has returned to normal moisture levels. Pulling equipment too early is one of the most common shortcuts, and it leads to mold growth weeks later.

**Fire and smoke damage restoration** varies significantly depending on what burned. Synthetic materials produce oily, acidic smoke that penetrates porous surfaces quickly. Natural materials like wood produce dry smoke that is easier to clean from hard surfaces but still embeds in fabrics and insulation. Odor removal is not masking. It requires thermal fogging, ozone treatment, or hydroxyl generators depending on the situation, followed by sealing affected surfaces.

**Mold remediation** is a containment and removal process, not a spray-and-wipe job. Proper remediation involves isolating the affected area with negative air pressure, removing contaminated materials, cleaning and treating surfaces, and verifying clearance with post-remediation testing. The source of the moisture that caused the mold must also be identified and corrected, or the mold will return.
