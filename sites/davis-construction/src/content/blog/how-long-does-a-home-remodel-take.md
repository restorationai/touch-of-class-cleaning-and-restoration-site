**TL;DR:** A bathroom remodel typically takes 3-6 weeks once work starts. A kitchen remodel runs 6-12 weeks. A whole-house renovation or home addition can take 4-9 months. But the clock doesn't start on demo day, design, permitting, and material lead times add 4-12 weeks before a single wall comes down. Plan for the full timeline, not just the construction phase.

If you've gotten a bid for a kitchen remodel and the contractor said "six weeks," you probably assumed that meant six weeks until you're cooking dinner in a finished kitchen. It usually doesn't. That six weeks is often the active construction window, it doesn't count the three weeks waiting on cabinet delivery, the two weeks for permit approval, or the HOA architectural review that some newer subdivisions require before a contractor can pull a permit at all. This guide breaks down what actually happens, in what order, and how long each phase realistically takes.

## What phases make up a home remodel timeline?

Every remodel moves through four phases: design and selection, permitting, demolition and rough-in work, and finish installation. The total project duration is the sum of all four, not just the time workers are on site.

Here's how those phases stack up for a typical project:

**Phase 1: Design and material selection (2-8 weeks)**
This is where most homeowners underestimate. Choosing cabinets, tile, fixtures, countertops, and finishes takes time, and once you order, lead times vary widely. Stock cabinets from a local supplier might arrive in 1-2 weeks. Semi-custom cabinets from a manufacturer often run 4-6 weeks. Quartz countertops are typically templated after cabinets are set, then fabricated in 1-2 weeks after that. If you want a specific tile that has to ship from a distributor, add another 2-3 weeks. The fastest remodels happen when selections are locked in before demo starts.

**Phase 2: Permits (1-4 weeks, sometimes longer)**
Most structural, electrical, plumbing, and HVAC work requires a permit. In Madison and the surrounding area, a straightforward residential permit often takes 1-2 weeks for approval. Projects that touch load-bearing walls, add square footage, or require engineered drawings can take 3-4 weeks. If your home is in a newer subdivision with an HOA, common in areas like Hampton Cove in Huntsville or many of the planned communities near Research Park Boulevard in Madison, add time for the HOA's architectural review committee. Some HOAs meet monthly, which means a missed submission window can add 3-5 weeks to your start date. Factor this in early.

**Phase 3: Demolition and rough-in (1-4 weeks depending on scope)**
Demo is fast. A kitchen gut-out typically takes 1-3 days. The rough-in phase, framing changes, electrical, plumbing, HVAC ductwork, is slower and depends heavily on how much is being changed. Moving a single outlet is a half-day job. Relocating a load-bearing wall, moving a drain stack, or adding a bathroom where there wasn't one before can take 2-3 weeks of rough-in work, plus inspection time between trades.

**Phase 4: Finish installation (2-8 weeks)**
This is the most visible phase: drywall, paint, cabinets, countertops, tile, flooring, fixtures, and trim. It also has the most scheduling dependencies. Countertops can't be templated until cabinets are set. Tile work has to cure before grouting. Plumbing fixtures go in after tile. Each step has a handoff, and any delay in one trade ripples into the next.

## How long does each type of remodel take?

Timelines vary by project scope. These ranges reflect realistic full-project durations from design sign-off to final walkthrough, not just active construction days.

**Bathroom remodel (single bath, cosmetic to mid-range scope): 3-6 weeks active construction**
A straightforward bathroom update, new tile, vanity, fixtures, and paint without moving plumbing, can be done in 2-3 weeks of active work. Add a week or two if you're relocating the toilet or shower, or if the existing plumbing needs updating. Factor in 2-4 weeks of pre-construction for selections and permitting, and a typical bathroom remodel runs 5-10 weeks total.

**Kitchen remodel (mid-range, full gut): 6-12 weeks active construction**
Kitchens are the most sequenced remodel in residential construction. Cabinet delivery timing drives everything else. A realistic full kitchen remodel, new layout, cabinets, countertops, appliances, flooring, and electrical updates, runs 8-14 weeks total when you include pre-construction. If you're keeping the existing layout and just replacing surfaces, you can cut that to 5-7 weeks.

**Primary suite addition or major bathroom addition: 8-16 weeks**
Adding a bathroom where there wasn't one requires running new drain lines, venting to the exterior, and often adding an electrical circuit. This is closer to new construction than remodeling. Expect 2-4 weeks of permitting and design, 4-6 weeks of rough-in and structural work, and 4-6 weeks of finishes.

**Whole-house renovation: 4-9 months**
If you're renovating multiple rooms, updating systems (electrical panel, HVAC, plumbing), and making structural changes, you're in a different category. The [National Association of Home Builders](https://www.nahb.org/) notes that whole-house renovations on older stock, particularly homes built before 1990, frequently encounter hidden conditions (outdated wiring, undersized drain lines, moisture damage behind walls) that add 2-6 weeks of unplanned work. Budget time for it.

**Home addition: 3-6 months**
A room addition involves foundation work, framing, roofline tie-in, exterior finish, and full interior buildout. Permit timelines are longer because engineered drawings are typically required. In Madison and Huntsville, expect 3-5 weeks just for permitting and plan review on an addition.

## What causes remodel timelines to run long?

Most delays fall into a handful of categories. Knowing them in advance lets you either prevent them or build in buffer.

**Material delays.** The single most common cause of extended timelines. Custom or semi-custom cabinets, specialty tile, and certain appliances all have lead times that fluctuate. Order materials before demo starts, not after. A good contractor will help you sequence this.

**Permit and inspection delays.** Inspections have to be scheduled and passed before the next phase can begin. If rough-in electrical fails inspection, you're waiting for a re-inspection slot, sometimes 3-5 business days out. Structural changes that require an engineer's stamp add another layer.

**HOA architectural review.** In newer Madison subdivisions and Hampton Cove developments, exterior changes, siding, rooflines, window additions, deck additions, often require HOA approval before a permit can be pulled. Some HOA review committees meet only once a month. If you miss the submission deadline, you're waiting another 4-5 weeks. Check your HOA covenants before finalizing a project start date.

**Hidden conditions.** Older homes, particularly the 1970s-1980s ranch homes common in established Madison and Athens neighborhoods, frequently have surprises behind walls: knob-and-tube wiring that has to be replaced before new work can be permitted, cast-iron drain lines that need lining or replacement, or moisture damage from a slow leak that was never addressed. A thorough pre-construction walkthrough catches some of this, but not all of it.

**Scope changes mid-project.** Every time a homeowner changes a selection or adds work after demo has started, the timeline extends. This isn't a contractor problem, it's a planning problem. Locking in all selections before work begins is the single best thing you can do to protect your timeline.

## How do you get a realistic timeline before signing a contract?

Ask your contractor to walk you through the project in phases, not just give you a start date and an end date. A contractor who can tell you "cabinets arrive week three, countertop template happens week five, final inspection is scheduled for week eight" has actually thought through the sequencing. One who gives you a single end date without the milestones in between is guessing.

Also ask: when does the permit application get submitted? What's the current review timeline at the city? Are there any HOA submittals required, and when is the next committee meeting? These questions take five minutes and can save you weeks of frustration.

If you're comparing bids and one contractor's timeline is significantly shorter than the others, ask specifically what's different about their sequencing. Sometimes a faster timeline reflects genuine efficiency. More often it reflects an optimistic assumption about permit turnaround or material delivery that won't hold up.
