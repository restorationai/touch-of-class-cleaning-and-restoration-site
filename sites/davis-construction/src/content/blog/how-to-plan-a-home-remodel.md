**TL;DR:** Planning a home remodel well before breaking ground saves money, prevents delays, and reduces stress. Start with a clear scope and realistic budget, get your design locked in before hiring anyone, pull the permits your jurisdiction requires, vet contractors carefully, and build a timeline with buffer for the unexpected. Most successful remodels follow these five steps in order, not all at once.

You have a list of things you want to change about your house and a rough number in your head. Maybe it's a kitchen that hasn't been touched since 1998, a master bath that needs a full gut, or an addition that would finally give your family enough room. The gap between that mental picture and a finished project is where most remodels go wrong. Not because the work is hard, but because the planning was skipped or rushed. Here is how to do it right.

## What scope and budget should you set before anything else?

Define your scope in writing before you talk to a single contractor. A written scope is a list of every room, system, and finish you want changed. It does not need to be a blueprint, but it needs to be specific enough that two different contractors can price the same job. Without it, you will get bids that are impossible to compare.

Once you have a scope, build your budget in three layers:

1. **Hard costs** are materials and labor. For a full kitchen remodel, expect $30,000 to $80,000 depending on size, cabinet quality, and appliance selections. A primary bathroom gut runs $15,000 to $40,000. A room addition starts around $150 to $250 per square foot for finished space.
2. **Soft costs** are design fees, permit fees, and engineering if required. Budget 10 to 15 percent of hard costs for this category.
3. **Contingency** is the buffer for what you find inside the walls. A 10 to 15 percent contingency is standard. On older homes, 20 percent is smarter. Homes built before 1978 may contain lead paint, which requires specific handling under [EPA Renovation, Repair and Painting (RRP) rules](https://www.epa.gov/lead).

The [National Association of Home Builders](https://www.nahb.org/) publishes annual cost data that can help you pressure-test your numbers against regional averages before you finalize a budget.

## How do you design a remodel before hiring a contractor?

Lock in your design before you solicit bids. This is the step most homeowners skip, and it is the reason so many projects run over budget. When a contractor bids against a vague description, they price the cheapest version of what you described. When they bid against a set of drawings and a finish schedule, they price what you actually want.

Design does not have to mean a full architectural package for every project. Here is a rough guide:

- **Kitchen or bath remodel**: A kitchen designer or interior designer producing a floor plan, elevation drawings, and a finish schedule is usually enough. Expect to pay $1,500 to $5,000 for this service.
- **Structural changes** (removing a load-bearing wall, adding a dormer, building an addition): You need a licensed engineer or architect. Structural drawings are required for permits in most jurisdictions.
- **Design-build firms**: Some contractors handle design and construction under one contract. This simplifies coordination and can compress the schedule, because design and preconstruction happen in parallel.

During design, nail down your finish selections: cabinet door style, countertop material, tile, plumbing fixtures, lighting. Selections that change after construction starts are the single biggest driver of cost overruns and schedule delays.

## Do you need a permit for a home remodel?

Most structural, electrical, plumbing, and mechanical work requires a permit, regardless of how small the project feels. Skipping permits creates real problems: unpermitted work can void your homeowner's insurance, complicate a future home sale, and require expensive tear-out if discovered during an inspection.

What typically requires a permit:
- Structural changes (removing walls, adding square footage)
- Electrical panel upgrades or new circuits
- Plumbing rough-in changes
- HVAC system replacements or additions
- New windows or doors that change the opening size

What often does not require a permit:
- Cosmetic work (paint, flooring, cabinet refacing)
- Direct appliance replacements on existing circuits
- Most fixture swaps (faucets, light fixtures on existing circuits)

If you live in a newer subdivision, check your HOA documents before you start design. Many planned communities in the Madison and Huntsville areas, including developments in Hampton Cove, require an architectural review committee (ARC) approval for exterior changes, additions, and sometimes even window replacements. ARC approval is separate from the city building permit and often has its own timeline of 30 to 60 days. Missing this step can result in fines or a stop-work order.

Your contractor should pull permits in their name. If a contractor asks you to pull your own permits, that is a red flag: it shifts liability to you and may indicate they are not properly licensed for the work.

## How do you choose the right contractor for a remodel?

The right contractor for a remodel is licensed, insured, experienced in your specific project type, and willing to give you a written contract with a detailed scope before you sign anything.

Here is a practical vetting checklist:

- **Verify their license**. Alabama requires general contractors working on projects over $10,000 to be licensed through the Alabama Licensing Board for General Contractors. You can verify status on the board's website.
- **Confirm insurance**. Ask for a certificate of insurance showing general liability and workers' compensation. Call the insurer to confirm it is current.
- **Check references for similar work**. A contractor who is excellent at water damage restoration may not be the right fit for a full kitchen remodel. Ask for references specifically from remodel projects, not just any past job.
- **Get at least three bids**. Compare them line by line against your scope. If one bid is dramatically lower, find out why before assuming it is the best deal.
- **Review the contract carefully**. It should include a detailed scope of work, payment schedule tied to milestones (not arbitrary dates), a change-order process, and a completion date.

## What does a realistic remodel timeline look like?

A realistic timeline has four phases, and each takes longer than most homeowners expect.

**Phase 1: Planning and design (4 to 12 weeks)**
Scope development, design drawings, finish selections, HOA review if required. Do not rush this phase. Every week you save here typically costs two weeks during construction.

**Phase 2: Permitting and contractor selection (2 to 8 weeks)**
Permit review times vary by jurisdiction. Simple permits in smaller municipalities may be approved in a week. Complex structural permits in busy jurisdictions can take 6 to 8 weeks. Run contractor bidding in parallel with permitting when possible.

**Phase 3: Construction (4 to 20 weeks depending on scope)**
A bathroom remodel typically runs 3 to 6 weeks of active construction. A kitchen runs 6 to 12 weeks. A full addition can run 4 to 6 months. These are active construction timelines and assume materials are on site before work begins.

**Phase 4: Punch list and closeout (1 to 3 weeks)**
Final inspections, touch-up work, and permit closeout. Do not release final payment until the permit is closed and your punch list is complete.

For a full breakdown by project type, see our post on [how long a home remodel takes](/blog/how-long-does-a-home-remodel-take/).

One timing note for fall planning: contractor schedules in North Alabama tend to fill up fast heading into the new year. If you want a spring construction start, begin the design and bidding process now. Waiting until February to hire a contractor for a March start is a recipe for taking whoever is available rather than whoever is right for your project.
