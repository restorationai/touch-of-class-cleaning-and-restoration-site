**TL;DR:** A remodeling contractor focuses specifically on renovating existing homes: kitchens, bathrooms, additions, and whole-home updates. A general contractor manages any construction project, including new builds, commercial work, and major structural changes. In practice, many contractors do both. The right choice depends on what your project actually involves, not just what title is on a business card.

You've got a project in mind. Maybe it's a kitchen gut-and-redo, or an addition off the back of the house, or a full interior overhaul before listing. You start calling around and half the contractors call themselves remodelers, the other half say general contractor, and a few say both. The titles feel interchangeable, but they're not quite the same thing, and hiring the wrong type can mean delays, gaps in scope, or a contractor who's great at one thing but not set up to handle the full picture.

## Is a remodeling contractor the same as a general contractor?

No, but the overlap is significant. A remodeling contractor specializes in renovating existing structures: updating what's already there rather than building from scratch. A general contractor is a broader role that covers any construction project, including new construction, commercial builds, and ground-up residential work. The key difference is scope and specialization, not licensing category.

Both types typically hold a general contractor license (in Alabama, that's issued by the [Alabama Licensing Board for General Contractors](https://www.albgc.state.al.us/)). The license doesn't tell you what they specialize in. Their portfolio and project history do. A contractor who's spent 15 years doing kitchen and bath remodels has a different skill set than one who's spent those years framing new subdivisions, even if both carry the same license class.

## What does a remodeling contractor actually do?

A remodeling contractor renovates, updates, or reconfigures existing space inside a home. They work within the existing structure and coordinate all the trades needed to complete the project: framing, electrical, plumbing, drywall, tile, cabinetry, painting, and finish work.

Typical remodeling contractor projects include:

- **Kitchen remodels**: Layout changes, cabinet replacement, countertop installation, appliance rough-ins, flooring
- **Bathroom renovations**: Tile work, fixture replacement, vanity installation, shower or tub conversion
- **Room additions**: Expanding the footprint of an existing home, tying new space into existing HVAC and electrical
- **Whole-home renovations**: Updating a 1970s ranch from top to bottom without changing the foundation or exterior envelope
- **Interior reconfiguration**: Opening up floor plans, moving non-load-bearing walls, updating trim and finishes throughout

A good remodeling contractor understands how existing homes are built, where the surprises hide (old wiring behind plaster, undersized joists, previous-owner shortcuts), and how to sequence trades so the project moves efficiently. That last part matters a lot. On a kitchen remodel, the plumber, electrician, tile setter, and cabinet installer all have to show up in the right order or the project stalls.

## What does a general contractor do that's different?

A general contractor manages construction projects at any scale, including work a remodeling specialist may not take on. The GC role is fundamentally about project management: hiring and scheduling subcontractors, pulling permits, coordinating inspections, managing the budget, and keeping the project on schedule.

Projects that typically require a general contractor rather than a remodeling specialist:

- **New home construction**: Building from a foundation up requires a different set of subs and a different sequencing logic than renovation work
- **Large structural changes**: Removing load-bearing walls, adding a second story, or reconfiguring a home's structural system requires engineering coordination and a contractor comfortable working from structural drawings
- **Commercial construction**: Office buildouts, retail spaces, and multi-family projects have different code requirements and inspection processes than residential remodeling
- **Mixed-scope projects**: A project that combines, say, a full addition, a roof replacement, and an interior renovation may need a GC who can hold the whole scope together rather than a remodeler who focuses on the interior finish side

The [National Association of Home Builders](https://www.nahb.org/) notes that the GC role is primarily one of coordination: the GC is responsible for the project outcome even when individual trades are handled by subcontractors. That accountability is what you're paying for when you hire a GC.

## Do I need a general contractor or a remodeling contractor for my project?

For most home renovation projects, a remodeling contractor with GC-level project management experience is the right fit. If your project stays within the existing footprint, doesn't involve major structural changes, and is primarily about updating or reconfiguring interior space, a remodeling specialist is well-suited.

You likely need a full general contractor if:

- You're adding square footage that requires a new foundation or structural framing
- The project involves significant structural engineering (removing load-bearing walls, adding a second floor)
- You're combining multiple major scopes: new construction plus remodeling plus exterior work
- The project has a commercial component or requires commercial-grade permitting

For most homeowners doing a kitchen renovation, a bathroom overhaul, or even a whole-home interior update, the distinction matters less than finding a contractor who has done your specific type of project many times and can show you the work.

## What about design-build vs general contractor?

Design-build is a project delivery method, not a contractor type. In a traditional model, you hire an architect or designer separately, get drawings, then hire a contractor to build from those drawings. In a design-build model, one firm handles both design and construction under a single contract.

Design-build can work well for remodeling projects where the scope is complex enough to benefit from integrated design and construction planning, but where you don't want to manage two separate relationships. The tradeoff is that you have less independent oversight: the same firm designing your kitchen is also the one building it, so errors in the design don't get caught by a second set of eyes before construction starts.

For straightforward renovation projects, most homeowners don't need a formal design-build arrangement. A remodeling contractor who does in-house design consultation, or who works regularly with a local designer, can achieve the same coordination without the formal structure.

## How do you find the right fit for your project?

Start with scope. Write down what you want done in plain terms: which rooms, what changes, what the end state looks like. Then ask any contractor you're considering how many projects like yours they've completed in the last two years and whether you can see photos or speak with a past client.

A few other things worth checking:

- **Licensing**: In Alabama, contractors on projects over $10,000 are required to be licensed. Verify the license is current before signing anything. Our post on [licensed vs unlicensed contractors in Alabama](/blog/licensed-vs-unlicensed-contractor-alabama/) covers how to check.
- **Insurance**: General liability and workers' compensation coverage protect you if something goes wrong on site. Ask for certificates before work starts.
- **Written scope**: Whether you hire a remodeler or a GC, get a detailed written scope of work before any money changes hands. Vague contracts are where disputes start.
- **Permit responsibility**: Ask who pulls the permits. On most residential renovation projects in Alabama, the contractor should pull permits, not the homeowner. A contractor who asks you to pull your own permits is shifting liability onto you.
