**TL;DR:** You need a general contractor when your remodel touches more than one trade, requires permits, or involves structural work. A single-trade job, painting a room, replacing a water heater, installing flooring, usually doesn't need a GC. But a kitchen gut, a bathroom addition, or anything that moves walls almost always does. The GC's job is to coordinate permits, schedule subcontractors in the right order, manage inspections, and be the single accountable party if something goes wrong.

You're probably here because you got a GC bid that felt high and you're wondering whether you actually need one, or because you're staring at a remodel scope that keeps growing and you're not sure where the line is. The honest answer is: project complexity is the deciding factor, not project cost. A $40,000 kitchen remodel almost always needs a GC. A $40,000 flooring replacement across an entire house probably doesn't.

## What does a general contractor actually do?

A general contractor manages the people, permits, and sequence of a construction project, they don't necessarily swing the hammer themselves. On a multi-trade remodel, a GC pulls the building permit, hires and schedules licensed subcontractors (plumber, electrician, HVAC, tile setter, framer), coordinates the inspection schedule with the local building department, and acts as the single point of contact if a sub's work fails or a timeline slips. They carry general liability insurance and typically require their subs to carry it too, which means you have a coverage chain if something goes wrong on your property.

The markup a GC charges, typically 15-25% of total project cost, buys you that coordination, that accountability, and that insurance chain. Whether it's worth it depends on what you're building.

## When do you need a general contractor?

You need a GC any time your project involves more than one licensed trade, requires a building permit, or touches structural elements. Here's the practical breakdown:

**Hire a GC for:**
- Kitchen remodels that move plumbing, add circuits, or relocate walls
- Bathroom additions or full gut-renovations
- Room additions and home additions of any size
- Finishing a basement (framing, electrical, plumbing, HVAC, drywall)
- Any project that requires a building permit and multiple inspections
- Work on load-bearing walls or structural framing
- [New home construction](/services/new-construction/) from the ground up

**You can often skip a GC for:**
- Cosmetic repaints (interior or exterior)
- Flooring replacement in rooms with no subfloor issues
- Cabinet refacing (no plumbing or electrical moves)
- Deck replacement where the footings are already in place and no permit is required in your jurisdiction
- Single-trade work: hiring one licensed plumber, one electrician, or one roofer for a defined scope

The permit question is a reliable proxy. If your jurisdiction requires a permit for the work, that's a signal the project is complex enough to warrant a GC. Permits exist because the work affects health, safety, or structural integrity, the same reasons you want a professional managing the sequence.

## What are the real risks of being your own general contractor?

Acting as your own GC, sometimes called owner-builder, is legal in most states, and some experienced homeowners do it successfully on straightforward projects. But the risks are specific and worth understanding before you decide.

**Scheduling is harder than it looks.** Subcontractors work on their own timelines. If your electrician runs two weeks late, your drywall crew can't start, which pushes your painter, which pushes your cabinet installer. A GC manages these dependencies daily. As an owner-builder, every delay becomes your problem to solve, and subs often deprioritize owner-builders who aren't repeat customers.

**Permit and inspection sequencing is easy to get wrong.** Most jurisdictions require inspections at specific stages, framing before drywall, rough plumbing before concrete pour, electrical rough-in before insulation. Miss an inspection and you may have to open up finished work. A GC knows the local inspection sequence cold; a first-time owner-builder is learning it on your house.

**Insurance gaps can be significant.** When you act as your own GC, you may not have the same coverage chain you'd have under a licensed GC's umbrella. If a subcontractor is injured on your property and isn't carrying workers' comp, you could be liable. The [National Association of Home Builders (NAHB)](https://www.nahb.org/) recommends verifying that every sub carries both general liability and workers' compensation before they set foot on a project.

**Warranty and recourse are thinner.** If a GC's sub does defective work, the GC is your point of escalation. As an owner-builder, you're negotiating directly with each sub, and if they've been paid and won't return, your options are limited to small claims court or licensing board complaints.

For a [home remodeling](/services/home-remodeling/) project that involves multiple trades, these risks compound. The more moving parts, the more value a GC provides.

## What's the difference between a general contractor and a subcontractor?

A subcontractor is a licensed specialist in one trade: a licensed electrician, a licensed plumber, a framing crew, a tile installer. They do one thing well. A general contractor coordinates multiple subcontractors under one contract and one permit. The GC is responsible for the whole project; each sub is responsible for their portion.

When you hire a GC, you sign one contract. The GC signs separate contracts with each sub, manages their payments, and holds them accountable to the project schedule and quality standards. When you act as your own GC, you sign individual contracts with each sub, which means you're managing those relationships, those payment schedules, and those quality disputes yourself.

For a single-trade job, hiring the sub directly makes sense. For a multi-trade remodel, the GC layer pays for itself in coordination and accountability.

## How do you decide? A quick framework

Ask yourself these four questions before deciding:

1. **Does this project require a permit?** If yes, lean toward a GC.
2. **Does it involve more than one licensed trade?** If yes, lean toward a GC.
3. **Does it touch structural framing, load-bearing walls, or the foundation?** If yes, hire a GC.
4. **Do I have construction experience and contractor relationships?** If no, the owner-builder path will cost you more in time, mistakes, and stress than the GC markup saves.

If you answered yes to any of the first three, a GC is the lower-risk path. If you answered yes to all four, the decision is straightforward.

For reference, a well-run kitchen remodel typically takes 6-12 weeks from permit to punch list. A bathroom addition runs 4-8 weeks. A basement finish can run 8-16 weeks depending on scope. These timelines assume a GC who is actively managing the schedule. Owner-builder timelines routinely run 30-50% longer on first attempts.

## What should you do next?

If your project scope is still taking shape, get a written scope-of-work before you decide anything. A good contractor will walk the project with you, identify every trade that needs to be involved, and give you a clear picture of what the permit process looks like in your jurisdiction. That conversation is free and will tell you more than any online estimate.

If you've already decided you want a GC, the next step is vetting them properly. Check that they're licensed and insured, ask for references on similar-scope projects, and get a contract that specifies the payment schedule, scope, and timeline in writing. Our post on [how to choose a general contractor](/blog/how-to-choose-a-general-contractor/) walks through the full checklist. And if you want to understand what the GC's fee actually covers, [how much does a general contractor cost](/blog/how-much-does-a-general-contractor-cost/) breaks down the markup structure and what you're paying for.

For projects in the planning stage, the earlier you bring in a GC, the better. They can flag permit requirements, identify structural issues, and give you a realistic budget before you've committed to a design that's expensive to build.
