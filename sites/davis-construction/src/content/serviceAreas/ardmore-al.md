## Restoration emergencies common in Ardmore

Ardmore sits in the part of the Tennessee Valley known for sudden, severe spring storms, straddling the Alabama-Tennessee line in rural Limestone County. Straight-line winds and tornado activity are the dominant risk here, and they bring the two problems we get called about most: roof and siding damage that lets water into the attic and wall cavities, and downed trees that puncture roofing or crush outbuildings. Because much of Ardmore is low-density and surrounded by farmland and pasture, drainage around older homes often relies on grading and ditches rather than municipal storm sewer, so heavy rain events can pool against foundations faster than they would in a denser subdivision. That combination, aging roofs plus marginal drainage, is why we see repeat water intrusion at the same properties after multiple storm seasons if the original repair didn't address the underlying grading or flashing issue.

## Building stock, site conditions, and permits in Ardmore

Much of Ardmore's housing stock is older rural construction: wood-frame farmhouses and ranch homes from the mid-20th century alongside a growing number of newer builds and manufactured homes. Crawlspace foundations are common, which means water intrusion from grading or gutter failure tends to show up as damp subfloor and musty crawlspace air before it's visible inside the house. Plumbing in the older homes is a mix of galvanized and copper supply lines, with cast iron or early plastic drain lines in some cases, so corrosion-related pinhole leaks are a realistic failure point we plan around during any reconstruction scope.

Limestone County's clay-heavy soil holds water and expands and contracts with seasonal moisture swings, which can shift crawlspace piers and stress foundation walls over time, something we check for whenever we're repairing water damage near a foundation.
