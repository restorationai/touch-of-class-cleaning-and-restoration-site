## Why Somerville Homes Often Need More Than a Surface Refresh

Morgan County's climate swings between humid summers and cold snaps that can push into the teens in January. That cycle of expansion and contraction works on wood framing, subflooring, and older plaster over years in ways that aren't always obvious until a wall comes down. Many homes in the Somerville area were built during the mid-century suburban push, when construction methods were solid but insulation standards were minimal and vapor barriers were often an afterthought.

The clay soils common across this part of the Tennessee Valley also shift with moisture levels, which means foundations move slightly with the seasons. Before any interior remodel touches load-bearing walls or floor systems, it pays to understand what the structure has been doing. Skipping that step leads to finish work that looks right for a year and then starts showing cracks where the framing has settled unevenly.

## Local Note: What Somerville's Clay Soil Means for Your Remodel

One thing that comes up repeatedly in this area: homes built on Morgan County's clay-heavy soil sometimes show interior door frames that have racked slightly out of square over the years, even when the foundation itself is in good shape. It's a slow process, and most homeowners don't notice it until they're replacing interior doors and find that nothing is plumb. During a whole-home remodel, it's worth taking the time to re-square door and window openings rather than simply trimming new units to fit the existing out-of-square rough openings. That extra step makes the finished work look right and keeps doors and windows operating smoothly for years instead of binding up after the first full weather cycle.
