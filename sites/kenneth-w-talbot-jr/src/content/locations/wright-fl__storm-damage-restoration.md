## Why Wright Properties See Distinctive Storm Damage

The Florida Panhandle's storm season is not a single-month event. Named hurricanes, fast-moving tropical storms, and isolated tornado touchdowns can all reach Okaloosa County from late spring through November, and winter nor'easters occasionally add wind and rain damage outside that window. Wright's position near the bay means properties absorb moisture from two directions during major events: rainfall driving in from above and bay surge or sheet flow pushing in from below.

The housing stock in this area includes a meaningful share of homes built in the 1960s through 1980s, when roof decking was typically thinner and fastener patterns did not meet current Florida Building Code wind-load requirements. When a storm peels back shingles or lifts a section of decking, the interior can take on water within minutes. Insulation batts in attic spaces absorb and hold that moisture long after the storm passes, and without proper extraction and drying, secondary mold growth can begin in as little as 24 to 48 hours in Panhandle humidity levels.

Large live oaks and slash pines throughout the area are a specific hazard. Root systems in the sandy, well-drained soils common to this part of Okaloosa County do not always anchor trees as deeply as clay-heavy soils further north, and saturated ground during a storm can tip trees that look healthy. A tree through a roof or exterior wall is a structural emergency that also opens the building to ongoing water intrusion until it is properly tarped and stabilized.

## Our Storm Damage Restoration Process in Wright

When we arrive, the first priority is stopping active water entry. That means emergency tarping of roof breaches, boarding compromised windows and doors, and assessing whether the structure is safe to enter. We document everything photographically before any material is moved or removed, which matters significantly when an insurance adjuster reviews your claim.

Once the building envelope is stabilized, we conduct moisture mapping using thermal imaging and calibrated meters to find water that has migrated into wall cavities, subfloor assemblies, and insulation. In older Panhandle construction, water frequently travels further than it appears on the surface. Walls that feel dry to the touch can be holding moisture behind plaster or behind original bead-board paneling.

Drying equipment, including commercial-grade dehumidifiers and air movers, is placed based on the moisture map rather than by guesswork. We monitor readings daily and adjust equipment placement until materials reach target drying goals. Debris from tree strikes or structural damage is removed and documented. Reconstruction coordination follows once the structure is dry and structurally sound.

## Local Note

One pattern we see repeatedly in Wright and the surrounding Okaloosa County communities is storm damage that looks minor from the street but is significantly worse in the attic. The combination of wind-driven rain, older roof underlayment that has dried and cracked over decades of Panhandle sun, and blown-out ridge vents means water can enter a roof system without leaving obvious ceiling stains right away. By the time a stain appears on a bedroom ceiling, the insulation above it has often been wet for days. If your roof took any visible wind damage in a storm, an attic inspection is worth doing before you assume the interior is fine.
