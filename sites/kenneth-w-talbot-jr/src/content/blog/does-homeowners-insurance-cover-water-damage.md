Whether homeowners insurance covers water damage depends almost entirely on *how* the water got in. Sudden, accidental damage, a pipe that bursts overnight, a washing machine supply line that fails, is typically covered under a standard HO-3 policy. Gradual damage, a slow drip under the sink that's been there for months, a roof that's been missing shingles since last hurricane season, usually is not. And flooding from outside your home, whether from storm surge, an overflowing river, or heavy rain sheeting across the yard, is almost never covered without a separate flood policy. That's the short answer. Here's how to apply it to your specific situation.

## The Coverage Divide That Trips Most Homeowners Up

Insurance adjusters are trained to look for one thing when a water claim comes in: the *origin event*. Was it sudden and accidental, or was it ongoing and preventable?

**Generally covered under a standard homeowners policy:**
- A supply line bursts behind your refrigerator while you're at work
- Your water heater ruptures without warning
- A pipe freezes and cracks during an unexpected cold snap
- An upstairs toilet overflows because a float valve fails
- Rain enters through a hole in your roof caused by a falling tree branch (the storm damage is covered; the resulting interior water damage usually is too)

**Generally NOT covered:**
- A slow leak under a sink that has been dripping for weeks or months, insurers call this "seepage" or "continuous leakage" and exclude it on the grounds that a reasonable homeowner would have caught it
- A roof that leaks because it's old and worn, not because of a specific storm event
- Water that backs up through a floor drain or sewer line (this requires a separate "water backup" endorsement, which many policies offer as an add-on)
- Any water that enters from outside the structure, storm surge, rising groundwater, overland flooding

Along the Gulf Coast in the Florida Panhandle, that last category is where homeowners get caught off guard. Standard homeowners policies written in this region almost universally exclude flood damage, and given the area's low elevation and proximity to Choctawhatchee Bay and the Gulf, a named storm can push water into homes that have never flooded before. If you don't have a separate NFIP (National Flood Insurance Program) policy or a private flood policy, that water is your expense.

## How to Read Your Own Policy Before You File a Claim

Before you call your agent, pull out your declarations page and look for two sections: **Coverage A** (dwelling) and the **exclusions section**. The exclusions section is where you'll find the language about seepage, groundwater, and flood. It reads like legal boilerplate, but the words that matter are usually plain enough: "continuous or repeated seepage," "surface water," "overflow of a body of water."

Also look for these optional endorsements, which not all policies include by default:

- **Water backup and sump overflow**, covers damage from a backed-up drain or sump pump failure
- **Service line coverage**, covers breaks in underground pipes between the street and your home
- **Equipment breakdown**, can cover sudden failure of appliances that cause water damage

If you're not sure what your policy includes, call your agent and ask specifically: *"If a pipe bursts inside my wall tonight, what's covered and what's my deductible?"* Get the answer in writing, or at least take notes with a date and the agent's name.

## What to Do in the First Hour After Water Damage

If you're dealing with active water damage right now, coverage questions can wait five minutes. Do these first:

1. **Shut off the water source.** If it's a burst pipe or appliance line, find the shutoff valve for that fixture. If you can't locate it, go to the main shutoff, usually near the water meter at the street or at the point where the main line enters the house.
2. **Cut power to affected areas if water is near outlets, panels, or appliances.** Don't step into standing water in a room where electricity may be active.
3. **Document everything before you move anything.** Take video, walk the room slowly, narrate what you see, open cabinet doors, show the ceiling, show the floor. Your claim will be stronger for it.
4. **Call your insurance company to open a claim.** Most carriers have 24-hour claim lines. Opening a claim doesn't commit you to anything, but waiting can give the adjuster reason to question the timeline.
5. **Begin basic mitigation if you can do it safely.** Move wet rugs and furniture off wet flooring. Use towels or a wet-vac on standing water. Open windows if outdoor humidity is lower than indoor. Do not run fans into a space that may have mold already, that spreads spores.

One thing homeowners often don't realize: your policy likely includes a **duty to mitigate** clause. That means you're expected to take reasonable steps to prevent further damage after a loss. If you leave soaked drywall sitting for a week before calling anyone, the adjuster may argue that secondary damage, mold, structural deterioration, resulted from your inaction, not the original event.

## What NOT to Do When Filing a Water Damage Claim

- **Don't throw anything away before the adjuster sees it.** Damaged flooring, drywall, cabinetry, all of it is evidence of the loss. Photograph it, then leave it until the adjuster has visited or explicitly told you it's okay to remove.
- **Don't sign a contractor's authorization to bill your insurance directly without reading it carefully.** Assignment-of-benefits arrangements can complicate your claim and, in some cases, limit your control over the repair process.
- **Don't assume the adjuster's estimate is final.** You have the right to get your own contractor's estimate and to dispute the adjuster's scope. A public adjuster or an attorney who handles insurance disputes can help if the gap is significant.
- **Don't delay.** Most policies require you to report a loss "promptly" or within a specific number of days. Check your policy for that language.

## When the Damage Is Bigger Than It Looks

Water is deceptive. A half-inch of standing water in a kitchen can mean saturated subfloor, wet insulation in the crawl space below, and moisture wicking up into wall cavities, none of which is visible once the surface dries. In older homes along the Panhandle, where pier-and-beam construction is common, water under the floor can sit against wood framing for days before anyone notices the smell.

Mold can begin colonizing wet organic material, drywall paper, wood framing, insulation, within 24 to 48 hours under the right temperature and humidity conditions. Northwest Florida's warm, humid summers create exactly those conditions. By the time you see discoloration on a baseboard or smell that earthy, musty odor, the colony is already established.

This is where professional water damage restoration becomes less optional. Restoration technicians use moisture meters and thermal imaging cameras to find water that surface drying misses. Industrial drying equipment, high-capacity dehumidifiers and air movers, can reduce drying time significantly compared to household fans, which matters both for preventing mold and for satisfying your insurer's documentation requirements.
