The first 24 hours after water damage are the most consequential ones. Stop the water source, get people and pets out of standing water, document everything with your phone, and begin moving air through the space. What you do, and don't do, in the next few hours determines whether you're dealing with a drying job or a mold remediation project weeks from now.

## Stop the Water First

Before anything else, cut off the source. If a pipe burst or a supply line failed, find your main water shutoff, in most Florida homes it's near the meter at the street or along an exterior wall near the garage, and turn it off completely. If the water is coming from a roof leak or storm intrusion, you can't stop the rain, but you can move valuables and use buckets or towels to slow the spread.

If there's any chance the water has reached an electrical panel, outlets, or appliances, don't wade into it. Shut off the circuit breaker for the affected area first, or call your utility company if you're not sure how. Saltwater and gray water (from overflowing toilets or backed-up drains) carry additional hazards, treat those as contaminated and avoid skin contact.

Once the source is controlled, open windows and interior doors to start moving air. If you have box fans, point them across wet surfaces, not directly at walls, which can trap moisture behind drywall. Ceiling fans help too. The goal right now is evaporation, not perfection.

## Document Before You Touch Anything

This step costs you nothing and protects your insurance claim. Walk through the affected area with your phone and record a video, narrate what you see, including the water source, the affected rooms, and any damaged belongings. Then take still photos of:

- The water source or point of entry
- Standing water depth (a ruler or pen in the frame gives scale)
- Wet flooring, baseboards, and walls
- Damaged furniture, electronics, and personal property
- Any visible mold, staining, or swelling

Don't move or throw away damaged items until you've photographed them. Insurance adjusters need to see the loss in place. After documentation, you can move salvageable belongings to a dry area, but keep a written list of everything you relocate.

Call your insurance company as soon as it's safe to do so. Most homeowner policies cover sudden and accidental water damage (a burst pipe, an appliance failure) but not gradual leaks that went unaddressed. The sooner you report, the sooner your claim clock starts.

## What Not to Do in the First Few Hours

Some instincts that feel helpful can make the situation worse or complicate your claim.

**Don't use a household vacuum or shop vac on standing water unless it's rated for wet use.** A standard shop vac can handle small puddles, but it won't move enough volume to matter in a flooded room, and running it continuously can burn out the motor.

**Don't run the HVAC system if ducts run through the wet area.** Pulling air through wet ductwork spreads moisture and can seed mold spores into parts of the house that weren't affected.

**Don't peel up flooring or open walls yet.** It feels productive, but disturbing materials before a professional can assess moisture readings can destroy the documentation trail your insurer needs. Let the adjuster or a restoration technician guide demolition.

**Don't use heat to dry things faster.** Closing windows and running the heat might seem logical, but high heat without airflow traps humidity. The goal is air movement and dehumidification, not just warmth.

**Don't assume it's dry because the surface feels dry.** Water travels. It wicks up drywall, migrates under hardwood, and pools inside wall cavities. A surface that feels dry to the touch can have a moisture content three or four times the acceptable threshold when measured with a meter.

## When to Call a Professional

Some water damage situations are genuinely manageable with fans, towels, and time, a small appliance leak caught immediately, a minor roof drip that soaked one ceiling tile. Many are not.

Call a water damage restoration professional if any of the following apply:

- The water source was a toilet, sewage line, or backed-up drain (this is category 3 water, contaminated, and not safe to handle without protective equipment)
- The affected area is larger than roughly 10 square feet of flooring or one full wall
- The water sat for more than a few hours before you found it
- You can smell a musty or earthy odor, mold can begin colonizing porous materials within 24 to 48 hours in Florida's humidity
- The water reached insulation, which holds moisture long after surrounding materials appear dry
- You have a crawl space, and water has entered it
- You're not sure where the water went

Professional restoration crews use thermal imaging cameras and calibrated moisture meters to find water that isn't visible. They set industrial air movers and commercial dehumidifiers that pull significantly more moisture than household equipment. ## The Longer Recovery: What Comes After the First Day

Once the immediate crisis is stabilized, the real drying process begins, and it takes longer than most homeowners expect. Structural drying typically runs several days, depending on the materials involved, the volume of water, and how quickly mitigation started. Hardwood floors, plaster walls, and concrete slabs hold moisture differently than drywall and carpet, and each requires its own drying approach.

Expect a professional crew to:

1. Take baseline moisture readings throughout the affected area
2. Place air movers and dehumidifiers in a calculated pattern
3. Return daily to take readings and adjust equipment
4. Remove equipment only when materials reach target moisture levels, not just when things look dry

After drying is confirmed, any materials that couldn't be saved, saturated drywall, buckled flooring, compromised insulation, are removed and the reconstruction phase begins. That's a separate scope of work from mitigation, and it's worth understanding the difference when you're talking to your insurance adjuster.

Keep all receipts for anything you spend during the emergency phase: fans, dehumidifiers, hotel stays if the home is uninhabitable, replacement items for necessities. These costs may be reimbursable under your policy's additional living expenses provision.
