## What emergency water removal & cleanup actually involves

Emergency water removal is the physical extraction of standing or trapped water, followed by the structural drying that prevents a wet building from becoming a moldy one. This isn't a wet-vac and a box fan. A real extraction uses truck-mounted or portable extraction units rated for continuous pull on carpet, pad, hardwood, and tile. Depending on the source, category water classification matters too: clean water from a supply line behaves very differently than water that's backed up through a drain or sat long enough to turn contaminated. Equipment on a typical job includes submersible pumps for standing water over an inch or two, extraction wands for carpet and pad, and in many cases, pulling carpet pad entirely since pad almost never dries fast enough to prevent odor and mold. After extraction comes structural drying: air movers positioned to create airflow across wet surfaces, and dehumidifiers sized to the cubic footage of the affected area, not just dropped in a corner. Drying timelines vary by material. Drywall and exposed studs can dry in a few days under the right airflow. Hardwood and subfloor under tile take considerably longer and sometimes require specialty drying mats or injection systems to pull moisture out of the assembly without tearing it apart.

## Our process

1. **Source control and standing water extraction.** Before anything else, the water source gets identified and stopped if it hasn't been already, then standing water is pumped or extracted out using truck-mount or portable units.
2. **Moisture mapping.** Moisture meters and sometimes thermal imaging get used to find where water has traveled, under flooring, behind baseboards, inside wall cavities, so nothing gets missed because it looks dry on the surface.
3. **Material assessment and removal where needed.** Saturated carpet pad, swollen baseboards, or drywall that's wicked water past a salvageable point get removed so drying equipment isn't fighting a losing battle against materials that should come out.
4. **Structural drying and daily monitoring.** Air movers and dehumidifiers get placed based on the moisture map, then checked and logged daily, adjusting equipment as materials dry at different rates.
5. **Containment for Category 2 or 3 water.** If the water is contaminated, from a sewage backup or water that's been standing long enough to turn, affected materials get contained and handled separately from the clean-water portions of the job.

## What separates a good water removal response from a bad one

The biggest mistake in water cleanup is treating it like a surface problem. A crew that pulls visible standing water, runs a couple of fans overnight, and calls it done is leaving moisture behind walls and under flooring that will show up as a mold problem in two to three weeks. Moisture meters should be used on every affected wall and floor, not just the areas that look wet. Carpet pad that's been saturated for more than a few hours should come out, drying pad in place almost never works and traps moisture against the subfloor. Adjusters reviewing a water claim look for documented moisture readings over time, not just a single before-and-after photo, because that log is what proves the structure was actually dried to standard rather than just dried to the point the equipment got picked up. Good operators also distinguish category of water correctly from the start, since treating contaminated water like clean water is both a health issue and a documentation gap that can come back on a claim.
