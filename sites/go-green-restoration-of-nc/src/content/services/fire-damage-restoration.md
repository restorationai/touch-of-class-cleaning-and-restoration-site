Smoke doesn't stop at the burn line. After a house fire, soot travels through HVAC ducts, settles into closets two rooms away, and embeds in porous surfaces within hours. The smell you notice a week later — that sharp, oily residue clinging to walls and fabric — is protein or synthetic soot continuing to off-gas and etch finishes. Fire damage restoration isn't just cleaning what burned; it's tracing everywhere combustion byproducts traveled and reversing the damage before it becomes permanent.

## What fire damage restoration actually involves

The visible char is usually the smallest part of the problem. Soot particles — some as fine as 0.1 microns — coat surfaces, infiltrate wall cavities, and migrate through air pressure differentials created when a structure cools after a fire. Depending on what burned, you may be dealing with wet soot (from slow, smoldering combustion), dry soot (fast, high-temperature fires), or protein residue (from kitchen fires — nearly invisible but intensely pungent and notoriously difficult to remove from painted surfaces).

Beyond soot, fire damage restoration typically involves:

- **Structural assessment** — identifying compromised framing, subfloor, and load-bearing elements before any cleaning begins
- **Contents evaluation and pack-out** — determining what can be restored off-site versus what must be documented as a total loss
- **Odor neutralization** — thermal fogging and ozone treatment to break down odor molecules embedded in materials that surface cleaning cannot reach
- **Water damage mitigation** — suppression water from firefighting sits in walls and under flooring and must be extracted and dried before reconstruction begins

Timelines vary with the size and intensity of the fire. A contained kitchen fire may resolve in days; a fire that reached the attic or spread through multiple rooms typically requires weeks of coordinated mitigation, contents handling, and reconstruction.

## Our process

**1. Emergency stabilization and safety assessment**
Before any cleaning equipment enters the structure, the building is evaluated for structural integrity, active utility hazards, and air quality. Utilities may need to be isolated. Areas with compromised framing or roofing are documented and secured against weather intrusion — a tarp on an open roof in the first 24 hours prevents a secondary water loss that can dwarf the original fire damage.

**2. Soot characterization and containment**
Not all soot responds to the same cleaning chemistry. Dry soot brushes off porous surfaces but smears if wiped with the wrong technique. Protein soot requires enzymatic or alkaline cleaners and multiple passes. Synthetic soot from burning plastics and foam contains compounds that can permanently stain if treated incorrectly. Affected zones are contained to prevent cross-contamination to unaffected areas during cleaning.

**3. Structural and surface cleaning**
Ceilings first, then walls, then floors — always working top-down so dislodged residue doesn't re-contaminate cleaned surfaces. HEPA-filtered vacuums remove loose soot before any wet cleaning begins. Ductwork is cleaned or sealed to prevent redistributing soot through the HVAC system when heat or air conditioning is restored.

**4. Thermal fogging and ozone treatment**
Surface cleaning removes visible soot but rarely eliminates odor entirely. Thermal fogging disperses a deodorizing agent in particle sizes that mimic smoke, reaching the same cavities and porous materials the smoke penetrated. Ozone treatment — applied only after occupants and pets have vacated — oxidizes odor molecules at the molecular level. These steps are sequenced, not interchangeable.

**5. Reconstruction scope and rebuild**
Once the structure is clean, dry, and odor-free, a written reconstruction scope documents every material to be replaced — drywall, insulation, flooring, cabinetry, trim — with line-item detail that supports the insurance claim. Reconstruction proceeds from framing outward, with inspections at each stage.

## What separates a good fire damage response from a bad one

The most common mistake is starting reconstruction before the structure is fully dry. Suppression water trapped behind freshly installed drywall creates the conditions for mold colonization within days. A thorough moisture survey — using thermal imaging and pin-type meters in wall cavities, not just surface readings — is non-negotiable before any rebuild begins.

A second frequent failure is incomplete soot mapping. Smoke follows air currents, and in a forced-air home, those currents reach every room with a supply or return vent. Limiting cleaning to the "fire room" and adjacent spaces routinely leaves protein or synthetic soot in bedrooms and closets that resurfaces as odor months later.

Insurance adjusters look for itemized documentation: photographs of affected materials before removal, moisture readings with timestamps and meter calibration records, and a contents inventory that distinguishes restorable items from total losses. Thorough documentation protects the homeowner's claim and avoids disputes over scope during the settlement process.
