## What Commercial Restoration actually involves

Commercial restoration covers the same core disciplines as residential work, water extraction, fire and smoke cleanup, structural drying, but scaled and sequenced around a building that still needs to function. That might mean phasing drying equipment through one wing of an office while staff keep working in another, coordinating after-hours access with a tenant's security desk, or scheduling demo and rebuild around a retailer's open hours. Commercial properties also bring different materials into play: suspended ceiling grids, VCT and sheet vinyl flooring, HVAC systems that can spread smoke or moisture odor building-wide, and multi-tenant wiring and plumbing that complicates source identification. Timelines run from a few days for a contained office flood to several weeks for a fire loss requiring demolition, permitting, and full interior rebuild.

## Our process

1. **Site assessment and stakeholder coordination.** We walk the affected space with the property manager or business owner, identify the source, and map out which areas can stay operational versus which need to be closed off.
2. **Extraction and emergency mitigation.** Standing water, fire suppression runoff, or soot-laden debris gets removed first to stop secondary damage, water intrusion into wall cavities, smoke odor absorbing deeper into ceiling tile and carpet, from spreading.
3. **Containment and drying or soot removal.** We set up physical barriers to keep dust, moisture, and odor out of occupied areas, then run commercial-grade dehumidifiers and air movers or begin soot and residue cleanup depending on the loss type.
4. **Daily monitoring and documentation.** Moisture readings, photos, and progress notes get logged throughout the job, both to confirm materials are drying correctly and to build the paper trail commercial adjusters expect.
5. **Reconstruction and reopening.** Once the space is dry or clean, we scope repairs, pull any required permits, and rebuild to get the business back to a usable, code-compliant condition.

## What separates a good commercial restoration response from a bad one

The most common mistake on commercial jobs is treating them like a scaled-up residential loss. A less experienced crew will dry a space without accounting for what's above the ceiling grid or behind a chase wall where a second tenant's plumbing runs, and the moisture resurfaces weeks later as a mold callback. Another frequent miss is poor containment, letting dust or odor drift into areas where customers or employees are still working, which can trigger complaints or even temporary closure orders. Adjusters reviewing commercial claims look for daily moisture logs, clear photo documentation of pre-existing conditions versus new damage, and a scope of work that separates mitigation costs from reconstruction costs. Jobs that get flagged for additional review are usually ones missing that paper trail, not ones where the drying or cleanup itself was done poorly.
