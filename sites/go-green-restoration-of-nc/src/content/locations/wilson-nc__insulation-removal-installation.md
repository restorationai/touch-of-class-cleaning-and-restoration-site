## Why Wilson Properties See Insulation Problems More Than Most

Wilson sits in a humid subtropical climate where summer dew points regularly climb above 70°F. That moisture doesn't just stay outside. In crawl spaces beneath the older homes in West Nash and similar established neighborhoods, vapor migrates upward through unencapsulated dirt floors and settles into insulation that was never designed to handle sustained humidity. Over time, batts sag and fall away from floor joists entirely, leaving conditioned air to bleed directly into unconditioned space.

Attics face a different version of the same problem. Wilson's summer heat loads are intense — attic temperatures routinely exceed 130°F from June through September — and older blown-in insulation settles and compacts under that thermal cycling. ## Our Insulation Removal & Installation Process in Wilson

Every project starts with a full assessment — attic, crawl space, and any accessible wall cavities — before a single bag of old material is pulled. In Wilson's older homes, that inspection sometimes turns up surprises: vermiculite insulation from pre-1980 renovations, knob-and-tube wiring that prohibits direct insulation contact, or vapor barriers that were installed backward and have been trapping moisture against the subfloor for years.

Once the scope is clear, removal comes first. For attics, high-powered vacuum extraction pulls out old blown-in material without spreading debris through living spaces. Crawl space work involves hand-removal of deteriorated batts, disposal of any compromised vapor barrier, and a thorough inspection of the joist bays before new material goes in. We don't skip the prep — installing new insulation over compromised substrate just restarts the clock on the same problem.

Installation is matched to the space and the home's age. Crawl spaces in Wilson's humid climate almost always benefit from a combination of encapsulation and rigid foam or spray foam at the rim joists rather than fiberglass batts between the joists, which continue to absorb moisture. Attics typically receive blown-in fiberglass or cellulose brought up to current North Carolina energy code minimums — R-38 to R-60 depending on the zone and the home's existing air sealing condition.
