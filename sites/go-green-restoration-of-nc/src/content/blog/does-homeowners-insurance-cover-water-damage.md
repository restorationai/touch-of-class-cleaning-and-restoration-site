Homeowners insurance *usually* covers water damage — but only when the water arrived suddenly and accidentally. A burst pipe at 2 a.m.? Covered by most standard HO-3 policies. A slow drip under the sink that rotted the cabinet floor over six months? Almost certainly not. The distinction that matters to adjusters is **sudden and accidental vs. gradual and preventable**. Understanding that line before you file a claim can save you from a denial — and from accidentally admitting something that hurts your case.

---

## What Standard Homeowners Policies Typically Cover

Most HO-3 policies (the most common type sold in North Carolina) cover **sudden, accidental discharge or overflow** of water from within the home. That includes:

- A pipe that freezes and bursts during a cold snap
- A washing machine supply hose that fails without warning
- An ice dam that forces water under the roof shingles and into the attic
- An accidental overflow from a bathtub or toilet
- A dishwasher that leaks because an internal component failed

The key word in every one of those examples is *sudden*. The damage happened in a moment — or at least within a single event — rather than accumulating quietly over weeks.

One thing many homeowners in the Middlesex area discover too late: **flood damage from outside the home is not covered by a standard homeowners policy**. If a heavy rain event or a rising creek sends water through your foundation or under your doors, that loss falls under a separate flood insurance policy, typically through FEMA's National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) or a private flood carrier. If you live in a low-lying area of Johnston or Nash County, it's worth a conversation with your insurance agent before storm season.

## What Policies Almost Always Exclude

Insurance companies deny more water damage claims on the basis of **maintenance exclusions** than almost any other reason. The logic from the insurer's perspective: if the damage built up slowly, a reasonable homeowner would have noticed and fixed it. Common exclusions include:

- **Continuous or repeated seepage** — a slow drip from a corroded supply line, a toilet flapper that's been running for months
- **Lack of maintenance** — a roof that was known to be failing, caulk around a tub that was visibly cracked
- **Mold resulting from a long-standing moisture problem** — even if the original water source was covered, if you waited and mold colonized the wall cavity, the mold remediation cost may be excluded or subject to a separate, lower sublimit
- **Sewer or drain backup** — this one surprises people. If your floor drain backs up during a heavy rain and floods your basement, that is typically *not* covered under a standard policy. You need a sewer backup endorsement, which is usually inexpensive to add.
- **Flood from outside the structure** — as noted above, this requires separate flood coverage

A practical note on mold: in a humid North Carolina summer, mold can begin colonizing wet drywall and insulation within 24 to 48 hours of a water event. That's not a scare tactic — it's the reason your insurer's adjuster will want to know the exact date the damage occurred. The longer the gap between the event and the claim, the harder it becomes to argue the damage was sudden.

## How to Document a Claim the Right Way

If you've just discovered water damage and you think it's covered, the steps you take in the next few hours matter a great deal.

1. **Stop the water source first.** Turn off the supply valve under the fixture, or if you can't isolate it, shut off the main water supply to the house. Every minute of additional flow is additional damage.
2. **Photograph everything before you touch it.** Walk through with your phone and shoot wide shots showing the full room, then close-ups of the water source, the affected materials, and any visible damage. Don't move furniture or pull up flooring yet.
3. **Call your insurance company to open a claim.** You don't need to know the full scope of damage to open a claim — you just need to report the event. Get a claim number.
4. **Document the timeline in writing.** A simple note with the date, time, and what you observed when you first discovered the damage can be important if the insurer questions whether the loss was sudden.
5. **Mitigate — but document as you go.** Most policies require you to take reasonable steps to prevent further damage (moving belongings out of standing water, extracting water if you can). But photograph each step before you do it.
6. **Keep every receipt.** Emergency fans, hotel stays if the home is uninhabitable, replacement materials — all of it may be reimbursable.

One thing to avoid: **don't start major demolition before an adjuster has seen the damage**. Pulling out wet drywall before the adjuster visits can make it harder to prove the scope of the loss. A professional restoration company can place industrial drying equipment and begin moisture mapping while the structure is still intact — that documentation actually *helps* your claim.

## When the Damage Is Bigger Than a Shop Vac Can Handle

A towel and a box fan are fine for a minor splash. But if water has been sitting for more than an hour, if it has wicked into drywall or subfloor, or if you can smell anything musty within the first day, the moisture has already moved deeper than the surface you can see.

Professional water damage restoration involves moisture meters and thermal imaging cameras that map exactly how far the water has traveled — behind baseboards, under hardwood, inside wall cavities. Without that mapping, it's easy to dry the surface while leaving wet material inside a wall that will grow mold within the week.

## The Longer Recovery: What Comes After Drying

Drying is the beginning, not the end. Once moisture readings return to normal for your region's climate baseline, the structural and cosmetic repairs begin: replacing drywall, repainting, reinstalling flooring, addressing any cabinetry. If the event was significant, a full reconstruction scope may be needed.

Your insurance company will typically issue payment in two stages: **actual cash value (ACV)** upfront, which reflects the depreciated value of damaged materials, and then a **recoverable depreciation** payment after repairs are completed and you submit receipts. Read your policy to understand whether you have ACV-only coverage or replacement cost value (RCV) coverage — the difference can be thousands of dollars on a major loss.

Keep a dedicated folder — physical or digital — for every document related to the claim: the adjuster's estimate, every contractor invoice, every photo, every email. Insurance claims can take weeks to fully settle, and having an organized paper trail makes every conversation easier.
