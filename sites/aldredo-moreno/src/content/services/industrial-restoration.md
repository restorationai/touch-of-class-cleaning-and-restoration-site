## What industrial restoration actually involves

Industrial water damage restoration is a different scale of problem than a flooded bedroom. A 40,000 square foot warehouse with water sheeting across a sealed concrete slab needs truck-mount extraction units and submersible pumps sized for the volume, not a handful of shop vacs. Facilities with epoxy-coated floors, raised electrical panels, or sensitive machinery require moisture readings taken at multiple depths, because water can travel under a coated slab and sit there long after the surface looks dry. Manufacturing plant restoration also means working around production schedules, lockout/tagout procedures, and inventory that can't simply be moved to a dry corner. Timelines run longer than a residential job, often several days of monitored drying rather than a single visit, because the structure, the slab, and the stored product all have to come back into an acceptable moisture range before the space is safe to reopen.

## Our process

1. **Facility assessment and water categorization.** We walk the affected zones, identify the source, and determine whether the water is clean, gray, or contaminated, which matters more in a plant with floor drains, process chemicals, or sewage lines than it does in a house.
2. **Large-volume extraction.** High-capacity pumps and truck-mount extraction clear standing water from warehouse floors, loading docks, and lower-level mechanical rooms before it migrates further into racking, insulation, or subflooring.
3. **Industrial-scale drying and moisture mapping.** Desiccant dehumidifiers and commercial airmovers are positioned for high ceilings and open floor plans, and we map moisture under epoxy coatings, slabs, and wall assemblies rather than relying on a single surface reading.
4. **Containment around active operations.** Where production has to continue, we set up containment barriers so drying equipment can run in the affected zone without shutting down the whole facility.
5. **Monitoring, documentation, and sign-off.** Moisture levels are tracked and logged daily, dried per the IICRC S500 structural drying standard, with the documentation your insurance carrier or business interruption claim will need.

## What separates a good industrial restoration response from a bad one

The most common mistake on industrial jobs is undersized equipment. A warehouse floor that looks dry to the touch can still be holding moisture under sealed concrete or inside wall cavities, and a crew that doesn't map moisture at depth will sign off on a space that isn't actually dry, which shows up weeks later as a secondary problem. Another frequent miss is treating all water as the same category. A floor drain backup in a production area can carry contamination that clean groundwater intrusion doesn't, and skipping that distinction changes how materials should be handled. Adjusters reviewing industrial claims also look for daily moisture logs tied to specific readings, not just a start and end date, and for clear documentation of what was affected, what was extracted, and what equipment ran where. Facilities that coordinate drying schedules with production downtime, rather than treating the whole plant as one zone, generally get back to full operation faster and with a cleaner paper trail for business interruption coverage.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

West Texas swings hard between extremes, and both ends of that swing put industrial facilities at risk. Summer monsoon bursts can dump water fast enough to overwhelm loading dock drainage and push stormwater under roll-up doors before anyone notices. Winter cold snaps, even short ones, can freeze exposed supply lines or sprinkler risers in unheated warehouse sections, and a split pipe in a high-bay space can release a large volume of water before it's caught. Facilities with flat roofs and large footprints are also more exposed to ponding after heavy rain, which can work its way through roof penetrations over time.
