A storm doesn't give you a warning before the roof decking splits or the window fails at 3 a.m. What it leaves behind, standing water mixing with insulation debris, saturated drywall that looks intact from the surface, roof sheathing exposed to a second round of rain before anyone's had a chance to tarp it, creates a damage timeline that compounds fast. The window between a manageable repair and a full structural remediation is often measured in hours, not days.

## What Storm Damage Restoration actually involves

Storm damage is rarely one problem. A single severe weather event can combine wind-driven rain intrusion, impact damage from hail or falling trees, structural displacement, and secondary water infiltration, all in the same structure. Each of those failure modes requires a different response.

Wind damage opens the envelope of a building: shingles lift, ridge caps blow off, fascia separates, and suddenly every subsequent rain event is an interior water event. Hail can fracture roof membrane and siding in ways that aren't visible from the ground but show up clearly on thermal imaging. Tree strikes are the most dramatic, a limb through a roof introduces not just structural compromise but immediate weather exposure and, within 24 to 48 hours in West Texas summer heat, the beginning of moisture-driven microbial activity in whatever materials got wet.

Restoration work here means emergency stabilization first (tarping, board-up, debris removal from the structure), followed by moisture mapping of every affected interior cavity, then coordinated repair and reconstruction scoped to what the structure actually needs, not just what's visible.

## Our process

1. **Emergency stabilization and weather protection.** Before any assessment can be meaningful, the structure has to stop getting worse. That means tarping compromised roof sections, boarding broken windows and doors, and removing debris that's creating additional load or blocking drainage. This step happens the same day, regardless of what the repair scope will eventually look like.

2. **Damage documentation and moisture mapping.** Once the structure is stabilized, the full scope of damage gets documented, photographs, measurements, and moisture readings across every affected surface and cavity. Thermal imaging identifies water that has migrated behind walls or under flooring and isn't visible at the surface. This documentation is also the foundation of the insurance claim.

3. **Water extraction and structural drying.** Any storm-driven water intrusion gets extracted and the affected materials are put into an active drying protocol. In Midland's arid climate, ambient conditions can create a false sense of security, surface materials dry quickly while framing members and wall cavities stay elevated. Drying is monitored with calibrated meters until readings return to acceptable baselines for the material type, not just until things feel dry.

4. **Debris removal, tree damage cleanup, and structural assessment.** Fallen limbs and impact debris are removed, and any structural members that took direct hits are assessed for load-bearing integrity before reconstruction begins. A tree through a rafter isn't just a hole in the roof, it can transfer lateral load in ways that affect adjacent framing.

5. **Repair and reconstruction.** With the structure dried, documented, and assessed, the repair scope is written and work begins, roofing, siding, framing, drywall, insulation, and finish work as the loss requires. The goal is a structure that is restored to pre-loss condition, not patched to close.

## What separates a good storm damage response from a bad one

The most common failure in hurricane damage cleanup and tornado damage cleanup is treating the visible damage as the whole damage. A crew that patches the roof and calls it done may be leaving saturated wall cavities that will grow mold within two weeks. Insurance adjusters increasingly look for moisture logs and drying records, not just repair invoices, because carriers have learned that undocumented drying leads to reopened claims.

A second common problem is inadequate emergency tarping. A tarp that isn't properly secured or doesn't extend far enough past the damaged area allows wind-driven rain to continue entering at the edges. The repair cost for a second intrusion event is often higher than the original loss.

Good storm cleanup services also distinguish between cosmetic hail damage and functional damage, a distinction that matters both for the repair scope and for what a carrier will pay. Surface denting on metal trim is different from hail that has fractured the granule layer on shingles and compromised the waterproofing membrane.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Midland and the Permian Basin sit in a region where severe weather arrives fast and without much lead time. Spring and early summer bring the highest risk of large hail and straight-line wind events, storms that can exceed 70 mph and produce hailstones large enough to puncture roof decking. Late summer occasionally brings the remnants of Gulf systems that push heavy rainfall into an area where the caliche-heavy soil doesn't absorb water quickly, leading to rapid surface flooding and wind-driven rain intrusion even without a direct hit.

Because Midland's climate is arid the rest of the year, property owners sometimes underestimate how much moisture a single storm event can drive into a structure. The dry ambient air accelerates surface drying while cavities stay wet, a pattern that makes professional moisture monitoring more important here, not less.
