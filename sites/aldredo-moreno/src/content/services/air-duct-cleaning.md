When you run your HVAC system and notice a faint musty smell that disappears after a few minutes, or when family members with allergies seem worse indoors than out, the ductwork is often the overlooked culprit. Dust, pollen, pet dander, construction debris, and in some cases mold spores accumulate inside supply and return ducts over years, and every time the system kicks on, that material circulates through every room. Professional air duct cleaning removes what no filter catches once it's already past the grille.

## What air duct cleaning actually involves

Duct cleaning is not a shop-vac and a brush. A proper job uses truck-mounted or high-powered portable negative-air machines, typically rated at 4,000 to 16,000 CFM, to place the entire duct system under continuous negative pressure while agitation tools (rotary brushes, compressed-air whips, and skipper balls) dislodge debris from duct walls and drive it toward a HEPA-filtered collection unit. The process covers supply ducts, return ducts, the air handler cabinet, blower assembly, evaporator coil housing, and all accessible registers and grilles.

A full residential cleaning on a single-system home typically takes three to five hours. Larger homes with zoned systems or extensive ductwork runs take longer. At the end, registers are reseated, the system is restarted, and airflow is checked at each vent to confirm nothing was inadvertently blocked during the process.

Dryer vent cleaning is a related but separate scope, lint accumulation inside the exhaust duct is a leading cause of residential fires and is often added on when a technician is already on-site.

## Our process

1. **System inspection and photo documentation.** Before any equipment is connected, a technician inspects accessible ductwork, photographs register conditions, notes any visible mold growth, pest evidence, or damaged flex duct, and reviews the system layout. This step determines which agitation tools are appropriate and whether sanitization is warranted.

2. **Negative pressure setup.** The main collection unit is connected to the largest accessible duct opening, typically a return plenum or a supply trunk, and the system is brought under negative pressure. All registers are sealed with foam plugs or magnetic covers so suction is concentrated through the active work zone rather than bleeding off at every grille.

3. **Zone-by-zone agitation.** Working from the farthest supply runs back toward the air handler, technicians open one register at a time, insert the appropriate agitation tool, and drive loosened debris toward the collection unit. Rotary brushes are used on rigid metal duct; softer air-whip tools are used on flex duct to avoid tearing the inner liner.

4. **Air handler and coil cabinet cleaning.** The blower wheel, blower housing, and evaporator coil access area are vacuumed and wiped. Debris that bypasses the filter and coats the blower wheel is a common source of odor and reduced airflow that duct cleaning alone does not address.

5. **Sanitization (when indicated) and final inspection.** If microbial growth is confirmed or suspected, an EPA-registered sanitizing agent is fogged or sprayed into the duct system after cleaning. Registers are reinstalled, the system is powered on, and airflow is verified at each supply and return before the technician leaves.

## What separates a good duct cleaning from a bad one

The most common shortcut is skipping negative pressure entirely, a technician with a portable vacuum and a rotating brush can dislodge debris without ever capturing it, which means loosened dust and allergens end up in the living space. Legitimate duct cleaning always uses a collection unit powerful enough to keep the system under negative pressure throughout the job.

A second frequent miss is stopping at the ducts and ignoring the air handler. Dirty blower wheels and coil surfaces recontaminate clean ductwork within a few weeks. Any scope that does not include the air handler cabinet is incomplete.

For homes with suspected mold growth inside ducts, the correct sequence is cleaning first, then sanitization, not sanitization sprayed over a layer of settled debris. Coating debris with a sanitizer does not remove it; the underlying material remains and can continue to support microbial growth.

Finally, post-cleaning airflow verification matters. Improperly reseated flex duct connections or a register left partially blocked will reduce system efficiency and can cause pressure imbalances that shorten equipment life.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Midland and the Permian Basin run HVAC systems hard. Triple-digit summers mean air conditioners cycle almost continuously from May through September, and the region's fine caliche dust and blowing sand work their way past filters and into duct systems faster than in more temperate climates. Homes built during the oil-boom construction periods often have older galvanized or fiberglass-lined ductwork that holds debris differently than modern sheet metal. If your system has not been cleaned since a renovation, drywall dust is particularly dense and coats duct walls quickly, or since you moved into the home, the interior condition is worth inspecting regardless of the season.
