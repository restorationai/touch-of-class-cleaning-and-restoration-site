When sewage backs up into a bathroom, laundry room, or basement, the clock starts immediately. Raw sewage carries bacteria, viruses, and parasites that begin contaminating porous surfaces within hours. Drywall wicks it upward. Subfloor panels absorb it from below. Grout lines trap it where a mop cannot reach. This is not a cleanup job that waits until morning or gets handled with bleach and a bucket. Sewage backup cleanup requires containment, proper extraction, antimicrobial treatment, and verification that the contamination is actually gone.

## What sewage cleanup and sanitization actually involves

Sewage water is classified as Category 3 under industry standards, meaning it carries the highest level of biological contamination. That classification changes everything about how the work is done. Personal protective equipment is required before anyone enters the affected area. Materials that have absorbed sewage, including carpet, carpet pad, drywall below the flood line, and certain types of insulation, typically cannot be salvaged and must be removed.

Once the contaminated materials are out, the remaining structure is treated with EPA-registered antimicrobial agents designed to address the specific pathogens present in raw sewage. This is not a surface wipe-down. The treatment has to reach the same depth the contamination reached. After sanitization, the area is dried thoroughly, because moisture left behind after sewage cleanup creates a secondary problem: ongoing microbial growth in the structure.

Equipment used in a proper sewage cleanup includes truck-mounted or portable extraction units, HEPA-filtered air scrubbers to address airborne particulates, and moisture meters to confirm that structural materials have returned to acceptable readings before any restoration work begins. The process takes anywhere from two to five days depending on how far the sewage traveled and how long it sat before extraction began.

## Our process

1. **Containment and safety staging.** Before extraction begins, the affected area is isolated to prevent cross-contamination to unaffected rooms. HVAC vents in the zone may be sealed. Anyone entering wears appropriate PPE. The source of the backup is confirmed with the homeowner so that the drain line is not reactivated during cleanup.

2. **Extraction and gross removal.** Standing sewage and solid waste are extracted using commercial-grade equipment. Saturated materials, including flooring, baseboards, and drywall, are removed and bagged for proper disposal. This step cannot be rushed. Material left in place that appears dry at the surface may still be holding contamination deeper in the substrate.

3. **Antimicrobial application.** EPA-registered disinfectants are applied to all affected structural surfaces, including concrete slab, wall cavities, and floor framing. Application method and dwell time matter here. The product has to remain in contact with the surface long enough to be effective against the pathogens in Category 3 water.

4. **Structural drying and moisture verification.** Air movers and dehumidifiers are positioned to dry the structural cavity. Moisture readings are taken at multiple points across multiple days. Drying is not complete when the surface feels dry to the touch. It is complete when the readings confirm that the wood framing, concrete, or other structural materials have returned to normal moisture content for this climate.

5. **Final inspection and documentation.** Before equipment is removed, the affected area is inspected and the moisture readings are logged. This documentation is provided to the homeowner and is also what insurance adjusters use to verify that the work was performed correctly. If mold is discovered during the cleanup process, ACS handles the sewage and water damage side and can refer you to a qualified mold specialist for that portion of the work.

## What separates a good sewage cleanup response from a bad one

The most common mistake in sewage backup cleanup is treating it like a water damage job. It is not. Category 3 contamination requires a different protocol, different chemistry, and a higher standard for what counts as clean. Operators who skip antimicrobial treatment or apply a surface disinfectant without removing saturated materials first are leaving biological contamination behind inside the wall cavity or under the floor.

A second common failure is inadequate drying documentation. Insurance adjusters increasingly require moisture logs that show readings at the start, middle, and end of the drying period, taken at multiple structural points. A single final reading is not sufficient. If those logs do not exist, a carrier may dispute the claim or require re-inspection.

A third issue is improper waste disposal. Sewage-saturated materials are regulated waste in Texas and must be bagged, labeled, and disposed of at an approved facility. Leaving bags at the curb or disposing of them in a standard dumpster creates liability for the homeowner and the contractor.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

In Midland and the broader Permian Basin, the soil is predominantly caliche and clay. Clay soil does not drain well, and during heavy rain events, it can saturate quickly and put pressure on older sewer laterals and septic systems. The region also experiences significant temperature swings between seasons. Rapid temperature drops in late fall can stress aging clay sewer pipes, and the combination of shifting soil and older pipe materials is a common cause of sewer line backups in Midland's established neighborhoods.

Homes built in Midland before the 1980s frequently have cast iron or Orangeburg sewer laterals that have degraded over decades. These materials are prone to root intrusion and collapse, both of which can cause a sewer line backup to recur if only the cleanup is addressed and the pipe condition is not evaluated afterward.
