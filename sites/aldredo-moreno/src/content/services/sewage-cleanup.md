The smell hits before you see anything, a sulfuric, unmistakable odor rising from a floor drain, a toilet that won't flush, or a wet patch spreading across a basement slab. Sewage backups don't announce themselves politely. Raw or partially treated wastewater carries bacteria, viruses, and parasites that can colonize porous surfaces within hours, and the longer it sits, the deeper it migrates into subfloor assemblies, wall cavities, and HVAC returns. This is not a mop-and-bleach situation. It requires containment, proper extraction, EPA-registered disinfection, and documentation that your insurance carrier will actually accept.

## What sewage cleanup and sanitization actually involves

Sewage water is classified as Category 3, the highest contamination tier under industry standards, regardless of whether it came from a municipal sewer line backup, a septic overflow, or a failed ejector pump. That classification drives every decision on a job site: what PPE the crew wears, how affected materials are bagged and removed, which disinfectants are applied, and how long the space must be treated before it's safe to re-occupy.

The work itself involves more than removing visible waste. Sewage wicks rapidly into concrete, drywall, insulation, and wood framing. Carpet and pad are almost always non-salvageable in a Category 3 loss. Drywall that absorbed contaminated water typically needs to be cut out to a height above the waterline, not just dried in place. Subfloor assemblies, especially the tongue-and-groove OSB common in West Texas tract construction, can trap moisture and biological material between layers that surface readings won't detect.

Equipment on a sewage job includes truck-mounted or portable extraction units, HEPA-filtered air scrubbers to capture aerosolized pathogens, commercial dehumidifiers, and thermal imaging cameras to find saturation hidden behind finished surfaces. Disinfection uses EPA List N or List G registered products applied at dwell times long enough to achieve the kill claims on the label, not a quick spray-and-wipe.

## Our process

1. **Containment and safety staging.** Before extraction begins, the affected area is isolated with physical barriers and negative air pressure where needed to prevent cross-contamination to clean areas of the structure. The crew suits up in appropriate PPE, this step protects both the occupants and the technicians.

2. **Extraction and gross contamination removal.** Standing sewage and saturated materials are removed using extraction equipment and disposed of as regulated waste. Solid waste, non-salvageable porous materials (carpet, pad, saturated drywall), and contaminated debris are bagged, labeled, and removed from the structure before any disinfection begins.

3. **Structural assessment and selective demolition.** Moisture meters and thermal imaging identify how far contamination has traveled beyond the visible loss area. Affected drywall, insulation, and subfloor sections are removed to clean, unaffected material, leaving contaminated substrate in place and drying over it is one of the most common errors on Category 3 losses.

4. **EPA-registered disinfection and deodorization.** All affected structural surfaces, framing, concrete, remaining subfloor, are treated with appropriate antimicrobial agents at proper dwell times. This step is documented with product names, EPA registration numbers, application rates, and surface temperatures, because your adjuster will ask.

5. **Drying, verification, and clearance.** After disinfection, the structure is dried using commercial dehumidification and airflow equipment. Final moisture readings are logged against pre-loss baselines. The job is not complete until readings confirm the structure has returned to acceptable moisture levels, not when it looks dry.

## What separates a good sewage response from a bad one

The most common failure on sewage jobs is treating a Category 3 loss like a Category 1 water loss, extracting the visible water, running some fans, and calling it done. That approach leaves biological contamination in the structure and creates liability for the contractor and a health risk for the occupants.

A second frequent problem is inadequate demolition scope. Technicians who are reluctant to cut drywall or pull subfloor sections leave contaminated material sealed behind new finishes. Mold colonization typically begins within 24 to 48 hours in warm, humid conditions, and West Texas summers provide exactly that environment once a structure is closed back up.

On the documentation side, insurance adjusters handling sewage claims look for Category 3 classification in the loss report, moisture logs with equipment readings and serial numbers, disinfection records with EPA registration numbers and dwell times, and a demolition scope that matches the moisture data. Jobs without that paper trail frequently result in underpaid or disputed claims.

## What does sewage cleanup and sanitization cost?

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Midland sits over expansive clay soils that shift significantly with moisture changes. During the wet cycles that follow West Texas thunderstorms, that soil movement stresses sewer lateral connections between the house and the municipal main, a common cause of line breaks and backups that aren't related to clogs at all. Septic systems in the unincorporated areas surrounding Midland face similar stress during heavy rain events when saturated soil prevents normal percolation, forcing sewage back toward the structure. Summer heat accelerates bacterial growth in affected materials, which compresses the window between a backup event and the point at which salvageable materials become non-salvageable.
