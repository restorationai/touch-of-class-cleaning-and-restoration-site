Stanton sits in the heart of Martin County, where the Permian Basin's alkaline, caliche-heavy soil plays havoc with buried infrastructure. That same soil, dense and slow to drain, means a sewage backup or septic overflow doesn't just stay in your basement; it saturates the ground around your foundation and lingers. Whether the problem starts with a sewer line backup after a hard West Texas rain or a failing septic system on a rural property at the edge of town, raw sewage inside a home or business demands immediate removal and thorough sanitization before secondary contamination spreads.

## Why Stanton Properties See Sewage Backup Issues

Martin County's housing stock spans decades of construction, and many of the older homes in and around Stanton were built when clay or cast-iron sewer laterals were standard. Those materials crack, root-intrude, and collapse over time, often without warning. When a lateral fails, sewage backs up through the lowest drain in the house: a floor drain, a toilet, or a laundry tub.

The region's soil chemistry compounds the problem. Caliche layers near the surface resist percolation, so septic systems that are even slightly undersized or past their service life can overflow during wet periods. Stanton receives modest annual rainfall, but when storms do arrive, often as intense, short-duration events, the ground can't absorb runoff fast enough, and older septic tanks get hydraulically overloaded. The result is sewage surfacing in yards or backing into homes through fixtures connected to the drain field line.

Properties on the outskirts of Stanton that rely on private septic rather than the municipal sewer system face a distinct risk profile. Septic overflow cleanup requires not just removing the visible waste but also assessing whether the drain field itself has been compromised, a step that affects how the site is sanitized and what documentation is needed for any insurance or health department follow-up.

## Our Sewage Cleanup and Sanitization Process in Stanton

Raw sewage is classified as Category 3 water, the most contaminated type, and the cleanup sequence reflects that. The process begins with containment: isolating the affected area to prevent cross-contamination to clean spaces. Porous materials that have absorbed sewage, drywall, insulation, carpet padding, wood subfloor, are removed and bagged for disposal rather than dried in place. Drying contaminated porous material is not an acceptable substitute for removal when Category 3 water is involved.

Once the bulk waste and unsalvageable materials are out, surfaces are cleaned with EPA-registered disinfectants rated for sewage contamination. This includes structural cavities, floor joists, concrete slabs, and any HVAC components that may have drawn contaminated air. Deodorization follows, sewage odor in a West Texas home in summer, where interior temperatures can spike quickly, becomes embedded in wall cavities fast if not addressed at the source.

Documentation runs throughout the process: moisture readings, photographs of affected materials before removal, and a written scope. That documentation matters whether you're filing a homeowner's insurance claim or simply need a record that the property was properly remediated.
