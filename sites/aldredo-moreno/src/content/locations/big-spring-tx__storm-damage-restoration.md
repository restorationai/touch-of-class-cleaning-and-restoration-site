West Texas storms don't ease into Big Spring, they arrive fast and leave behind a landscape of stripped roofing, shattered windows, and waterlogged interiors before most homeowners have had time to move their vehicles into the garage. The same open-plains geography that makes Howard County beautiful makes it a corridor for severe weather: hail the size of golf balls, straight-line winds pushing 70 mph or better, and the occasional tornado that tracks across the Permian Basin with little warning. When that kind of storm rolls through, the damage it leaves behind is a race against time.

## Why Big Spring Properties Take a Hard Hit from Severe Weather

Big Spring sits at the edge of the Southern Plains, where cold fronts dropping out of the Panhandle collide with warm, moist air pushing north from the Gulf. That collision zone produces some of the most intense convective storms in Texas, and Howard County sees its share of them every spring and early summer.

The region's housing stock adds another layer of vulnerability. A significant portion of Big Spring's residential neighborhoods were built during the mid-20th century oil boom, when construction standards for wind uplift were far less stringent than current Texas Residential Code requirements. Older asphalt shingles, original wood fascia, and single-pane windows are common on homes throughout the city, materials that hold up fine in calm conditions but fail quickly under sustained wind loads or large hail. When a storm peels back a section of aging roof decking, the interior can take on water within minutes.

Flat and semi-flat commercial roofing is also widespread in Big Spring's business corridors, and ponding water after a heavy storm event is a known problem on those structures. A roof that looks intact from the street can be hiding standing water that is already wicking into insulation below.

## Our Storm Damage Restoration Process in Big Spring

Every storm job starts with a thorough assessment before any material is removed or covered. That means walking the full perimeter, getting eyes on the roof, checking attic spaces for daylight or moisture intrusion, and using moisture meters to map where water has traveled beyond the obvious wet spots. In this climate, hail damage to HVAC condenser coils and exterior venting is easy to overlook during the initial walk, we check those too, because a bent condenser fin or crushed vent cap creates a secondary problem that shows up weeks later.

Once the scope is documented, with photographs and written notes formatted for insurance review, emergency stabilization comes first: tarping compromised roof sections, boarding broken windows, and extracting any standing water before it migrates into wall cavities or subfloor assemblies. Structural drying follows using commercial-grade dehumidifiers and air movers calibrated to the ambient conditions. West Texas summers mean low relative humidity outdoors, which actually accelerates drying in some situations, but interior spaces that have been sealed against the heat can trap moisture longer than you'd expect.

Debris removal, downed tree limbs, displaced roofing material, broken glass, is handled as part of the process, not as a separate line item to negotiate.

## Big Spring Insurance Coordination

Storm damage claims in Texas are governed by the Texas Department of Insurance, and Howard County homeowners have the same right to a prompt adjuster inspection as anyone in the state. We document damage in a format that aligns with how adjusters evaluate losses, line-item scope, photographs with timestamps, and moisture readings that establish the extent of water intrusion. That documentation matters because insurers look for evidence that damage is storm-related and not pre-existing deterioration, and a well-organized claim file moves faster than a disorganized one.

If your carrier requires a public adjuster or a second inspection, we work alongside that process rather than around it.

## Local Note

One thing that catches homeowners off guard in Big Spring: after a hail event, the caliche-heavy soil around foundations can shift as it cycles through wet and dry phases, especially when gutters are overwhelmed and water pools against the house. That soil movement is slow, but it's real, and it means that a storm inspection here should include a look at the foundation perimeter and any attached flatwork like driveways or sidewalks, not just the roof and walls. It's a West Texas quirk that doesn't come up much in restoration guides written for wetter climates.
