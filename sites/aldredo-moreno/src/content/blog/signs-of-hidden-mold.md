If you've noticed a musty smell that won't go away, a water stain that keeps spreading, or a patch of drywall that feels soft to the touch, you may be dealing with mold growing somewhere you can't see. The most common hidden spots are behind baseboards, under carpet padding, inside wall cavities near plumbing, and in HVAC ductwork. The fastest way to confirm it is to track down the moisture source first, since mold almost never shows up without water feeding it somewhere nearby. Below are the seven signs worth paying attention to, along with what to do once you spot them.

## The 7 Signs to Watch For

1. **A persistent musty or earthy smell**, especially in a bathroom, basement, or closet that doesn't air out well. Mold produces this odor as it digests organic material, and it's often detectable before any visible growth appears.
2. **Discoloration on walls or ceilings** that looks like a yellow, brown, or black stain, particularly in a ring or cloud pattern rather than a straight water line.
3. **Peeling, bubbling, or cracking paint and wallpaper**, which usually means moisture is trapped behind the surface and has been there long enough to break down the adhesive.
4. **Warped baseboards, buckled flooring, or soft drywall**, a sign that water has been sitting against building materials for days or longer, not just a one-time splash.
5. **Condensation on windows or pipes that keeps coming back**, which points to excess humidity in the space, a condition mold needs to establish itself.
6. **Visible spotting in HVAC vents or on the supply registers**, which can mean the ductwork or the coil inside the unit has moisture sitting in it.
7. **Worsening allergy-like symptoms at home that improve elsewhere**, such as sneezing, congestion, or eye irritation. This isn't something a homeowner can diagnose, but a pattern tied specifically to one building is worth taking seriously enough to investigate the physical cause.

## Immediate Steps to Take

Start with the moisture, not the mold itself. Find out whether the spot is tied to a known leak (under a sink, around a window, near a roof penetration) or whether it's a humidity problem from poor ventilation. A simple way to narrow it down: run a dehumidifier in the room for 48 hours and see if the smell or staining lessens. If it does, you're likely looking at an ambient humidity issue. If it doesn't, there's probably an active water source feeding it that needs to be traced, often with a moisture meter or a visual inspection behind the affected surface.

Open the area to light and air where you safely can. Pull furniture away from exterior walls where mold tends to form. If you can access the back side of a stained section of drywall (an attic, a crawlspace, a utility closet), take a look before assuming the worst. Some discoloration is old and dry, left over from a leak that's already been fixed.

## What Not to Do

- Don't paint over a stain to cover it up. Paint doesn't stop growth and makes it harder for anyone to assess the extent of the problem later.
- Don't rip out drywall or flooring yourself without first confirming the water source is resolved, since you can end up doing it twice if the leak is still active.
- Don't rely on bleach alone on porous materials like drywall, insulation, or carpet padding. Bleach can kill surface spores but doesn't reach into the material the way remediation work does, and it can mask the problem instead of solving it.
- Don't assume a small spot means a small problem. Mold visible on a surface is often just the part you can see; the colony frequently extends further into the wall cavity or subfloor than it appears.

## The Longer Process

Addressing hidden mold usually happens in two stages, even when two different contractors are involved. First, the moisture source gets fixed and the structure dried out, which in Midland often means dealing with a slow plumbing leak, a roof issue after a storm, or condensation from an HVAC system working overtime against the summer heat. Materials get dried per the IICRC S500 drying standard where applicable, with moisture readings taken over several days to confirm the structure is back to a normal range, not just surface-dry.

Second, once the area is dry and stable, a mold remediation specialist can assess what needs to be removed, cleaned, or treated, and confirm the space is safe to close back up. Skipping the drying step and going straight to cosmetic repairs is the most common reason mold comes back in the same spot a few months later.
