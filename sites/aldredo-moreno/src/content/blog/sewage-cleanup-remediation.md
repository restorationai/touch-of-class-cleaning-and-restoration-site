**TL;DR:** Sewage cleanup and remediation means safely removing contaminated ("black water") material, disinfecting every surface it touched, and drying the structure to stop bacteria and mold growth. It typically costs $500 to $7,000 depending on how far the water traveled and what materials it soaked. Homeowners insurance often covers sudden sewer backups but usually excludes gradual seepage, so documentation matters. A licensed remediation crew, not a mop and a bottle of bleach, is the safe way to handle it.

If your toilet backed up onto the bathroom floor, or a sewer line broke under your Big Spring yard and pushed wastewater into your utility room, you're dealing with what the industry calls Category 3 water, also known as black water. That's not a term restoration companies use to sound dramatic. It means the water is carrying bacteria, viruses, and parasites, and it changes how the cleanup has to be done. Howard County's older clay and cast-iron sewer lines, common in neighborhoods around downtown Big Spring, are especially prone to root intrusion and collapse, which is one of the more common causes of a backup here.

## What Counts as a Sewage Cleanup Emergency?

Any contact between your living space and wastewater from a toilet, sewer line, or septic system counts as a sewage cleanup emergency. That includes a single overflowing toilet, a slow floor drain backup during heavy rain, or a full sewer line failure flooding a basement or crawlspace.

The source matters for how the job is classified. A clean supply-line break is Category 1 water. A backed-up toilet with no solid waste is usually Category 2. Anything touching a sewer main, septic tank, or standing floodwater that's picked up ground contaminants is Category 3, the [IICRC](https://www.iicrc.org) classification that requires full PPE, containment, and disposal of most porous materials the water touched.

In Big Spring, the most common triggers we see are aging clay sewer laterals cracking from tree roots, municipal line backups during heavy spring storms, and septic systems on the outskirts of town (around Coahoma and the county line) reaching capacity after prolonged rain. Any of these can push wastewater back through your lowest drains, which is usually a shower, floor drain, or basement toilet.

## How Does Professional Sewage Remediation Work?

Professional sewage remediation follows a set sequence: extraction, removal of contaminated materials, cleaning and disinfection, and controlled drying, verified with moisture readings before anything gets rebuilt.

Here's what that looks like on an actual job:

1. **Containment and PPE setup.** The crew suits up in Tyvek, gloves, and respirators before touching anything, and seals off the affected area to keep contamination from spreading to clean parts of the house.
2. **Extraction.** Standing wastewater gets pumped and wet-vacced out using equipment dedicated to Category 3 work, never mixed with clean-water equipment.
3. **Removal of contaminated porous materials.** Carpet, carpet pad, baseboards, and drywall that absorbed black water typically can't be saved. The [EPA](https://www.epa.gov) treats sewage-contaminated porous material as a health risk that cleaning alone doesn't resolve, so it gets bagged and disposed of, not dried in place.
4. **Cleaning and disinfection.** Hard surfaces, framing, and subfloor get cleaned with an EPA-registered antimicrobial, then re-treated after drying begins.
5. **Drying and verification.** Air movers and dehumidifiers run until moisture readings in the studs and subfloor return to normal, dried to the IICRC S500 standard, not just "looks dry."
6. **Rebuild.** Drywall, flooring, and trim get replaced once the space passes a final moisture check.

Most single-bathroom backups take 3 to 5 days from extraction through drying, before any rebuild work starts. Larger basement or crawlspace losses can run a week or more.

## Why Can't You Just Mop Up a Sewage Backup Yourself?

You can't safely mop up a sewage backup because black water carries pathogens that survive on surfaces long after the visible mess is gone, and household cleaners aren't formulated to neutralize them. This is one of the few restoration jobs where DIY genuinely puts your health at risk.

Bacteria like E. coli and salmonella, along with norovirus and other pathogens, can live in carpet padding, drywall, and subfloor for days. A wet-vac and bleach will make a room look clean without actually disinfecting what's inside the walls or under the flooring. That's why porous materials get removed rather than cleaned. We cover the specific health risks in more detail in [Why You Can't DIY Sewage Backup](/blog/sewage-backup-health-risks/), including what happens if contaminated water sits for more than a day or two.

The other risk is cross-contamination. Walking contaminated water through the rest of the house on shoes, or running a household wet-vac that then gets used elsewhere, spreads the problem instead of containing it.

## What Does Sewage Cleanup Cost, and Will Insurance Cover It?

Sewage cleanup in the Midland-Odessa-Big Spring area typically runs $500 for a small, contained toilet overflow up to $7,000 or more for a basement or crawlspace flooded by a collapsed sewer line. The biggest cost driver is how far the water traveled and how much drywall, flooring, and framing it reached before anyone caught it.

Most homeowners policies cover sudden, accidental sewer backups, but many require a specific sewer backup rider or endorsement, so it's worth checking your declarations page before assuming you're covered. Gradual seepage from a slow leak that built up over weeks is usually excluded, which is why documenting the failure point (a burst line, a sudden backup) matters from the first phone call. A written scope of work and moisture-loss documentation is what most adjusters ask for, and it's something ACS Enterprise provides before work begins, since every loss is different and there's no honest way to quote sewage cleanup without seeing what the water actually touched.

## What Should You Do Right Now If You Have a Sewage Backup?

If you have an active sewage backup, stop using any drains or toilets connected to the affected line, keep people and pets out of the area, and call a restoration crew rather than attempting cleanup yourself. Turning off the water supply to a backed-up fixture can also slow the spread while you wait.

Don't run a shop vac meant for other household use, and don't let anyone walk through the affected area without protective footwear. If you can safely photograph the damage from a doorway before anyone enters, that documentation helps your insurance claim later.
