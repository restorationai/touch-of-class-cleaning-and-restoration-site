## What Does Storm and Wind Damage Restoration Actually Include?

Storm and wind damage restoration is the process of stabilizing a property after wind, hail, or a related storm event, then drying out and cleaning up whatever moisture got in. That typically means emergency tarping or board-up, water extraction, structural drying, and removing storm debris before the next weather system arrives.

The work usually happens in this order: stop active intrusion (tarp the roof, board a broken window), extract any standing water, then set air movers and dehumidifiers to dry framing and insulation to the IICRC [S500 standard](https://www.iicrc.org) for water damage restoration. If wind-driven rain soaked carpet or upholstered furniture, that gets cleaned or extracted separately. Debris from downed branches, torn siding, or collapsed outbuildings gets hauled off so the property isn't a liability while repairs happen.

In Andrews, where properties tend to sit on wide, exposed lots with little tree cover to break wind, the exterior envelope takes the brunt of a storm. Metal roofing and vinyl siding common in the area can look fine from the ground while wind has already lifted seams enough to let water through.

## How Do You Know If Wind Damage Is Serious Enough to Call a Restoration Company?

Any wind event that brings water inside the structure, cracks a window, or visibly lifts roofing material is serious enough to call a restoration company the same day. Waiting even 48 hours lets moisture spread into drywall, insulation, and subfloor where it's harder and more expensive to dry out.

Signs worth acting on right away:

- Water stains spreading on a ceiling or upper wall after a storm
- Shingles, tiles, or roofing granules on the ground around the foundation
- A door or window that no longer seals, letting wind and rain through the gap
- Siding panels cracked, bowed, or missing
- A musty smell starting within 24-48 hours of the storm, which usually means moisture is already trapped

Damage that looks cosmetic from the street, a few missing shingles, a dented gutter, can still mean water got into the attic or wall cavity during the event. A moisture meter reading on the drywall or decking tells you what the eye can't.

## What Should You Do in the First 24 Hours After a Wind Storm?

In the first 24 hours, stop further water entry, document the damage with photos, and get moisture out before it spreads. Order matters here, because every hour wet building material sits is an hour closer to secondary damage.

1. **Photograph everything before you touch it.** Wide shots of the exterior, close-ups of the specific damage, and any interior water intrusion. This is the documentation your adjuster will ask for.
2. **Tarp or board open areas** to stop more wind and rain from getting in, especially if another system is forecast.
3. **Move anything in the path of water** (furniture, electronics, rugs) and start extracting standing water if it's safe to do so.
4. **Shut off power to any circuit near water intrusion** if you're not sure it's safe.
5. **Call a restoration company for a moisture assessment**, even if the visible damage looks minor. Hidden moisture in insulation or subfloor doesn't show up for days, sometimes as mold or a soft spot in the floor.

## Will Homeowners Insurance Cover Wind and Storm Damage?

Most homeowners and commercial property policies in Texas cover sudden wind and storm damage, including hail, as long as the policy doesn't carry a separate wind/hail exclusion or a high wind deductible. Many West Texas policies carry a percentage-based wind/hail deductible rather than a flat dollar amount, so it's worth checking your declarations page before assuming full coverage.

What typically gets covered: roof damage from wind or hail, broken windows from wind-blown debris, water damage that results directly from the storm event, and the restoration work needed to dry and clean up afterward. What's often excluded or limited: damage from long-term neglect (a roof that was already failing before the storm), and flooding from rising water, which usually requires separate flood coverage through [FEMA's National Flood Insurance Program](https://www.fema.gov/flood-insurance).

A written scope of work and dated photos from the restoration crew make the claims conversation faster. Adjusters move quicker when the documentation is already organized.
