## Restoration emergencies common in Imperial

Imperial sits in Pecos County, deep in the Trans-Pecos region where the climate swings hard in both directions. Summers push surface temperatures well past 100°F, which accelerates mold colonization once moisture gets behind a wall, within 24 to 48 hours, organic materials in a wet cavity can begin supporting growth. Winters bring periodic hard freezes that catch older supply lines off guard, especially in homes where pipes run through uninsulated exterior walls or unconditioned spaces like garages and utility rooms. When a freeze-thaw event hits, burst pipes can discharge hundreds of gallons before anyone notices the pressure drop.

Severe thunderstorms roll through the region in spring and early summer, dropping concentrated rainfall on caliche-heavy soil that sheds water rather than absorbing it. That runoff finds its way under doors, through foundation cracks, and into low-lying areas of a slab before the storm is even over. Sewage backups follow heavy rain events when municipal laterals get overwhelmed, pushing contaminated water into floor drains and tub surrounds.

## Building stock, site conditions, and permits in Imperial

Most of Imperial's residential housing dates to the mid-twentieth century, built during and after the Permian Basin oil booms. Construction from that era is predominantly slab-on-grade with masonry or wood-frame exterior walls, which means water that breaches the envelope tends to wick into the slab edge and wall base rather than pooling in a crawlspace or basement. Supply lines in homes of this vintage are often galvanized steel or early copper, both of which corrode from the inside out and can fail without obvious exterior warning signs. Restoration scopes in pre-1980 structures should account for the possibility of asbestos-containing materials in floor tile, mastic, and pipe insulation.
