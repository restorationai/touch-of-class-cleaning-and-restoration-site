## Restoration emergencies common in Largo

Largo sits in the heart of Pinellas County, where the combination of a subtropical climate, a shallow water table, and decades of dense residential development creates predictable, and recurring, restoration risks. Hurricane season runs June through November, and even a storm that makes landfall well south of Tampa Bay can push enough wind-driven rain through aging window seals and roof penetrations to soak interior walls within hours. Outside of named storms, the daily afternoon thunderstorms that build over the Gulf from late spring through early fall drop rainfall fast enough to overwhelm older stormwater infrastructure and send water through low-lying slabs and garage floors.

The housing stock matters here. A large share of Largo's single-family homes were built between the 1950s and the 1980s, when slab-on-grade construction was the norm and vapor barriers were minimal by today's standards. That means moisture that gets under a slab or behind a stucco wall can stay hidden until mold colonies are already established, sometimes weeks after the original event. The region's consistently high humidity (routinely above 70 percent even in the dry season) slows evaporation and accelerates microbial growth once building materials are wet.

## Building stock, site conditions, and permits in Largo

Most of Largo's residential construction dates from the post-war boom through the 1980s. Slab-on-grade is the dominant foundation type, basements are essentially nonexistent this close to sea level, and crawlspaces are uncommon. Homes built before the mid-1980s may contain asbestos-containing materials in floor tile, mastic, and textured ceilings, which must be assessed before any demolition work begins. Plumbing in that same era often includes galvanized steel supply lines and cast-iron drain lines, both of which corrode and fail in ways that can go undetected inside slabs.

Pinellas County's soils are predominantly sandy with a high water table, in many parts of Largo, the water table sits only a few feet below grade. That limits how much moisture the ground can absorb during heavy rain events and means water that enters a structure has nowhere to drain passively.
