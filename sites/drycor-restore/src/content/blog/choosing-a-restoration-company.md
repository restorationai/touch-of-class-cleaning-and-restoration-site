The single most important thing to do when choosing a restoration company is to verify credentials *before* you sign anything, not after the crew is already in your home pulling up flooring. Restoration contractors can arrive within hours of a disaster, and the pressure of that moment makes it easy to skip due diligence. This guide walks you through exactly what to check, what red flags to walk away from, and what questions to ask so you end up with a company that actually fixes the problem instead of one that disappears after the check clears.

## Why the Restoration Industry Attracts Bad Actors

Water damage, fire damage, and mold are high-stress, high-dollar situations. Homeowners are often displaced, emotionally raw, and facing insurance paperwork they've never seen before. That combination is a magnet for contractors who specialize in looking credible rather than being credible.

The pattern usually goes like this: a storm rolls through, and within 24 hours your neighborhood fills up with unmarked vans and door-knockers promising fast work and full insurance coverage. Some of those companies are legitimate. Some will collect your Assignment of Benefits signature, do a partial job, and leave you in a billing dispute with your insurer that drags on for months.

After a significant rain event, demand for restoration services spikes fast, which is exactly when shortcuts happen.

## What to Verify Before You Let Anyone Through the Door

Treat this like a short checklist. It takes ten minutes and can save you thousands.

**1. Ask for proof of IICRC certification.**
The Institute of Inspection, Cleaning and Restoration Certification sets the technical standards for water damage drying (S500), fire and smoke restoration (S700), and mold remediation (S520). A technician who has completed IICRC coursework understands psychrometrics, moisture mapping, and containment protocols, not just how to run a fan. Ask specifically which certifications the company holds, and look them up at iicrc.org if you want to confirm.

**2. Confirm they carry general liability and workers' compensation insurance.**
If a technician is injured in your home and the company doesn't carry workers' comp, you could be liable. Ask for a certificate of insurance, not just a verbal assurance. A legitimate company will email it to you before the job starts.

**3. Check their license status with the state.**
In Florida, contractors performing structural repairs as part of a restoration project typically need a state-issued contractor's license. You can verify license status through the Florida Department of Business and Professional Regulation (DBPR) online. This takes about two minutes.

**4. Read recent reviews, and read the negative ones.**
A company with 200 five-star reviews and zero complaints is statistically unusual. Look for how they respond to criticism. A company that engages professionally with a bad review is more trustworthy than one with a suspiciously perfect record.

**5. Get the scope of work in writing before anything starts.**
A written scope should specify what materials will be removed, what drying equipment will be used and for how long, how the work will be documented, and what the billing arrangement is. Vague contracts protect the contractor, not you.

## Red Flags That Should Make You Walk Away

Some of these are obvious in hindsight. They're harder to spot when you're standing in a flooded living room.

- **They ask you to sign an Assignment of Benefits (AOB) immediately.** An AOB transfers your insurance claim rights to the contractor. This isn't always predatory, it can simplify billing, but signing it before you understand the scope of work hands over significant control. Read it carefully, or have your insurance agent review it first.
- **They can't give you a written estimate or scope before starting.** "We'll figure it out as we go" is not a restoration plan.
- **They discourage you from calling your insurance company.** A legitimate company works alongside your insurer, not around them.
- **They guarantee mold will be completely eliminated.** No honest contractor makes that claim. Mold remediation means reducing mold to normal environmental levels and removing the moisture source, not a sterile guarantee.
- **They arrived unsolicited after a storm and pressure you to decide on the spot.** Take 24 hours. A real emergency has already happened; waiting a day to vet a contractor rarely makes the damage meaningfully worse.

## Questions to Ask Before You Hire

These aren't trick questions, a competent company will answer them without hesitation.

1. **Who will actually be on-site doing the work?** Some companies subcontract heavily. Know whether the crew that shows up is employed by the company or hired day-of.
2. **How will you document moisture readings before, during, and after drying?** Proper water damage restoration involves daily moisture mapping with calibrated meters. Ask to see a sample report.
3. **How do you handle contents, furniture, personal items, during the job?** Contents packing and storage is a separate process, and you want to know upfront what happens to your belongings.
4. **Will you communicate directly with my insurance adjuster?** Most established restoration companies are experienced with insurance documentation and will coordinate directly. If a company seems unfamiliar with the claims process, that's worth noting.
5. **What happens if hidden damage is found after work begins?** Scope changes happen, especially with water damage behind walls or mold discovered during fire cleanup. Ask how change orders are handled and whether you'll be notified before additional work is authorized.

## Understanding the Full Scope of a Restoration Project

One reason homeowners get burned is that they don't realize restoration is a multi-phase process. The emergency mitigation phase, stopping the water, boarding up windows, extracting standing water, is just the beginning.

After mitigation comes the drying phase, which for water damage can take several days depending on the materials affected. Concrete block walls common in Hillsborough County construction absorb moisture differently than wood-framed walls, and drying times reflect that. Then comes the assessment phase: testing for mold, evaluating structural integrity, documenting what needs to be replaced versus what can be restored.

Only after all of that comes reconstruction, replacing drywall, flooring, cabinetry, or whatever was removed or damaged. Some restoration companies handle reconstruction in-house; others hand off to a general contractor. Either can work, but you want to know which model you're dealing with before you start.

Fire damage adds another layer. Smoke residue penetrates porous materials, insulation, wood framing, HVAC ductwork, and the odor can return months later if the cleaning phase was rushed or incomplete. Mold remediation, meanwhile, requires identifying and correcting the moisture source, not just treating visible growth. If the source isn't fixed, the mold comes back.
