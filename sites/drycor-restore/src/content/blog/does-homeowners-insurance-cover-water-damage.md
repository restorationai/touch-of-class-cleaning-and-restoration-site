Whether your homeowners insurance covers water damage depends almost entirely on *where the water came from*, not how much damage it caused. A burst pipe that soaks your hardwood floors is usually covered. Rainwater that crept in through a foundation crack over two seasons usually is not. Understanding that single distinction, sudden and accidental versus gradual or flood-related, will tell you most of what you need to know before you ever call your adjuster.

## The Core Rule: Sudden vs. Gradual

Standard homeowners policies (HO-3 is the most common form in Florida) cover water damage that is **sudden and accidental**. The policy language matters less than the concept: the insurer wants to know that you couldn't have prevented it with routine maintenance and that it happened in a discrete event, not over months.

**Generally covered under a standard HO-3:**

- A supply line bursts behind a washing machine and floods the laundry room
- An upstairs toilet overflows and water damages the ceiling below
- An HVAC drain pan cracks and soaks the subfloor
- A neighbor's pipe breaks and water migrates into your unit (in a condo or townhome)
- A contractor accidentally punctures a pipe during a renovation

**Generally NOT covered:**

- A slow drip under the sink that has been rotting the cabinet floor for months
- Seepage through a cracked foundation or block wall
- Flooding from a storm, rising creek, or storm-surge, that requires a separate flood policy through the NFIP or a private carrier
- Sewer or drain backup, often excluded unless you've added a specific endorsement
- Roof leaks that developed because the roof was overdue for replacement

The line between "sudden" and "gradual" is where most claim disputes happen. If an adjuster finds staining, mold growth, or deteriorated materials that suggest a long-running problem, they can, and often will, deny coverage on the grounds that the damage was not sudden.

## What Florida Homeowners Need to Know Specifically

Florida's climate creates a few wrinkles worth understanding before you file.

First, **flood is almost never covered by homeowners insurance** in Florida, and the distinction matters enormously here. When a named storm pushes water inland or a heavy afternoon thunderstorm overwhelms drainage, the resulting water intrusion is classified as flood, not a covered peril under your HO policy. If you're in a FEMA-designated flood zone, your mortgage lender likely required you to carry a separate NFIP policy. If you're not in a mapped zone, you may have no flood coverage at all.

Second, Florida's humidity means that water damage left unaddressed can produce visible mold growth within 24 to 48 hours in summer conditions. Most policies cover mold remediation *only when it results directly from a covered water loss*, and only up to a sublimit (often $10,000 on standard policies, though this varies). If mold is discovered months later and the original water event can't be documented, the mold claim is likely to be denied on its own.

Third, slab construction, which is common throughout the Tampa Bay area and Hillsborough County, creates a specific exposure. A pinhole leak in a copper supply line running under a concrete slab can go undetected for weeks. When it surfaces, the remediation involves not just drying but potentially cutting through concrete. Some policies include "slab leak" coverage; many do not. Check your policy's "service line" and "water damage" sections carefully.

## How to Document a Water Loss Before You Touch Anything

If you've just discovered water damage, documentation is the most important thing you can do in the first hour, before you move furniture, pull up carpet, or start fans.

1. **Stop the water source.** Turn off the supply valve closest to the break. If you can't find it, shut off the main at the meter.
2. **Photograph everything before cleanup.** Shoot wide angles showing the full room, then close-ups of the water line, affected materials, and the source of the leak. Include a timestamp if your phone allows it.
3. **Note the date and time** the damage was discovered and, if you know it, when the event likely occurred.
4. **Save the broken part.** If a supply line failed, keep the line. If a fitting cracked, keep the fitting. Physical evidence supports the "sudden and accidental" argument.
5. **Call your insurance company to open a claim** before you begin significant cleanup. Most policies require "prompt notice", waiting days can complicate your claim.
6. **Begin mitigation.** You are required under most policies to prevent further damage. That means extracting standing water, moving belongings out of wet areas, and ventilating the space. It does *not* mean waiting for an adjuster to arrive before doing anything, that's a common misconception that leads to preventable mold growth.

## What an Adjuster Is Looking For

When an adjuster walks through a water-damaged property, they're building a timeline. They'll look at the staining pattern, the condition of building materials, whether mold is present, and whether there's evidence of prior repairs or ignored maintenance. Their job is to determine whether the loss fits the policy's covered peril.

A few things that can complicate your claim:

- **Pre-existing moisture.** If there's old water staining behind a wall that's opened up, an adjuster may argue the damage predates the event you're claiming.
- **Deferred maintenance.** A supply hose that's visibly cracked or corroded may lead to a denial on the grounds that the failure was foreseeable.
- **Incomplete documentation.** If you cleaned up before photographing, or discarded the failed component, you've made it harder to prove the loss was sudden.

This is also why having a professional restoration company document the loss alongside you can matter. When a crew uses moisture meters, thermal imaging, and written scope reports, that documentation often carries more weight with an adjuster than a homeowner's description of what they found.

## When to Call a Water Damage Professional

Not every water event needs a restoration contractor. A small toilet overflow that you caught in minutes and dried thoroughly with towels and a fan may be genuinely fine. But call a professional if:

- Water has been sitting for more than an hour on wood, drywall, or carpet
- The source was a sewage line or drain backup (contaminated water requires different handling)
- You can smell a musty odor, that's often the first sign mold has already started
- Water has migrated into walls or under flooring (surface dryness doesn't mean structural dryness)
- The affected area is more than a few square feet

Water behind drywall can read completely dry at the surface while the wood framing and insulation behind it stay saturated for weeks. The only way to know is with a calibrated moisture meter or thermal camera, tools a trained technician brings to the job.
