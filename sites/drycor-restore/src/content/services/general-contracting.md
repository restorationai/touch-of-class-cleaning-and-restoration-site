## What Renovations, Remodels and General Contracting actually involves

After a water loss, a fire, or years of deferred maintenance, the structure you're left with is rarely the one you want to live or work in. Sometimes the damage dictates the scope, a burst pipe that soaked through subfloor and cabinetry means the kitchen has to come apart anyway. Other times the trigger is simpler: a bathroom that hasn't been touched since the 1980s, a floor plan that no longer fits how your family actually uses the space, or a rental unit that needs a full turn before the next tenant. Renovation and remodeling work sits at the intersection of construction craft and project management, and when it follows a damage event, it also has to satisfy an insurance adjuster's scope of loss.

General contracting means holding together every trade on the job, demo crews, framers, plumbers, electricians, tile setters, cabinet installers, painters, under a single licensed contractor who is accountable for the finished product. License #CBC1253966 covers that coordination in Florida, which matters because unlicensed work can void a homeowner's insurance policy and create title problems at resale. The work itself ranges from a single-room bathroom remodel (new tile, vanity, fixtures, and exhaust fan) to a full post-damage rebuild that requires pulling permits, opening walls, and matching finishes to an existing structure.

Timelines vary with scope. A kitchen remodel involving new cabinets, countertops, appliances, and flooring typically runs four to eight weeks once materials are on site. A bathroom remodel with tile work runs two to four weeks. Post-damage reconstruction that requires structural framing, drywall, and finish work can stretch longer depending on permit lead times and material availability. The EPA Lead-Safe certification the team carries matters on any pre-1978 home, disturbing painted surfaces without proper containment and disposal is a federal violation, not just a best practice.

## Our process

1. **Scope development and written estimate.** Before any work starts, the project gets a line-item written scope, materials, labor, allowances, and exclusions. For post-damage work, this scope is formatted to align with insurance carrier documentation requirements. Nothing is vague.

2. **Permitting and trade scheduling.** Hillsborough County requires permits for structural, electrical, plumbing, and mechanical work. Pulling permits is not optional, it protects the homeowner's insurance coverage and ensures inspections happen at the right stages. Trade scheduling is sequenced so rough-in inspections clear before walls close.

3. **Demolition and substrate preparation.** Demo is controlled, not destructive. Lead-safe practices apply on pre-1978 homes. Substrate conditions, concrete flatness, framing moisture content, existing plumbing rough-in locations, are documented before new materials go in, because surprises discovered after tile is set cost far more than surprises found during demo.

4. **Installation and finish work.** Framing, drywall, tile, cabinetry, countertops, fixtures, and paint are installed in the correct sequence. Punch lists are generated before the final walkthrough, not during it.

5. **Final inspection and closeout.** Permit inspections are scheduled and passed. The homeowner receives copies of all inspection records, warranties on installed materials, and a final lien waiver, documentation that matters at resale and for insurance purposes.

## What separates a good renovation response from a bad one

The most common failure in post-damage renovation is scope creep that nobody documented. An adjuster approves replacement of water-damaged drywall; the contractor opens the wall and finds rotted framing. If that discovery isn't photographed, documented, and submitted as a supplement before the framing is replaced, the homeowner absorbs the cost. Good general contractors document every hidden condition the moment it's exposed.

The second failure is skipping permits to move faster. In Florida, unpermitted work discovered during a future sale or insurance claim can require the work to be demolished and redone at the homeowner's expense. There is no shortcut worth that risk.

On kitchen and bathroom remodels specifically, the mistakes that surface later are almost always in the substrate: tile set over an uncured or deflecting subfloor, a shower pan that wasn't properly sloped to the drain, cabinet boxes shimmed rather than leveled because the floor wasn't addressed first. These aren't visible at completion. They show up as cracked grout, standing water in a shower corner, or cabinet doors that won't close, six months after the contractor has been paid.

Insurance adjusters look for unit-cost documentation that matches Xactimate line items, photos of conditions before and after, and permit records that confirm code-compliant installation. A general contractor who speaks that language moves claims forward; one who doesn't creates delays.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

In Thonotosasa and the broader Hillsborough County area, the renovation calendar has real friction points. Hurricane season runs June through November, and material lead times for windows, roofing, and exterior doors can stretch during active storm years when regional demand spikes. Scheduling exterior work, roofing, siding, painting, around Florida's afternoon thunderstorm pattern matters for adhesion, curing, and worker safety.

Humidity is a year-round factor indoors. Drywall mud, paint, and tile adhesives all have manufacturer-specified temperature and humidity ranges. In an un-air-conditioned space during a Florida summer, those ranges can be difficult to maintain without temporary climate control, something worth discussing during scope development, not after finishes start failing.
