A broken window at 2 a.m. or a roof torn open by a fast-moving storm doesn't wait for business hours. Every hour a structure sits open to wind, rain, and opportunistic entry, the damage compounds, water migrates into wall cavities, personal property becomes a theft target, and an insurance adjuster's first question becomes harder to answer: *why wasn't this secured?* Emergency board-up and tarping is the single fastest intervention that stops a bad situation from becoming a catastrophic one, and the quality of that response in the first few hours shapes everything that follows.

## What emergency board-up and tarping actually involves

Board-up and tarping isn't nailing plywood over a hole. Done correctly, it's a measured, documented process of sealing a compromised structure against weather infiltration, unauthorized entry, and further structural stress.

**Board-up** typically covers broken windows, knocked-out doors, garage openings, and any wall breach caused by impact or fire. Panels are cut to fit, secured with structural screws or lag bolts into the surrounding framing, not just the siding, and sealed at the edges to prevent wind-driven rain from entering around the panel perimeter. On fire-damaged structures, board-up also prevents the draft effect that can reactivate smoldering materials.

**Roof tarping** addresses storm damage, fallen tree limbs, or fire-compromised decking. Heavy-duty polyethylene tarps (typically 6-mil or heavier for insurance documentation purposes) are secured with cap nails and wood battens, not just weighted down at the edges. The tarp must extend past the ridge line on sloped roofs and be anchored at multiple points to survive follow-on weather. A tarp that lifts in the next rain event is a liability, not a repair.

Timeline: a standard residential board-up, one or two openings, is typically completed within one to three hours of arrival. Larger losses involving multiple roof sections or extensive wall breaches take longer, and that scope is documented before work begins.

## Our process

1. **Damage assessment and photo documentation**, Before a single panel is cut, every breach is photographed and measured. This documentation becomes part of the insurance claim file and establishes the pre-secured condition of the structure. Adjuster-ready photo sets are standard, not optional.

2. **Hazard identification**, Broken glass, compromised framing, and unstable roofing materials are identified and flagged. On fire-loss board-ups, the structural integrity of the area being accessed is evaluated before crew members work overhead or near load-bearing elements.

3. **Material sizing and panel fabrication**, Plywood panels are cut on-site to match each opening precisely. Oversized panels that bridge past the rough opening into undamaged siding create new problems; undersized panels leave gaps at the edges that defeat the purpose of the board-up entirely.

4. **Fastening and sealing**, Panels are fastened into structural members, not just sheathing. Tarp installations are battened and anchored per the ridge line, with seams overlapping in the direction of prevailing drainage. Edge sealing with foam backer or caulk is applied where the opening meets finished interior surfaces.

5. **Completion documentation and scope handoff**, A written scope of the secured openings, materials used, and any conditions observed (active water intrusion, visible mold, compromised electrical) is provided before the crew leaves. This document travels directly into the insurance claim.

## What separates a good board-up response from a bad one

The most common failure in emergency board-up is speed prioritized over documentation. A crew that arrives fast, covers the hole, and leaves without photographs has created a coverage gap, the adjuster has no baseline for what the structure looked like before the securing work, and any pre-existing condition becomes a dispute.

A second frequent problem is inadequate fastening depth. Panels screwed into OSB sheathing rather than into studs or rafters will pull free under wind load. In Florida, where follow-on weather is almost guaranteed after any storm event, this isn't a theoretical risk.

On roof tarps, the failure point is almost always at the ridge. A tarp that doesn't cross the ridge and get anchored on the dry side of the slope will channel water directly into the breach rather than away from it. Adjusters and public adjusters both flag this on inspection.

Insurance carriers increasingly require that board-up work be performed by a licensed contractor, not a handyman or a general laborer, to qualify for mitigation reimbursement. DRYCOR RESTORE holds contractor license #CBC1253966 and carries the documentation carriers require.
