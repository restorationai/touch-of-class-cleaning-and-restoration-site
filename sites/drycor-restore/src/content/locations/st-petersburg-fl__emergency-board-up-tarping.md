## Why St. Petersburg Properties Face Repeated Board-Up and Tarping Needs

The Pinellas Peninsula's geography concentrates storm exposure in ways that inland Florida cities don't experience. St. Petersburg is flanked by open water on three sides, and that fetch, the unobstructed distance wind travels across water before hitting land, amplifies storm surge and wind speed at landfall. Neighborhoods built close to the waterfront absorb the first wave of damage; neighborhoods further inland still face debris impact and wind-driven rain that finds every gap in a roof or wall.

The city's housing stock adds another layer of vulnerability. A significant portion of St. Petersburg's residential and commercial buildings were constructed before modern hurricane-resistant building codes took effect in Florida. Older wood-frame structures with original single-pane windows and aging roof decking are far more susceptible to wind damage than newer construction built to post-2002 Florida Building Code standards. When a storm strips shingles or pushes in a window on a 1940s bungalow, the exposed framing and interior can absorb damaging moisture in a matter of hours in the area's high-humidity climate.

Fire and break-in events add to the board-up workload year-round. A structure fire that blows out windows or compromises a roof section needs immediate closure to prevent water intrusion from afternoon thunderstorms, a near-daily occurrence in St. Petersburg's summer months. Vacant properties and commercial storefronts also face periodic vandalism that requires rapid window boarding to satisfy both insurance requirements and city code.

## Our Emergency Board-Up and Tarping Process in St. Petersburg

When a crew arrives on site, the first step is a walk-around assessment to identify every breach point, not just the obvious ones. Wind events routinely lift flashing, crack soffit panels, and open seams at roof penetrations that aren't visible from the street. Missing those secondary openings means moisture finds its way in even after the primary damage is covered.

For roof tarping, we use reinforced polyethylene tarps rated for high-wind conditions, secured with baton strips screwed into the decking rather than simply weighted at the edges. In St. Petersburg's storm environment, a tarp that isn't mechanically fastened is a tarp that becomes a projectile in the next squall. We run the tarp past the ridge and down the opposite slope where possible, eliminating the ridge as a water entry point.

For window and door board-up, we cut plywood to fit each opening and fasten it with structural screws rather than nails, which back out under repeated wind pressure. On older masonry block construction, common in St. Petersburg's mid-century commercial corridors, we use appropriate anchoring methods for the substrate rather than assuming wood-frame fastening will work.

Documentation runs parallel to every step: photos of the damage before we touch it, photos of each covered opening, and a written scope that your insurance adjuster can use directly.

## St. Petersburg Insurance and HOA Coordination

Most standard homeowners' and commercial property policies in Florida cover emergency protective measures, board-up and tarping, as part of the loss itself, not as a separate out-of-pocket expense. We document the damage and the protective work in a format that aligns with what carriers expect, which reduces back-and-forth during the claims process.

For properties governed by a homeowners' association, temporary board-up and tarping typically fall under emergency repair exceptions in most HOA governing documents, meaning you don't need prior approval to protect the structure. That said, some associations have specific requirements about material color or fastening methods for anything visible from the street. If you know your HOA has particular rules, let us know when you call, we can often accommodate them without slowing the work.

## Local Note

St. Petersburg's older Craftsman and Mediterranean Revival bungalows, many of them built in the 1920s through 1940s in the city's historic residential neighborhoods, frequently have clay tile roofs. Clay tile holds up well in moderate wind but sheds in large sections under significant gusts, and the underlayment beneath it degrades over decades. When tile is displaced, the exposed underlayment is often brittle and tears easily, which means tarping over a tile roof requires extra care to avoid creating new punctures while covering the existing damage. Crews unfamiliar with that substrate sometimes cause secondary damage by walking the roof without appropriate staging. We treat tile-roof tarping as a distinct procedure, not a variation on standard shingle-roof work.
