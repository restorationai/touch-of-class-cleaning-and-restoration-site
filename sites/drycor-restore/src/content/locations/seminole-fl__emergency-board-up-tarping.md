## Why Seminole Properties Face Elevated Board-Up and Tarping Needs

Pinellas County's barrier-island geography creates wind and surge conditions that are more aggressive than what inland Hillsborough properties typically see. Seminole's housing stock reflects that exposure: many homes were built in the 1960s and 1970s, when single-pane jalousie windows and hip-roof framing were standard. Jalousie windows fail at relatively low wind speeds, and once the louvers blow out, rain enters horizontally. A standard window board-up on a modern double-pane frame is straightforward; boarding a jalousie opening requires cutting plywood to fit the full frame rather than just the glass plane, which takes longer and demands more material.

Fire damage board-up calls follow a different pattern here. The density of attached villas and townhome communities along the Seminole corridor means that a fire in one unit can expose the adjacent unit's shared wall or attic space to the elements before the fire marshal clears the scene. Securing those shared openings quickly is not optional, it protects neighboring property and satisfies the insurer's duty-to-mitigate language in most policies.

## Our Emergency Board-Up and Tarping Process in Seminole

Every call starts the same way: a brief phone assessment to understand the type of opening (window, door, roof breach, or structural gap), the approximate size, and whether the structure is safe to enter. That information lets the crew load the right materials before leaving, rather than making a second trip.

On arrival, the crew photographs the damage in its pre-secured condition, date-stamped images that become part of the insurance documentation package. Plywood panels are cut to overlap the structural framing, not just the opening, and fastened with screws rather than nails so removal during reconstruction does not split the surrounding material. Roof tarping follows a ridge-anchor method: the tarp runs over the peak and is secured on both sides of the slope, which prevents wind from catching the edge and peeling it back during a second weather event. In Seminole, where afternoon thunderstorms can follow a morning hurricane remnant by only a few hours, that edge-anchor detail matters.

## Local Note

Seminole's older concrete-block homes, common in the neighborhoods built out during the 1960s construction boom, present a specific challenge for window board-up: the original steel-framed jalousie and awning window units were often set directly into the masonry with a mortar bed rather than a wood buck. When those frames corrode and fail, there is no wood nailer to fasten plywood against.
