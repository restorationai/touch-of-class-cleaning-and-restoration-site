## Why Largo Properties See Distinctive Storm Damage

Largo's housing stock tells the story. A significant portion of the city's residential neighborhoods were built during the 1960s and 1970s Florida construction boom, when concrete block was the dominant material but roofing systems, windows, and soffit details were far less wind-resistant than today's code requires. When a tropical storm or hurricane pushes through, those older roof-to-wall connections and single-pane jalousie windows, still common in the city's mid-century neighborhoods, become the first points of failure.

The city's relatively flat terrain and high water table mean that once storm surge or heavy rainfall overwhelms drainage, water has nowhere to go quickly. Largo's soil profile doesn't absorb standing water the way higher-elevation inland areas might, so moisture lingers under and around slab foundations long after the skies clear. That extended ground saturation is one reason mold colonization can begin within 24 to 48 hours of a storm event here, faster than many homeowners expect.

Tall, mature trees, a defining feature of many Largo residential streets, become liabilities during high-wind events. Slash pine and laurel oak root systems are relatively shallow in Pinellas County's sandy soil, making wind-throws and large-limb failures common during tropical systems. A fallen tree doesn't just damage the roof; it creates an open envelope that exposes the interior to rain intrusion for however long it takes to get a tarp in place.

## Our Storm Damage Restoration Process in Largo

The first priority after any storm event is stopping ongoing intrusion. Our crews arrive with tarping materials and board-up supplies to close the building envelope before any interior work begins, an open roof in Largo's humidity is an invitation for accelerated mold growth. Once the structure is secured, we conduct a full moisture mapping of the affected areas using thermal imaging and calibrated moisture meters, because water that enters through a compromised roof or wall cavity travels further than the visible stain suggests.

Debris removal, downed branches, displaced roofing material, shattered glass, is handled as part of the initial assessment so the crew can safely access all affected zones. From there, the process follows the IICRC S500 and S520 standards for water and microbial damage, which means drying is documented with daily readings rather than estimated by feel. Structural drying equipment, desiccant dehumidifiers, high-velocity air movers, is placed based on the moisture map, not by guesswork.

For homes with wind-driven water behind stucco or block walls, we use non-invasive moisture detection first; controlled drying ports or limited material removal is a last resort, not a first response. Every step is photographed and logged for the insurance claim file.
