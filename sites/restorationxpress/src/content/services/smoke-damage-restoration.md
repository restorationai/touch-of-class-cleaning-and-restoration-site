Even after the flames are out and the fire department leaves, smoke keeps working. In the first 24 to 72 hours after a fire, acidic soot particles etch into porous surfaces — drywall, wood trim, upholstery, grout — and the oily residue from burned synthetics bonds to whatever it touches. What smells like a campfire on day one smells like a chemical plant by day three. Smoke damage restoration is the specialized process of stopping that progression, neutralizing odor at the molecular level, and recovering materials that look, at first glance, like they should be thrown away.

## What Smoke Damage Restoration actually involves

Smoke damage is not one problem — it's several that overlap. There are at least four distinct soot types: dry soot from fast-burning paper or wood, wet soot from slow smoldering fires, protein residue from kitchen fires (nearly invisible but intensely odorous), and synthetic soot from burned plastics, wiring insulation, or foam. Each type responds to different chemistry and different application methods. Dry soot can be dry-sponged off painted walls; wet soot requires alkaline detergent solutions applied with controlled agitation. Protein residue — the kind left by a stovetop grease fire — requires enzymatic cleaners because it bonds to surfaces at a molecular level and resists standard degreasers.

Beyond surfaces, smoke travels. It infiltrates HVAC ductwork, settles inside electrical boxes, and migrates into wall cavities through gaps around outlets and light fixtures. In South Florida homes — many built with concrete block and stucco construction — smoke can also penetrate through the porous texture of interior stucco finishes in ways that aren't immediately visible. Restoration technicians use thermal imaging and air quality meters to map where smoke has traveled, not just where it's visible.

Timeline matters. Porous materials like drywall and unfinished wood begin absorbing soot within hours. Metals start to tarnish and pit from acidic residue within 24 to 48 hours. Chrome fixtures and appliances can show permanent etching within 72 hours if untreated. The faster professional cleanup begins, the more material can be saved rather than replaced.

## Our process

1. **Scope and soot characterization.** Before any cleaning begins, the affected areas are inspected to identify soot type, smoke migration pathways, and the condition of structural materials. This step determines which cleaning agents and methods are appropriate for each surface — and documents pre-existing conditions for the insurance file.

2. **Containment and HVAC isolation.** To prevent cross-contamination during cleaning, HVAC systems are shut down and registers are sealed. If the system pulled smoke during or after the fire, duct cleaning is added to the scope. Clean areas of the structure are separated from affected zones using poly sheeting barriers.

3. **Surface decontamination.** Working from ceiling to floor and from structural surfaces outward, technicians clean each surface using the method matched to the soot type present — dry chemical sponges, alkaline cleaning solutions, or enzymatic treatments for protein residue. Heavily saturated materials that cannot be cleaned to an acceptable standard are documented for replacement rather than treated repeatedly.

4. **Thermal fogging and/or hydroxyl treatment.** Surface cleaning removes visible residue, but odor molecules have penetrated into wall cavities, subflooring, and soft goods. Thermal fogging — which produces a deodorizing vapor that follows the same pathways smoke traveled — neutralizes embedded odor at the source. For occupied or sensitive environments, hydroxyl generators are used as an alternative: they produce hydroxyl radicals that break down odor compounds without requiring evacuation.

5. **Clearance verification and contents assessment.** After treatment, air quality readings and surface wipe tests confirm that residue levels are within acceptable limits. Contents — furniture, clothing, documents, electronics — are assessed individually. Items that can be restored are packed out for professional cleaning; items that cannot are photographed and inventoried for the insurance claim.

## What separates a good smoke damage response from a bad one

The most common mistake in smoke cleanup is treating it like a cleaning job rather than a restoration job. Painting over soot without proper surface preparation is the most frequent failure point — the odor bleeds through within weeks, and the soot chemistry continues to degrade the paint layer. Encapsulation only works after thorough mechanical cleaning and the correct primer system.

A second common gap is incomplete smoke mapping. Technicians who clean only what's visibly sooty will miss the smoke that migrated through the HVAC system or into adjacent rooms. Insurance adjusters — especially those working large fire losses — look for documentation showing that smoke migration was assessed, not just assumed to stop at the room of origin.

Protein soot from kitchen fires is routinely underestimated. Because it leaves little visible residue, it's sometimes dismissed as a minor odor issue. In practice, protein residue requires full enzymatic treatment of every surface in the affected area, including cabinet interiors, range hood ductwork, and ceiling surfaces above the cooking area.

Finally, odor neutralization and odor masking are not the same thing. Spray deodorizers and scented products cover odor temporarily. Thermal fogging, hydroxyl treatment, and ozone treatment (in unoccupied spaces) break down the odor compounds chemically. A home that smells fine at the end of cleanup but returns to smelling like smoke within two weeks was masked, not restored.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

South Florida's climate creates specific smoke damage dynamics worth understanding. High ambient humidity — routinely above 70% relative humidity in Broward County from June through October — accelerates the bonding of wet soot to surfaces and promotes secondary mold growth in areas where fire suppression water was used. Smoke damage jobs that begin more than 48 hours after a loss in summer months frequently require moisture assessment alongside soot cleanup, because mold colonization can begin in as little as 24 to 48 hours in these conditions.

Wildfire smoke is a growing concern even in South Florida. When regional wildfires burn in the Everglades or along the urban-wildland interface west of Davie and Weston, fine particulate smoke infiltrates homes through HVAC systems and building envelope gaps. This type of loss typically involves no structural soot but leaves a persistent odor and elevated particulate levels indoors — a scenario that requires air quality documentation and targeted deodorization rather than surface cleaning.
