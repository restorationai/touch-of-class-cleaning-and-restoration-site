A house fire leaves behind more than charred walls. Even after the flames are out and the fire trucks are gone, you're dealing with smoke-saturated insulation, soot embedded in every porous surface, water damage from suppression efforts, and structural materials that may look intact but aren't. The restoration process typically unfolds in six distinct phases — and understanding each one helps you ask the right questions, avoid costly mistakes, and set realistic expectations for how long it takes to get your home back.

## What Happens in the First 24 Hours

The period immediately after a fire is controlled is the most consequential window for limiting secondary damage. Here's what should happen — and roughly in this order:

1. **Get a safety clearance before re-entering.** The fire department or a structural engineer needs to confirm the building is safe. Smoke-weakened floor joists and compromised load-bearing walls aren't always visible.
2. **Contact your insurance company.** File your claim as soon as possible. Most policies require prompt notice, and your insurer may need to authorize work before it begins.
3. **Document everything before anything is moved or cleaned.** Walk through with your phone and photograph every room — ceiling to floor. Capture serial numbers on appliances, the condition of furniture, and any structural damage. This documentation is your evidence for the claim.
4. **Board up windows and doors; tarp the roof if it's breached.** This is called emergency board-up and tarping. It prevents rain intrusion, keeps out animals, and reduces liability. A restoration contractor can handle this same day.
5. **Do not run the HVAC system.** Soot particles are fine enough to travel through ductwork and coat every surface in rooms that weren't directly affected. Running the system redistributes contamination.

In South Florida's humid climate, there's an added urgency: the combination of fire suppression water and Broward County's baseline humidity creates ideal conditions for mold colonization within 24–48 hours. Drying needs to start fast.

## Phase 1 – Assessment and Scope of Loss

Before any cleaning or demolition begins, a trained technician walks the entire property to map the damage. This isn't a quick visual scan — it includes moisture readings in walls and subfloors (suppression water migrates farther than it looks), air quality sampling in some cases, and a category assessment of what can be restored versus what needs to be replaced.

The scope of loss document that comes out of this assessment drives everything: the insurance estimate, the materials order, the subcontractor schedule. A thorough assessment at this stage prevents expensive change orders later when a contractor discovers wet framing behind drywall that wasn't accounted for.

## Phase 2 – Water Extraction and Structural Drying

This surprises most homeowners: a significant portion of early fire restoration work is actually water damage restoration. Firefighting hoses deliver hundreds of gallons per minute. That water saturates flooring, wicks into wall cavities, and pools in basements or crawl spaces.

Extraction comes first — truck-mounted vacuums pull standing water from hard surfaces. Then industrial air movers and dehumidifiers run continuously, often for three to five days, to bring structural moisture content down to acceptable levels. Technicians monitor readings daily with pin and pinless moisture meters. Drying isn't finished when the surface feels dry; it's finished when the framing and subfloor readings are within normal range for the region.

Skipping or rushing this phase is one of the most common reasons fire-damaged homes develop mold problems months after the restoration appears complete.

## Phase 3 – Smoke and Soot Removal

This is the phase most people picture when they think about fire restoration, and it's more technically involved than scrubbing walls.

Soot behaves differently depending on what burned. Protein fires (kitchen fires involving food or grease) leave a nearly invisible, extremely pungent residue that bonds tightly to painted surfaces. Natural material fires (wood, paper, cotton) produce dry, powdery soot that smears if wiped incorrectly. Synthetic material fires — foam furniture, plastics, electrical wiring — generate oily, sticky soot that carries toxic compounds and requires chemical sponges and specific cleaning agents.

The process typically includes:

- **Dry soot removal** using chemical sponges on ceilings and walls before any wet cleaning — wiping wet soot into a dry surface permanently stains it.
- **HEPA vacuuming** of all surfaces, including inside cabinets, light fixtures, and HVAC registers.
- **Thermal fogging or ozone treatment** to neutralize odor molecules that have penetrated porous materials like wood framing, drywall, and insulation.
- **Duct cleaning**, because smoke travels through return air pathways even when the system is off.
- **Content cleaning**, which may happen on-site or at a specialized facility for items like electronics, documents, and clothing.

Smoke odor that isn't fully addressed at this stage will resurface — especially in Florida's heat, which accelerates off-gassing from affected materials.

## Phase 4 – Demolition and Structural Repair

Materials that are too contaminated or structurally compromised to restore get removed in a controlled demolition. This includes charred framing, melted wiring, smoke-saturated insulation, and any drywall that absorbed suppression water or soot beyond cleaning thresholds.

Once the structure is clean and dry down to its bones, reconstruction begins. Depending on the extent of the fire, this ranges from patching drywall and repainting a single room to full framing replacement, new electrical rough-in, and a complete interior rebuild. In older Davie homes — particularly those built in the 1970s and 1980s with aluminum wiring — an electrician's involvement is often required before any drywall goes back up.

This phase is where the timeline varies most widely. A contained kitchen fire might reach move-in condition in three to four weeks. A fire that spread to multiple rooms or the attic can take three to six months.

## What Not to Do After a House Fire

A few actions consistently make fire damage worse:

- **Don't wipe soot with a wet cloth.** It smears and sets into the surface permanently.
- **Don't turn on ceiling fans or the HVAC.** Air movement spreads fine soot particles to unaffected areas.
- **Don't discard anything before the insurance adjuster visits** — even items that look unsalvageable. Your adjuster needs to see the damage in place.
- **Don't attempt to clean electronics yourself.** Soot is conductive and corrosive; improper cleaning causes short circuits.
- **Don't assume the smell will air out on its own.** Smoke odor molecules bond chemically to surfaces. Ventilation alone doesn't remove them.

## When the Job Is Done: What Full Restoration Looks Like

A completed fire restoration isn't just a repaired structure — it's a property that passes a clearance inspection, has documented moisture readings within normal range, and has no detectable smoke odor under normal temperature conditions. Your contractor should provide a final walk-through, a project completion report, and documentation that supports your insurance file.

If your home had pre-existing conditions uncovered during the work — old plumbing, inadequate ventilation, prior water damage — a reputable contractor will flag those separately rather than fold them into the fire claim.
