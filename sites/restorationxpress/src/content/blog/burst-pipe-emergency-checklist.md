When a pipe bursts, you have roughly minutes — not hours — before water migrates into wall cavities, soaks subfloor, and sets the stage for mold. The short version: shut off the water at the main valve, cut power to affected rooms at the breaker, document everything with your phone camera, then start removing standing water. The steps below walk through exactly how to do each of those things, what mistakes to skip, and how to know when the damage has moved beyond DIY territory.

## Step-by-Step: What to Do the Moment a Pipe Bursts

Work through this in order. Each step matters, and skipping ahead can make the next one harder.

1. **Shut off the main water supply.** Don't hunt for the individual shutoff near the burst pipe — go straight to the main. In most Davie homes built before the 1990s, it's near the meter at the street or on an exterior wall. Turn it clockwise until it stops. Water should slow within seconds.

2. **Turn off electricity to affected rooms.** Water and live circuits are a serious hazard. Go to your breaker panel and kill power to any room where water is visible on the floor or ceiling. If the panel itself is in a flooded area, don't touch it — call the utility (FPL for most of Broward County) and have them cut service at the meter.

3. **Open faucets to drain remaining pressure.** After the main is off, open the lowest faucet in the house — usually a hose bib or a bathroom sink on the ground floor. This bleeds residual pressure from the lines and reduces how much water continues draining out of the break.

4. **Photograph and video everything before you touch it.** Walk every affected room. Shoot wide angles showing the room context, then close-ups of the source, the waterline on walls, and any wet materials. This documentation is your insurance claim — the more thorough it is now, the less you'll argue about later.

5. **Remove standing water as fast as possible.** A wet-dry shop vac handles smaller volumes well. For anything more than an inch or two across a large area, a submersible pump speeds things up significantly. The faster you remove bulk water, the less it wicks into porous materials.

6. **Pull up saturated rugs and move wet furniture.** Rugs trap moisture against flooring for days. Get them outside or into a garage. Upholstered furniture sitting in water begins to grow mold in 24–48 hours in South Florida's humidity — move it to a dry area and prop cushions up so air can circulate.

7. **Ventilate the space.** Open windows if outdoor humidity is lower than indoor (not always the case in South Florida summers — check your phone's weather app). Run ceiling fans. If you have box fans, position them to push air across wet surfaces toward an exit.

## What NOT to Do After a Burst Pipe

A few common instincts actually make things worse:

- **Don't use a standard household vacuum on standing water.** They aren't sealed for liquids and can electrocute you or destroy the motor.
- **Don't assume the visible wet area is the full extent of the damage.** Water travels. A burst pipe in a second-floor bathroom can show up as a ceiling stain two rooms away. If you see a waterline on one wall, probe adjacent walls with your hand — cool, soft drywall means moisture has already migrated.
- **Don't crank the heat to dry things faster.** High heat without airflow just raises humidity in the room and accelerates mold growth. Dehumidification is what dries a structure — heat alone doesn't accomplish that.
- **Don't discard damaged materials before your adjuster or a restoration tech has seen them.** Throwing out saturated drywall before documentation can create disputes about the scope of the loss.
- **Don't ignore a musty smell that develops within a day or two.** In Davie's climate, mold can begin colonizing wet drywall and insulation within 24–48 hours of a water event. A smell that early is a sign the drying process isn't keeping up.

## When the Damage Is Beyond DIY

Some burst pipe situations are genuinely manageable with a shop vac and a few fans. Others look manageable on the surface and aren't. Here's how to tell the difference:

**Call a professional if any of these apply:**

- Water has been sitting for more than a few hours before you found it
- The affected area is larger than one room, or water has reached multiple floors
- You can see or smell mold already — even small patches
- The burst pipe is inside a wall, ceiling, or under a slab
- Flooring is buckled, soft, or separating at seams (a sign the subfloor is saturated)
- The water source was a drain line rather than a supply line (drain water carries bacteria)
- You're not sure whether drying is actually working after 24 hours

A professional water damage assessment uses moisture meters and thermal imaging cameras to map exactly where water has traveled — including inside wall cavities and under flooring where you can't see it. That matters because insurance documentation requires measured moisture readings, not visual estimates.

## The Recovery Process: What Happens After the Emergency

Once the immediate crisis is handled, you're entering a multi-day drying and restoration process. Here's roughly what that looks like:

**Days 1–2: Structural drying begins.** Industrial air movers and dehumidifiers run continuously. In South Florida's climate, professional equipment is typically sized to the square footage and adjusted based on daily moisture readings. Consumer-grade fans and box store dehumidifiers often can't keep up with the vapor load in a significantly wet space.

**Days 2–4: Monitoring and adjustments.** A restoration technician returns daily to take moisture readings and reposition equipment as wet zones shift. Drywall and framing that read above acceptable moisture thresholds get additional drying time before any reconstruction begins.

**Days 3–5 (or longer for larger losses): Demolition of unsalvageable materials.** Drywall that stayed wet too long, insulation that's saturated, flooring that has buckled — these come out. The goal is to expose the framing so it can dry completely before anything is closed back up.

**Reconstruction.** Once moisture readings confirm the structure is dry, reconstruction begins: new drywall, insulation, flooring, paint. For appliance-related leaks — a dishwasher supply line, a refrigerator ice maker connection, a washing machine hose — the appliance itself may need inspection or replacement before the space is closed back in.

If your water damage originated from an appliance rather than a supply line in the wall, the recovery process is similar but the source needs to be addressed and confirmed repaired before any reconstruction happens.
