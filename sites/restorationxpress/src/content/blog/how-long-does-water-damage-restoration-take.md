Most water damage restoration jobs take **3 to 5 days** from the moment a crew arrives to the moment the structure is dry enough for repairs. But that number can stretch to 2–3 weeks — or compress to 48 hours — depending on how much water got in, how long it sat, and what materials it soaked into. The single biggest factor in your timeline isn't the size of the flood. It's how fast extraction and drying start. Every hour standing water sits in your home, it migrates deeper into subfloors, wall cavities, and insulation — and the clock on potential mold growth starts at around 24–48 hours.

## What Actually Controls the Timeline

Restoration isn't one job — it's a sequence of jobs, and each phase has its own minimum time requirement that no amount of extra equipment can fully shortcut.

**Phase 1 — Water extraction (hours 1–6):** Truck-mounted extractors and portable units pull standing water fast. A flooded 1,200-square-foot first floor can be extracted in 2–4 hours. This phase ends quickly.

**Phase 2 — Structural drying (days 2–5, sometimes longer):** This is where most of the timeline lives. Industrial air movers and dehumidifiers run continuously, and a technician returns daily to read moisture meters embedded in walls, floors, and ceilings. Drying is not complete when surfaces *feel* dry — it's complete when moisture readings return to the baseline for your local climate. In South Florida, that baseline is higher than in a dry climate, which means equipment sometimes needs to run a day or two longer than the same job would in, say, Phoenix.

**Phase 3 — Demolition of unsalvageable material (variable):** Drywall that absorbed water past a certain threshold, wet insulation, and swollen hardwood flooring often have to come out before drying can even complete properly. If a technician cuts a 12-inch flood cut in your drywall on day one, that's not destruction — it's ventilation. It lets the wall cavity dry. Reconstruction doesn't start until the structure is verified dry.

**Phase 4 — Reconstruction (days 7–21+):** Hanging new drywall, taping, mudding, painting, reinstalling flooring — this phase runs on a normal contractor schedule and depends on material availability, permit requirements, and scope. A single bathroom can be rebuilt in a week. A finished basement with custom flooring and built-ins can take three weeks or more.

## Category of Water Matters More Than Volume

Restoration professionals classify water damage into three categories, and the category affects both the timeline and the approach.

- **Category 1 (clean water):** A burst supply line, an overflowing sink, a malfunctioning appliance. Drying timelines are shortest here — often 3–4 days if caught early.
- **Category 2 (gray water):** Washing machine discharge, dishwasher overflow, toilet overflow without solid waste. Contains contaminants. Affected materials need more aggressive treatment, and some porous materials (carpet padding, certain drywall) are typically removed rather than dried in place.
- **Category 3 (black water):** Sewage backup, rising floodwater from outside, any water that's been sitting long enough to become grossly contaminated. This category requires full protective protocols, and the timeline extends because contaminated materials must be removed and disposed of safely before any drying begins.

A Category 3 loss in a finished basement can easily run 2–3 weeks from extraction to a reconstruction-ready shell.

## The Hidden Variable: How Long the Water Sat Before Anyone Noticed

A slow leak behind a bathroom wall in a Davie home can run for weeks before a homeowner notices a soft spot in the floor or a musty smell in the hallway. By that point, the framing, subfloor, and insulation may have been wet long enough that mold has already colonized.

When that's the case, the timeline isn't just a drying timeline anymore — it's a mold remediation timeline layered on top of the water damage timeline. Remediation requires containment, HEPA air scrubbing, removal of contaminated materials, and clearance testing before reconstruction can begin. That adds days, sometimes a full week, to the project.

If you're seeing discoloration on drywall, a persistent musty odor, or warped baseboards in a room that had a leak months ago, assume mold is present until testing says otherwise.

## What You Can Do Right Now (Before a Crew Arrives)

If you're dealing with an active leak or recent flooding, these steps reduce the total restoration timeline:

1. **Stop the water source.** Find your main shutoff valve — in most Broward County homes it's near the meter at the street or on the side of the house — and close it. If the source is an appliance, pull it away from the wall and close the supply valve behind it.
2. **Remove standing water with whatever you have.** Towels, a wet/dry shop vac, buckets. You won't get it all, but every gallon you remove is a gallon that isn't soaking into your subfloor.
3. **Move furniture and rugs off wet flooring.** Wet rugs trap moisture against hardwood and tile, accelerating damage. Furniture legs can stain and warp flooring. Get them onto dry ground or elevate them on foil blocks.
4. **Open interior doors and run fans if the air outside is less humid than inside.** In Florida, this is often not the case — outdoor air in summer is frequently more humid than conditioned indoor air, so opening windows can actually slow drying. When in doubt, keep the AC running and let it dehumidify.
5. **Document everything with photos and video before you move anything.** Your insurance adjuster will need this, and so will any restoration crew that comes in.

Do not use a standard household vacuum on standing water. Do not run a ceiling fan if the ceiling above it is wet — a saturated ceiling can fail. And do not assume the damage is limited to what you can see.

## When to Call a Professional

Call a water damage restoration company if any of the following apply:

- The affected area is larger than roughly 10 square feet
- The water came from a contaminated source (sewage, outdoor flooding)
- The water reached drywall, insulation, or subfloor
- You can smell a musty or earthy odor anywhere in the affected area
- The loss happened more than 24 hours ago and you haven't started drying
- You're filing an insurance claim

For smaller clean-water events — a glass knocked over, a minor drip caught immediately — consumer-grade fans and a dehumidifier may genuinely be sufficient. But the threshold for calling in professional equipment is lower than most people think, because the cost of inadequate drying (mold, structural rot, a failed insurance claim) is almost always higher than the cost of the initial restoration.

## The Full Recovery Arc

Here's a realistic end-to-end timeline for a moderate water loss — say, a supply line failure behind a kitchen wall that went unnoticed for several hours:

- **Day 1:** Extraction, initial moisture mapping, placement of drying equipment, flood cuts if needed
- **Days 2–4:** Daily moisture readings, equipment adjustments, documentation for insurance
- **Day 4–5:** Final moisture verification, equipment removal, scope of repairs written
- **Week 2:** Reconstruction begins (drywall, paint, flooring)
- **Week 3:** Final walkthrough, punch list, project close

A more severe loss — a burst pipe discovered after a vacation, or a Category 3 sewage backup — can add a full week or more at every phase.

If you're in the middle of this process and the timeline feels unclear, that's worth a direct conversation with your restoration crew. A reputable company will show you the moisture readings each day and explain exactly what the numbers mean for your drying schedule.
