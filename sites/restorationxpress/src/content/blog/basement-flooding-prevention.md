South Florida basements are rare, but if your Davie home has a below-grade space, a utility room, or even a low-lying slab area that collects water, you already know how fast a heavy afternoon storm can turn into a soggy disaster. The good news: most basement and below-grade flooding is preventable with a handful of targeted fixes done before the wet season peaks. Here are eight specific things you can do right now — before the next band of rain rolls in off the Atlantic.

## 1. Regrade the Soil Around Your Foundation

Water follows gravity. If the ground around your foundation slopes *toward* the house rather than away from it, every rainstorm is essentially funneling runoff straight to your walls. Walk the perimeter of your home after a dry spell and look for low spots or soil that has settled against the foundation. The fix is straightforward: add fill dirt to create a slope that drops at least six inches over the first ten feet away from the house. This single change eliminates one of the most common entry points for water.

## 2. Clean and Extend Your Downspouts

Clogged gutters overflow and dump water at the base of your foundation. Disconnected or short downspouts do the same thing in a more concentrated stream. Clean gutters at least twice a year — more often if you have oak or pine trees nearby. Then check where each downspout terminates. Extensions should carry water at least four to six feet away from the foundation, and ideally direct it toward a swale or drainage area. In Davie, where afternoon thunderstorms can drop two inches of rain in under an hour, a downspout that ends six inches from your wall can overwhelm even a good waterproofing job.

## 3. Inspect and Seal Foundation Cracks

Hairline cracks in poured concrete or concrete block walls are common, especially in homes that have gone through years of Florida's wet-dry seasonal cycles. Not every crack leaks, but every crack is a potential entry point. Run your hand along the wall after a heavy rain — if the concrete feels damp or you see efflorescence (the white chalky mineral deposits left behind when water evaporates), water is moving through. Small cracks can be filled with hydraulic cement or a polyurethane injection kit available at any home improvement store. Larger cracks — anything wider than a quarter-inch, or cracks that run diagonally from window corners — warrant a professional evaluation before you seal them, because they may indicate structural movement.

## 4. Install or Test a Sump Pump

If your below-grade space already has a sump pit, test the pump before the season starts. Pour a five-gallon bucket of water into the pit and watch the float trigger the pump. If it doesn't kick on within a few seconds, the float switch may be stuck or the pump may have failed. Sump pumps typically last seven to ten years; if yours is older than that, replacement is cheaper than a flooded room.

If you don't have a sump pump and your space has flooded before, installation is a worthwhile investment. Pair it with a battery backup unit — Broward County power outages during storms are common, and a pump that loses power at the exact moment you need it most is useless.

## 5. Check Your Window Wells

Below-grade windows need window wells to keep soil away from the glass, but those wells can fill with debris and become miniature retention ponds during heavy rain. Clear them of leaves and dirt, and make sure the gravel at the bottom is draining freely. If a well consistently fills with water, you can add a window well cover (they're inexpensive and snap into place) or install a small drain at the base that ties into your interior drainage system.

## 6. Waterproof Interior Walls With Masonry Sealer

Interior waterproofing products — masonry sealers, crystalline coatings, and drain-and-seal systems — don't fix the source of water intrusion, but they can slow seepage and protect stored belongings while you address the root cause. Apply them to clean, dry concrete or block walls according to the manufacturer's instructions. Keep in mind that these products work best as a supplement to exterior grading and drainage corrections, not as a substitute for them. If you're seeing active water flowing in rather than mild seepage, a sealer alone won't hold.

## 7. Maintain Your Plumbing and Appliances

Not all basement flooding comes from outside. A slow drip from a water heater, a cracked washing machine hose, or a backed-up floor drain can cause just as much damage as a storm. Inspect supply lines to any appliances in or near the below-grade space — look for corrosion, mineral buildup, or soft spots in the hose material. Braided stainless steel supply lines are more durable than plain rubber and worth the upgrade. Check the pressure relief valve on your water heater annually; a valve that's been leaking slowly can saturate the surrounding concrete without being obvious until the smell of mold sets in.

## 8. Know Where Your Main Shutoff Is

This one sounds basic, but it matters. If a pipe bursts or an appliance fails during a storm when you're distracted, every second you spend searching for the shutoff valve is more water on the floor. Locate the main water shutoff — in most Davie homes it's near the water meter at the front of the property or in a utility closet — and make sure every adult in the household knows where it is and how to operate it. Label it with a piece of tape if it isn't already marked.

## What to Do If You Still Get Water

Even with all eight of these measures in place, a severe storm or unexpected plumbing failure can still result in standing water. If that happens, the clock matters more than most people realize. Mold can begin colonizing wet drywall, insulation, and wood framing within 24 to 48 hours in South Florida's humidity — faster than in most of the country. Removing standing water quickly, running dehumidifiers, and drying structural materials thoroughly are the steps that separate a manageable cleanup from a mold remediation project.

If the water is more than a surface puddle, if it's touched drywall or insulation, or if you're not sure where it came from, it's worth calling a water damage restoration professional before you start tearing anything out. Restoration teams use moisture meters and thermal cameras to find water that's hidden inside walls and under flooring — the kind you can't see but that causes the most damage over time.
