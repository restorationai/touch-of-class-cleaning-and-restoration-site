Fort Lauderdale sits at the crossroads of Atlantic storm tracks and Broward County's flat, low-lying terrain — a combination that turns a single hurricane or fast-moving tropical system into a multi-front damage event. When a storm tears through the waterfront neighborhoods along Las Olas Isles or pushes surge water into the older bungalows of Victoria Park, the damage rarely stops at a broken window or a missing roof panel. Wind, water, and debris work together, and the clock starts the moment the rain stops.

## Why Fort Lauderdale Properties Face Distinct Storm Damage Challenges

The city's geography makes storm damage here different from what you'd see inland. Much of Fort Lauderdale — ZIP codes like 33301 and 33316 near Port Everglades — sits within a few feet of sea level. Soil saturation happens fast, and once the ground is full, there's nowhere for stormwater to drain. That standing water migrates under slab foundations, into crawlspaces, and through the weep screed of stucco exteriors before most homeowners realize the structure itself is absorbing moisture.

The housing stock adds another layer of complexity. Coral Ridge and Rio Vista are lined with mid-century concrete block homes built before modern wind-load codes. Those walls are durable in many ways, but their older window and door assemblies — and the original single-ply roofing systems many still carry — are far more vulnerable to Category 1 and 2 wind speeds than newer construction. A storm that causes cosmetic damage to a 2015 build can drive water deep into a 1962 block home through gaps that aren't visible until you pull the drywall.

## Our Storm Damage Restoration Process in Fort Lauderdale

Every job starts with a thorough structural and moisture assessment before any debris is moved or materials are removed. Wind-driven rain hides in wall cavities, attic decking, and under flooring — especially in homes near Hugh Taylor Birch State Park where mature tree canopy means more roof punctures and debris impact than in open neighborhoods. We use thermal imaging and calibrated moisture meters to map the full extent of water intrusion, not just the visible wet spots.

From there, the process moves in a deliberate sequence:

- **Tarping and board-up** to stop additional water entry through compromised roofs, broken windows, and damaged siding
- **Controlled water extraction** from flooring, wall assemblies, and any below-grade spaces
- **Structural drying** using industrial air movers and dehumidifiers sized to the actual cubic footage of the affected area — not a one-size-fits-all setup
- **Debris and tree removal coordination** for fallen limbs and canopy damage, which is common along the residential streets running off Las Olas Boulevard
- **Damage documentation** with photographs, moisture readings, and written scope of loss for insurance submission

We don't close out a job until moisture readings return to normal baseline levels for South Florida's ambient humidity — a standard that matters more here than in a dry climate, because elevated readings that would be acceptable in Phoenix indicate active moisture problems in Broward County.

## Fort Lauderdale Insurance Coordination

Storm claims in Florida move through a specific process, and documentation quality directly affects settlement outcomes. Homeowners in flood-prone areas near the Intracoastal often carry both standard homeowners policies and separate NFIP flood policies — we help clarify which damage category applies to which policy so nothing falls through the gap between the two.

For properties in HOA-governed communities, we're familiar with the additional approval steps some associations require before exterior repairs begin. If your community has architectural review requirements, factor that into your timeline and let us know early — we can provide documentation that satisfies most HOA review boards without delaying the interior drying work.
