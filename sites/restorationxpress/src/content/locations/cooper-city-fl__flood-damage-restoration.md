## Why Cooper City Properties See Flood Damage Differently

Most of Cooper City's residential stock was built between the late 1970s and the mid-1990s, a period when South Florida construction relied heavily on concrete block exteriors paired with wood-framed interior walls and particleboard or OSB subfloors. Those materials behave predictably in a dry climate — but Cooper City isn't dry. Broward County averages roughly 62 inches of rain per year, most of it compressed into a June-through-October window. When a storm event pushes water under a slab or through a compromised door threshold, OSB subfloor panels begin swelling within the first few hours, and the paper facing on standard drywall starts delaminating not long after.

Cooper City also sits atop the Biscayne Aquifer, which means the water table is naturally high. After a significant rain event, groundwater can wick upward through slab cracks even after surface water has receded — a dynamic that catches homeowners off guard when they think the problem is solved. Homes near the retention ponds that line many of the community's planned developments, including those backing up to Brian Piccolo Park, are particularly exposed to this secondary saturation pattern.

## Our Flood Damage Restoration Process in Cooper City

The first thing we do on any flood call is a moisture mapping walk-through with thermal imaging and pin-type meters. In Cooper City's slab-on-grade homes, water travels laterally under flooring before it becomes visible — thermal imaging catches that hidden migration before it turns into a mold problem 72 hours later.

Once we've mapped the affected area, we extract standing water using truck-mounted and portable extraction units, then set a calibrated drying system — commercial-grade dehumidifiers and axial air movers positioned to create a directed airflow through walls and under flooring. Drying targets follow IICRC S500 guidelines, with daily moisture readings documented so you have a written record for your insurance claim. Affected materials that can't be dried in place — saturated insulation, swollen OSB, soaked carpet padding — are removed and properly disposed of before drying begins, because leaving them in place extends drying time and creates conditions where mold can colonize.

For Category 2 or Category 3 water intrusions (canal backflow, street flooding, sewage-contaminated water), we treat affected surfaces with EPA-registered antimicrobials before drying equipment goes in.
