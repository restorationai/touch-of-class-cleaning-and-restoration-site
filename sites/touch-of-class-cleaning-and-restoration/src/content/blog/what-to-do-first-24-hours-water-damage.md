If a pipe has burst, a water heater has failed, or you've come home to standing water, the first 24 hours determine how much damage you'll actually have to repair. The priority order is: stop the water source, cut power to any affected outlets or breakers, remove as much standing water as you can, and start air movement. Mold can begin colonizing wet drywall and wood within 24 to 48 hours, especially in a humid North Texas summer, so speed matters more than perfection. You don't need special equipment to get the first few hours right, just a clear head and the right sequence.

## Know What You're Dealing With

Water damage falls into rough categories, and the category changes what's safe to do yourself. A burst supply line or overflowing washing machine is usually "clean" water, at least initially. A sewage backup or water that's been sitting for more than a day or two is contaminated and should be treated as a biohazard, not a cleanup project. If the water came from outside, through a foundation crack, a window well, or storm runoff, assume it's carrying soil and bacteria even if it looks clear.

Richardson sits on expansive clay soils typical of the Dallas area, and a lot of the housing stock here was built on slab foundations from the 1960s through the 1980s. That combination means plumbing runs under the slab in many homes, so a slow leak can soak through concrete and carpet pad for days before anyone notices a damp spot or a musty smell. If you're seeing a stain on a slab-foundation home and you don't know how long it's been there, treat it more cautiously than a leak you watched happen.

## The First Steps, In Order

1. **Shut off the water source.** For a burst pipe or supply line, close the fixture's shutoff valve if there is one, or the home's main shutoff if you can't isolate it. Know where your main valve is before you need it; it's usually near the water meter or where the line enters the house.
2. **Cut power to the area if water is near outlets, switches, or appliances.** If reaching the breaker panel means walking through standing water, don't. Call an electrician or wait for help instead.
3. **Move anything you can lift.** Furniture legs, rugs, boxes, electronics, and anything with legal or sentimental value should come off a wet floor immediately. Wood furniture left sitting in water can swell and stain within hours.
4. **Start removing standing water.** A wet/dry shop vacuum handles small amounts well. For more than an inch or two across a room, towels and a mop will only slow you down; this is where a pump or professional extraction becomes worth it.
5. **Get air moving.** Open windows if the humidity outside is lower than inside, run box fans across wet flooring, and point them so air travels across the surface rather than just stirring the room. Run the AC if it's working; it pulls moisture out of the air as a side effect of cooling.
6. **Photograph everything before you move or discard it.** Wide shots of the room, close-ups of damaged materials, and photos of the source (the pipe, the appliance, the water line on the wall) all matter if you file an insurance claim.
7. **Check under and behind things that hide damage.** Baseboards, the bottom of cabinets, and carpet edges will show swelling or discoloration before the visible floor does. If the leak was behind drywall, look for a soft spot, a faint ring of discoloration, or paint that's started to bubble, that's the wall telling you the saturation goes deeper than the surface.

## What Not To Do

- **Don't run a regular household vacuum on standing water.** It's not built for it and you risk an electrical hazard.
- **Don't turn on ceiling fixtures or switches in a room with water on the ceiling or walls above.** Water and electricity sharing a space is a shock and fire risk.
- **Don't wait to see if it "dries on its own" before pulling up wet carpet pad.** Pad holds water against the subfloor far longer than the carpet itself shows, and it's usually cheaper to replace than to dry.
- **Don't assume insurance will cover everything regardless of what you do.** Most policies expect you to take reasonable steps to prevent further damage; leaving a known leak unaddressed for days can complicate a claim.
- **Don't walk through water if you're not sure it's clean,** especially if it has any odor, is near a bathroom or kitchen drain line, or has been sitting for more than 24 hours.

## When to Call a Professional

A shop vacuum and a couple of fans are enough for a small, caught-early leak with clean water and a few square feet of floor. Call a water damage restoration company when any of the following is true: the water covered more than a small area, it sat for more than a few hours before you found it, it came from a sewage line or outside floodwater, it reached a wall cavity or subfloor, or you're not confident the space is actually dry once it looks dry on the surface. Surface-dry and structurally dry are not the same thing, drywall and subfloor often hold moisture well after the carpet feels fine to the touch, which is exactly how hidden mold gets started.

A restoration technician uses moisture meters and thermal imaging to check what's happening inside walls and under flooring, not just what's visible. That matters because drying equipment placed in the wrong spot, or removed too early based on a visual check, leaves moisture behind to cause problems weeks later.

## What Happens After the First 24 Hours

Once the water is out and air is moving, the next few days are about verified drying, not guessing. Materials get monitored with moisture meters until readings return to a normal range for that material, not just until they look dry. Depending on how much water intruded and what it touched, this can mean commercial dehumidifiers and air movers running for several days, and in some cases removing wet drywall, insulation, or flooring that won't dry fully in place.

This is also when you'll want your insurance claim moving. Keep the photos from day one, hold onto receipts for anything you had to replace or discard, and ask for documentation of moisture readings throughout the drying process, that record is what carriers rely on to confirm the loss was handled correctly.
