## Do You Even Need a Test?

If you can see mold growing on a surface, you don't need a lab test to confirm what it is. Visible mold on drywall, grout, or wood trim should be treated as mold and addressed, regardless of the species. Testing earns its keep in a different situation: when you smell a musty odor but can't find the source, when a family member has unexplained respiratory irritation, when you're buying or selling a house, or when mold was remediated before and you want to confirm the air is clear afterward. In those cases, the question isn't just "is there mold" but "where, how much, and is it still active." That's where the difference between a drugstore kit and a professional inspection actually matters.

## How DIY Mold Test Kits Work

Most retail kits fall into two categories: petri dish swab kits you leave open on a shelf for several days, and tape-lift or swab kits you mail to a lab for spore identification. Both can tell you that mold spores are present in your home, which, to be fair, is true of almost every home. Mold spores exist in outdoor and indoor air everywhere. A positive result doesn't automatically mean you have a hidden problem.

What these kits generally can't do:

- Tell you where moisture is coming from or how far it's traveled behind a wall or under flooring
- Distinguish between a trace background spore count and an active colonization
- Measure the square footage of contamination, which matters for scoping a remediation job
- Detect moisture in building materials that hasn't produced visible mold yet

They're inexpensive and fine as a first gut check, especially if you're trying to decide whether a musty smell is worth pursuing further. Just don't treat a negative result as a clean bill of health if you still smell something or see staining, and don't treat a positive result as proof you need a full remediation crew. The number on the lab report means little without context about where the sample came from and what's "normal" for your specific home and season.

## What a Professional Inspection Adds

A mold inspection done by a restoration company isn't just a better version of the swab kit, it's a different process built to answer a different question: where is moisture entering this building, and how far has it spread. That typically includes a moisture meter survey of walls, framing, and subfloor near any suspect area, a visual inspection of crawlspaces, attics, and HVAC components where hidden growth is common, and often a thermal imaging scan to spot temperature differentials that indicate trapped moisture behind finished surfaces. Air and surface samples may still be collected, but they're interpreted alongside the moisture readings, not in isolation.

This matters a lot in North Texas housing stock. Richardson has a mix of slab-on-grade ranch homes from the 1960s and 70s and newer construction, and the area's clay-heavy soil shifts with wet and dry cycles, which can stress slab plumbing and create slow leaks that never show up as an obvious puddle. Combine that with hot, humid Gulf-influenced summers that push attic and crawlspace humidity up, and you get conditions where mold can establish behind a wall or under a sink cabinet long before a stain reaches the surface. A swab kit tells you mold exists somewhere in the house. A moisture-mapped inspection tells you it's behind the guest bathroom vanity because the supply line fitting has been seeping for weeks.

## What Not to Do

- **Don't paint or caulk over visible mold.** It seals in moisture and the growth typically returns within the material, often worse, because now it's hidden again.
- **Don't rely on bleach as a long-term fix for porous materials.** Bleach can lighten staining on nonporous surfaces like tile, but it doesn't reach mold growing inside drywall paper or wood grain, so the problem resurfaces.
- **Don't ignore a musty smell just because a cheap test came back negative.** Smell is often the earliest and most reliable indicator of hidden moisture, well before a spore count would flag it.
- **Don't run the HVAC system on recirculate if you suspect contamination in the ductwork**, since this can spread spores to rooms that were previously unaffected.
- **Don't assume small visible patches are harmless because they're small.** What you see on the surface is frequently a fraction of what's behind it.

## When to Call a Professional

A few specific signs point toward getting a professional mold inspection rather than relying on a kit alone:

1. **Recurring musty odor with no visible source**, especially near bathrooms, below sinks, or near exterior walls.
2. **A past water leak or flood event**, even one that was dried out, since trapped moisture inside wall cavities or subfloor can take weeks to show visible growth.
3. **Visible mold larger than roughly a few square feet**, where DIY cleanup risks spreading spores through the air during removal.
4. **Health symptoms that improve when someone leaves the house**, which warrants documentation from an inspection rather than guesswork.
5. **Real estate transactions**, where a buyer or seller wants a documented, dated assessment rather than a shelf-test result.

## What Remediation Looks Like After Testing

If an inspection confirms active growth, the remediation process generally follows the moisture map built during testing: isolating the affected area with containment barriers to stop spores from migrating to clean rooms, removing and disposing of contaminated porous materials like drywall or insulation that can't be fully cleaned, HEPA-filtered air scrubbing during the work, and a final clearance check once materials are dried and cleaned. The goal isn't just killing visible mold, it's correcting the moisture source so it doesn't come back in six months. That's why the inspection and moisture readings matter more than the species name on a lab report: fixing the leak or ventilation issue behind the growth is what actually resolves the problem.
