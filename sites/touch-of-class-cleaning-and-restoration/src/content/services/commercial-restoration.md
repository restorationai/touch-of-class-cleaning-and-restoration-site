## What commercial restoration actually involves

Commercial water damage and fire damage differ from a residential call mainly in scale and complexity, not in the physics. A burst supply line behind a breakroom sink can spread across a slab-on-grade office floor and wick into drywall and carpet tile before anyone notices it on a Monday morning. A kitchen fire in a strip-mall restaurant can push protein-based soot through shared HVAC returns into neighboring suites. The work itself (extraction, structural drying, soot and odor removal, content handling) uses the same category of commercial-grade extractors, air movers, and dehumidifiers used on a house, just sized and staged for thousands of square feet instead of a few rooms. What changes is the coordination: phased drying so part of the building can stay occupied, after-hours access with property management, and photo and moisture-log documentation built for a commercial claim file rather than a homeowner's policy.

## Our process

1. **Scope walkthrough with the decision-maker.** Before equipment goes in, we identify who owns the loss (tenant vs. landlord), what areas are affected, and what has to stay operational.
2. **Extraction and emergency mitigation.** Standing water is removed, affected contents are moved or elevated, and soot or smoke residue is contained to stop it from migrating through shared ductwork.
3. **Structural drying with monitoring.** Air movers and dehumidifiers are placed by square footage and material type, and moisture readings are logged on a schedule so drying can be verified rather than guessed at, following IICRC S500 drying principles.
4. **Phased work by zone.** In an occupied building we often dry and clear one zone before moving equipment to the next, so a retail floor or office wing can reopen in sections instead of waiting on the whole building.
5. **Documentation handoff.** Photos, moisture logs, and a written scope are compiled for the business owner, property manager, and whoever is managing the insurance claim.

## What separates a good commercial restoration response from a bad one

The most common mistake on commercial jobs is treating square footage like a residential job scaled up. A 3,000-square-foot office suite with a mix of carpet tile, VCT, and drywall partitions needs equipment placement planned around cubicle walls and data cabling, not a blanket drop of air movers. Adjusters also look for whether drying equipment stayed in place long enough and whether readings were actually logged, not just whether the floor "feels dry" on day three. On fire losses, a common shortcut is masking smoke odor instead of addressing the source: residue trapped in HVAC returns or above a drop ceiling will keep re-releasing odor long after surfaces look clean. Business interruption documentation matters too. A restoration record that only covers the physical work, without dated photos tied to specific zones, makes it harder for a property manager to support a loss-of-use claim.

## What does commercial restoration cost?

Commercial pricing depends heavily on square footage, category of water or type of soot involved, and whether the business needs to stay partially open during the work. Every loss gets a written scope before work begins, since no two commercial spaces dry or clean the same way. Most commercial property policies cover sudden water and fire losses and often include business interruption coverage, but coverage for slow leaks, deferred maintenance, or flood water typically depends on the specific policy.

| Scenario | Typical range |
|---|---|

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Richardson's office parks along the Telecom Corridor and its strip-center retail buildings are a mix of 1970s through 1990s construction, much of it on slab foundations with flat or low-slope commercial roofing. Flat roofs are prone to ponding and membrane failures after the heavy spring storms that roll through North Texas, and that ponding often shows up as a ceiling stain in an office weeks before anyone realizes the roof itself needs attention. Summer brings high humidity on top of heat, which speeds up secondary damage (odor, microbial growth) in any space that stays wet past a day or two, commercial or residential.
