When a loss stops being a single room and becomes an entire floor, an entire building, or a block of buildings, the math changes. A burst main at a 40-unit apartment complex or a fire that takes out a full office floor isn't a bigger version of a routine claim, it's a different kind of job entirely: multiple carriers, a property management company that needs daily answers, and a claim file that has to hold up under six or seven figures of scrutiny. Large loss and catastrophic response is built for that scale.

## What large loss and catastrophic response actually involves

Large loss work covers commercial buildings, multifamily properties, and multi-structure sites where the damage spans more square footage, more units, or more stakeholders than a standard residential claim. That might mean a hotel with water intrusion across three floors, a retail center after a hailstorm, or an apartment complex where a frozen pipe break affects a dozen units at once. The work itself still uses the same core tools, extraction units, desiccant and refrigerant dehumidifiers, air movers, moisture meters, but at a volume and coordination level that requires staging equipment by zone, running parallel crews, and tracking drying progress across dozens of readings instead of four or five. Timelines run from days to weeks depending on the number of structures involved and how quickly each zone reaches target moisture content.

## Our process

1. **Loss assessment and scope mapping.** We walk every affected structure, floor, or unit and document the damage zone by zone, not as one blanket estimate. This becomes the master scope that every carrier and adjuster works from.
2. **Resource mobilization.** Multiple drying and extraction crews, along with the equipment bank needed to run them, are deployed across the property simultaneously rather than moving room to room the way a single-loss job would.
3. **Phased mitigation.** Work is sequenced by occupancy, life-safety priority, and structural access, often running several zones in parallel while isolating others that need contents moved or demolition first.
4. **Daily reporting and documentation.** Moisture logs, photos, and equipment placement records are generated per zone and delivered on a recurring basis so property managers and adjusters aren't waiting until the end of the job to see progress.
5. **Reconciliation and sign-off.** Final moisture verification across every zone is compiled into a single documentation package for claim closeout, matched against the original scope.

## What separates a good large loss response from a bad one

The most common failure point on large losses is undersized mobilization, a crew sized for a residential job trying to cover a commercial footprint, which stretches drying timelines and lets secondary damage (microbial growth, delamination, warping) set in on the zones that get attention last. The second failure point is documentation that doesn't scale: a single moisture log for a building that actually has twelve distinct drying zones gives adjusters nothing to verify claim progress against, and that's where large loss claims stall or get disputed. Large loss adjusters are generally looking for zone-by-zone moisture tracking referenced to a documented drying standard, dated equipment placement photos, and a scope that gets updated in writing when conditions change, not just a final invoice. A coordinated response also means one point of contact managing communication across the ownership group, property management, and however many carriers are involved, instead of each crew lead fielding separate questions.

## What does large loss and catastrophic response cost?

Costs on large loss claims vary more than almost any other restoration category because they scale with square footage, number of units or floors affected, and whether contents and structural rebuild are part of the claim. Every loss gets a written scope before work begins, since square-footage estimates alone don't capture what a multi-structure or multi-floor job actually requires. Most commercial property policies cover large loss mitigation and the resulting documentation, though business interruption and contents coverage depend on the specific policy terms.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

North Texas sees large loss events on a fairly predictable seasonal pattern. Spring brings severe thunderstorms and hail that can hit an entire commercial park or apartment complex roofline at once, generating simultaneous claims across multiple buildings on the same property. Winter freeze events are the other major driver, a hard freeze across the Dallas-Fort Worth area can split pipe in multiple risers of the same multi-story building within hours of each other, turning what starts as one maintenance call into a building-wide loss. Richardson's mix of 1970s and 1980s garden-style apartment stock and newer mixed-use commercial buildings means pipe material, slab construction, and roofing age vary a lot property to property, which affects how fast a loss spreads and how a scope gets built.
