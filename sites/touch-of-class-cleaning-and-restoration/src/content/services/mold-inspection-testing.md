## What mold inspection and testing actually involves

Mold inspection and testing is two distinct steps that get lumped together in casual conversation. A visual inspection means a trained eye walking the property, checking under sinks, behind baseboards, inside HVAC returns, and along exterior walls for staining, bubbling paint, or that flat musty smell that tells you something's growing even before you see it. Testing adds data: air samples pulled through a calibrated pump onto a spore trap cassette, surface swabs or tape lifts from suspect staining, and sometimes a moisture map of the framing behind drywall. The samples go to an independent lab, and results typically come back in two to five business days depending on whether standard or rush turnaround is requested.

Homeowners usually call for one of three reasons: a musty odor with no visible source, a recent water event (a slow roof leak, a washing machine overflow, a crawlspace with standing moisture) that's had time to sit, or a real estate transaction where a buyer or seller wants documented air quality before closing. In Richardson's older brick ranch and split-level homes, slab foundations with plumbing runs beneath them can leak for months before a stain ever shows on the ceiling, so odor complaints without visible damage are common and legitimate.

## Our process

1. **Visual walkthrough and moisture mapping.** We check known risk points, bathrooms, kitchens, attic decking, crawlspaces, and areas near past water intrusion, using a moisture meter to flag damp framing or drywall that looks dry on the surface.
2. **Sample plan.** Based on what the walkthrough shows, we decide whether air sampling, surface sampling, or both make sense, and where to place an outdoor control sample for comparison.
3. **Sample collection.** Air samples run through a calibrated pump for a set volume of air; surface samples are swabbed or tape-lifted directly from the suspect material. Everything is logged with location and time.
4. **Lab analysis.** Samples go to a third-party lab, not analyzed in-house, so results are independent of whoever did the inspection.
5. **Written report and next step.** You get a plain-language report comparing indoor spore counts and types to the outdoor control, with photos and a recommendation: no action needed, further investigation, or remediation scope.

## What separates a good mold inspection from a bad one

The most common mistake is skipping the outdoor control sample. Mold spores exist everywhere outdoors; without a baseline, an indoor air sample is just a number with no context. A second mistake is testing before the moisture source is found and fixed, which gives you a snapshot of a moving target instead of a usable result.

Insurance adjusters and real estate inspectors both look for the same thing: documented sampling methodology, chain of custody paperwork, and a lab report from an accredited third party rather than a verbal opinion. Reports that skip the moisture reading and jump straight to a remediation quote are a red flag; testing and remediation should be treated as separate decisions, not bundled to justify a bigger job. A thorough inspection also checks areas that don't smell or look obviously wet, like the cavity behind a tub surround or the underside of subfloor near a dishwasher, because those are where slow leaks hide the longest.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

North Texas summers push indoor humidity up fast, especially in homes with undersized or aging HVAC systems that can't keep pace with the latent moisture load. Attics are a particular risk in Richardson's older housing stock, where bath fans vent into the attic space instead of outside and condensation collects on the underside of the roof deck through the hot months. Spring storm season adds another trigger: roof and window leaks that go unnoticed for a few weeks are often enough for colonization to start, which is why post-storm inspections make sense even when no interior damage is visible yet.
