When a water, fire, or storm loss finally dries out or gets cleared by remediation crews, homeowners are often left staring at open stud walls, missing drywall, charred framing, or subfloor that was cut out during mitigation. That gap between "the damage is stopped" and "my house is whole again" is where reconstruction services come in. This is the rebuild phase: framing, drywall, flooring, trim, paint, and in some cases full structural repair, carried out so the finished space matches what was there before the loss, or better.

## What reconstruction services actually involves

Post-damage reconstruction covers everything needed to return a structure to a safe, livable, code-compliant condition after mitigation work is finished. That can mean replacing a single section of drywall and baseboard after a small pipe leak, or it can mean reframing an entire wall, replacing subfloor, and rebuilding a kitchen after a fire. Scope depends entirely on what was removed or damaged during the event itself, not on the reconstruction crew's preference.

Timelines vary widely. A single-room drywall and paint job might run a few days once materials are on hand. A structural rebuild involving framing, electrical rough-in, insulation, drywall, and finish work can take several weeks, especially if permits and inspections are required at each stage. Henderson's building department requires permits for structural framing changes, electrical and plumbing rough-in, and most drywall replacement beyond small patches, so a reputable reconstruction contractor pulls permits before work starts rather than after an inspector flags it.

## Our process

1. **Scope of work.** We document existing damage, take moisture and structural readings where relevant, and write a detailed scope that lines up with what mitigation crews removed, not a generic estimate.
2. **Permits and code review.** Structural, electrical, and plumbing work gets permitted through the local building department before framing begins. We confirm current code requirements for the specific repair, since code can differ from what was originally built decades earlier.
3. **Framing and rough-in.** Studs, subfloor, and structural members are replaced or repaired first. Electrical, plumbing, and HVAC rough-in happen before anything gets closed up, and rough-in typically needs to pass inspection before drywall goes up.
4. **Insulation and drywall.** New insulation goes in where old material was removed, followed by drywall hanging, taping, and texture matched to the surrounding surfaces.
5. **Finish work.** Paint, trim, flooring, and fixtures are installed last, matched as closely as possible to the home's existing materials so the repair doesn't read as a patch job.

## What separates a good reconstruction response from a bad one

The most common failure point is a reconstruction crew that starts closing up walls before an insurance adjuster or inspector has signed off on the rough-in, which forces costly rework later. A second common mistake is mismatched materials: drywall thickness, texture pattern, flooring plank width, or paint sheen that doesn't match the untouched sections of the home, leaving an obvious seam.

Adjusters pay close attention to whether the scope of work reflects only what was actually damaged, with clear photo documentation tying reconstruction line items back to the original loss. A rebuild scope that's vague, or that bundles unrelated upgrades into the claim, is a frequent reason claims get delayed or partially denied. Good reconstruction work also accounts for code upgrades that weren't required when the home was originally built, things like GFCI placement, smoke detector interconnection, or insulation R-values, since those are often required once a wall is opened up regardless of the original construction.

## What does reconstruction services cost?

Reconstruction costs scale directly with how much was removed during mitigation and whether structural framing is involved, so there's no single number that applies across jobs. These are typical industry ranges, not a quote: every loss is scoped individually, and Life Savers Restoration LLC provides a written scope of work before any reconstruction begins. Homeowners insurance typically covers reconstruction tied to a covered peril, such as a burst pipe or fire, though code-upgrade costs and cosmetic preferences beyond the original condition are often excluded.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Henderson's dry climate is generally forgiving for reconstruction timelines, materials cure predictably and there's little risk of the humidity-related delays that slow drywall and paint work in more humid regions. The bigger regional factor is housing stock: a lot of Henderson construction dates from the 1990s through 2000s production-home boom, which means standardized stud spacing and drywall thickness that's usually easy to match, but also stucco exteriors and tile roofing that require specialized trades if reconstruction extends outside the building envelope.
