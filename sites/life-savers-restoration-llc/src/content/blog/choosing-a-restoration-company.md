Choosing a restoration company after a water loss, fire, or mold discovery is one of the most consequential decisions you'll make as a property owner — and you're usually making it under pressure, sometimes at midnight, sometimes while a ceiling is still dripping. The short answer: vet the company's credentials before you sign anything, understand what the contract actually commits you to, and never let urgency override due diligence. The sections below walk you through exactly how to do that, even when you're stressed and the clock is ticking.

## Why This Decision Matters More Than It Seems

Restoration isn't a commodity service. A plumber fixes a broken pipe; a restoration company determines how much of your home or building needs to come out, documents the loss for your insurance carrier, and sets the drying or remediation protocol that decides whether you'll be dealing with secondary mold growth six weeks from now.

In the Las Vegas valley — including Henderson — the dry desert climate creates a deceptive problem. Surfaces dry fast on the outside. A water intrusion behind stucco or under tile can read dry to the touch within a day or two while the substrate stays saturated for weeks. A contractor who eyeballs moisture instead of using calibrated meters can miss it entirely. That's not a minor oversight; it's the setup for a mold remediation job on top of the original water loss.

Fire damage has its own version of this problem. Smoke residue from synthetic materials — the kind found in most modern furnishings — is oily and acidic. It keeps corroding metal surfaces and embedding deeper into porous materials long after the flames are out. A company that cleans surfaces without addressing airborne particles and HVAC contamination is leaving the job half done.

The point: the company you choose shapes the outcome for months, not just the week they're on-site.

## The Four Things to Verify Before You Sign

When you're evaluating a restoration contractor, these are the non-negotiables — not nice-to-haves.

**1. Industry certification, not just a contractor's license.**
The restoration industry's primary credentialing body is the IICRC (Institute of Inspection, Cleaning and Restoration Certification). An IICRC-certified firm has technicians trained to specific standards — the S500 for water damage, the S520 for mold remediation, the S700 for fire and smoke. Ask directly: "Are your technicians IICRC-certified?" A legitimate company will tell you which certifications apply to your specific job.

**2. Licensing and insurance appropriate to the scope.**
In Nevada, contractors performing structural work — removing drywall, replacing flooring, rebuilding after a fire — need to be properly licensed through the Nevada State Contractors Board. Ask for their license number and verify it at the NSCB website before work begins. Also confirm they carry general liability and workers' compensation insurance. If an uninsured worker is injured on your property, you may be exposed.

**3. A written scope of work before any equipment goes in.**
Reputable restoration companies will assess the loss, document it with moisture readings or air sampling results, and give you a written scope before they start billing. Be cautious of any contractor who wants to begin immediately with no documentation and settle the paperwork later. That's how disputes over what was authorized — and what insurance will cover — get started.

**4. Direct insurance billing experience.**
Most residential and commercial losses go through a carrier. A company that regularly works with insurance adjusters knows how to document losses in the format carriers expect (Xactimate is the industry standard estimating platform). Ask: "Do you bill insurance directly?" and "Have you worked with [your carrier] before?" This doesn't guarantee a smooth claim, but it reduces the friction significantly.

## Red Flags That Should Slow You Down

The restoration industry, unfortunately, attracts storm chasers and opportunists after significant weather events or widespread losses. Here's what to watch for:

- **High-pressure assignment of benefits (AOB) agreements.** In some states, contractors ask homeowners to sign over their insurance rights so the contractor deals directly with the carrier. Nevada has specific rules around AOB. If someone is pushing you to sign one before you've read it carefully, slow down.
- **No physical address or local presence.** After a major storm, out-of-state contractors flood affected areas. There's nothing inherently wrong with that, but you want someone you can reach if a problem surfaces three months later.
- **Unusually low estimates.** Restoration has real costs — industrial drying equipment, certified labor, proper disposal of contaminated materials. An estimate that's dramatically lower than others usually means something is being skipped.
- **Pressure to skip the adjuster.** Your insurance company has the right to inspect the loss before major demolition begins (except in genuine emergency stabilization situations). A contractor who urges you to tear out materials before the adjuster sees them is creating a problem for your claim.
- **Vague answers about mold protocols.** For mold remediation specifically, ask whether they follow the IICRC S520 standard and whether post-remediation verification (clearance testing) is included or available. If they can't answer that question clearly, they may not be doing it.

## What to Do Right Now If You're in the Middle of a Loss

If you're reading this because something is actively happening — a burst pipe, smoke damage, a musty smell that turned into visible growth — here's the immediate priority list:

1. **Stop the source if you safely can.** For water, that means the main shutoff or the supply valve behind the fixture. For fire, that means the fire department has already been there. For mold, don't disturb it — agitating mold colonies releases spores.
2. **Document everything before you touch it.** Photos and video of the original condition matter enormously for your insurance claim. Take them before you move furniture, pull up wet carpet, or open walls.
3. **Call your insurance carrier to open a claim.** You don't need to have a contractor selected first. Opening the claim starts the clock and gets an adjuster assigned.
4. **Then call a certified restoration company** to assess the loss and begin emergency stabilization — extracting standing water, boarding up openings, or containing a mold area — while the claim process moves forward.

For water damage especially, timing matters. Mold can begin colonizing wet organic materials within 24 to 72 hours under the right conditions. In Henderson's climate, interior humidity is usually low enough to slow that window slightly, but don't count on it if the affected area has poor airflow or is behind walls.

## The Recovery Process: What to Expect After You've Chosen

Once a certified company is on-site, the work typically moves through distinct phases — and understanding them helps you ask the right questions.

For **water damage restoration**: extraction, then structural drying with air movers and dehumidifiers, then moisture verification with meters before any reconstruction begins. The drying phase typically takes several days; anyone promising it's done in 24 hours without meter readings to back that up is guessing.

For **fire damage restoration**: emergency board-up and tarping, then smoke and soot cleaning (which often involves content pack-out to an offsite facility), odor treatment, and finally reconstruction of structural elements. The smoke cleaning phase is often more labor-intensive than the structural repair.

For **mold remediation**: containment of the affected area, removal of contaminated materials, cleaning of salvageable surfaces, and post-remediation verification. The verification step — air sampling or surface testing after the work is done — is how you confirm the remediation was effective. Ask whether it's included.

At each phase, you should be receiving documentation: moisture logs, photos, a record of what was removed and why. That paperwork protects you with your carrier and gives you a record if issues resurface.
