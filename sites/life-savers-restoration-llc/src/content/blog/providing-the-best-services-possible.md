## What Makes a Restoration Company Actually Good?

A genuinely capable restoration company holds active IICRC certifications matched to the work they perform. The [IICRC](https://www.iicrc.org) (Institute of Inspection, Cleaning and Restoration Certification) sets the industry standards, including the S500 for water damage and the S520 for mold remediation. A company that holds IICRC WRT (Water Restoration Technician) and ASD (Applied Structural Drying) credentials has been trained to dry a structure correctly, not just move equipment around until things look dry on the surface.

Beyond certifications, look for:

- **24/7 emergency availability.** Water damage that sits overnight doubles in affected area and cost. A company that only answers during business hours is not an emergency restoration company.
- **Written scope of work before billing.** Reputable contractors provide a detailed scope, often using Xactimate estimating software, before work begins. This protects you and your insurance claim.
- **Direct insurance billing experience.** The best companies have worked with adjusters before. They know what documentation a claim requires: moisture readings, photo logs, drying logs, equipment placement records.
- **EPA Lead-Safe certification** if your home was built before 1978. Disturbing lead paint during demolition without proper protocols is a liability and a health hazard.

## What Services Should a Full-Service Restoration Company Cover?

A complete restoration company handles the full range of property damage, not just one specialty. If a company only does water extraction but can't address the mold that follows, you'll be coordinating two separate contractors during an already stressful situation.

The core services to look for:

**Water damage restoration** covers extraction, structural drying, and moisture monitoring. A qualified crew uses LGR (low-grain refrigerant) dehumidifiers and calibrated air movers, not just fans from a hardware store. Drying is validated with moisture meters and thermal imaging, not eyeballed.

**Mold remediation** follows the IICRC S520 standard. It includes containment with poly sheeting and negative air pressure, HEPA vacuuming, antimicrobial treatment, and post-remediation verification testing. A company with IICRC AMRT (Applied Microbial Remediation Technician) certification has the training to do this correctly. If you're unsure whether you have a mold problem, the post [7 Signs You Have Hidden Mold (and What To Do Next)](/blog/signs-of-hidden-mold/) walks through what to look for before you call anyone.

**Fire and smoke damage restoration** requires IICRC FSRT (Fire and Smoke Restoration Technician) credentials. Smoke penetrates porous materials deeply, and improper cleaning can set odors permanently. This work involves thermal fogging, ozone treatment, and careful content evaluation.

**Storm damage restoration** in the Las Vegas Valley typically means flash flood intrusion, wind-driven debris, or roof failure during monsoon season. Rapid tarping and board-up prevents secondary damage while the full scope is assessed.

**Sewage cleanup** (also called Category 3 water, or black water) is a biohazard situation. It requires full PPE, EPA-compliant disposal, and antimicrobial treatment of all affected surfaces. This is not a DIY job under any circumstances.

**Biohazard cleanup** and **contents restoration** round out a full-service operation. Contents restoration, in particular, is often overlooked: a company that can clean, pack out, and store your belongings during remediation saves you from replacing items that could have been saved.

## How Does the Restoration Process Actually Work?

Understanding the process helps you evaluate whether a company is cutting corners. Here's what a legitimate job looks like from first call to completion.

**Step 1: Emergency response and assessment.** A crew arrives, identifies the source and category of damage, and performs an initial moisture survey using thermal imaging and pin/pinless meters. They document everything with photos before touching a thing.

**Step 2: Mitigation.** Water is extracted, affected materials are identified for removal or drying, and equipment is placed. For mold or biohazard jobs, containment goes up first.

**Step 3: Structural drying (water jobs).** Air movers and dehumidifiers run continuously, typically for 3 to 5 days for a standard water loss. Daily moisture readings confirm progress. The [IICRC S500 standard](https://www.iicrc.org) defines the drying goals the crew is working toward.

**Step 4: Demolition if needed.** Saturated drywall, flooring, or insulation that can't be dried in place gets removed. This is documented for the insurance claim.

**Step 5: Post-remediation verification.** For mold jobs, a clearance test by an independent industrial hygienist confirms the remediation worked before containment comes down.

**Step 6: Reconstruction.** A full-service company can rebuild what was removed: drywall, flooring, cabinetry. This keeps your project under one contract and one point of accountability.

## Will Insurance Cover This?

Homeowners insurance typically covers sudden and accidental losses: a pipe bursts, a washing machine hose fails, a roof is damaged in a storm. It generally does not cover gradual damage, meaning a slow leak behind a wall that went unnoticed for months is often denied.

What protects your claim is documentation. A restoration company that provides daily moisture logs, photo records, and a detailed Xactimate scope gives your adjuster exactly what they need to process the claim. A company that starts tearing out materials before documenting the damage creates problems for your claim that are hard to fix later.

The [EPA's guidance on mold and moisture](https://www.epa.gov/mold) is a useful reference if your insurer is pushing back on remediation scope, as it establishes why mold must be addressed, not just dried over.
