Whether your homeowners insurance covers water damage comes down to one word: *sudden*. If water entered your home suddenly and accidentally — a pipe burst, an appliance supply line failed, a storm drove rain through a broken window — most standard HO-3 policies will cover the resulting damage. If water seeped in slowly over weeks or months — a dripping valve under a sink, a slow roof leak you hadn't addressed, moisture wicking through a foundation wall — most policies will not. That distinction, sudden versus gradual, is the single most important thing to understand before you file a claim.

## The Coverage That Usually Applies

A standard homeowners policy covers what the industry calls "sudden and accidental" water damage. In practice, that means:

- A supply line to your washing machine or refrigerator icemaker fails and floods the laundry room.
- A pipe freezes and bursts during a cold snap (yes, even in Henderson — temperatures can drop below freezing overnight in January).
- An upstairs toilet overflows and water soaks through to the ceiling below.
- A neighbor's plumbing failure damages your unit (in a condo or townhome scenario).
- A storm breaks a window and rain soaks your flooring before you can cover it.

In these cases, your policy's dwelling coverage (Coverage A) pays to repair the structure — subfloor, drywall, cabinetry — and your personal property coverage (Coverage C) pays to replace damaged belongings, subject to your deductible and any depreciation schedule.

One thing many homeowners miss: the cost of *tearing out* wet material to access the source of the leak is often covered separately under a provision called "tear-out" or "access" coverage. If a plumber has to open a wall to reach a burst pipe, your insurer may pay for that demolition even if the plumbing repair itself falls under a separate policy.

## What Homeowners Insurance Almost Never Covers

The exclusions matter as much as the coverage. These are the situations where most standard policies will deny a claim:

**Gradual damage and neglect.** If an adjuster determines the leak had been ongoing — rust staining, soft drywall, mold already colonizing behind a wall — they will likely cite "failure to maintain" and deny the claim. Insurers expect homeowners to address problems when they become apparent.

**Flooding from outside the home.** This is the exclusion that surprises people most. Flood damage — water that enters from the ground up, from an overflowing wash, or from storm surge — is *not* covered by a standard homeowners policy. In Nevada, flash flooding in desert washes is a real risk, particularly in Henderson's lower-elevation neighborhoods near the Pittman Wash and Lake Las Vegas area. Flood coverage requires a separate policy through the National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) or a private flood insurer.

**Sewer and drain backup.** If a city sewer line backs up into your basement or a floor drain, most standard policies exclude that loss. Some insurers offer a sewer backup endorsement as an add-on — worth checking your declarations page.

**Roof leaks from deferred maintenance.** If your roof is aging and an adjuster determines the leak resulted from deterioration rather than a sudden storm event, the claim may be denied or only partially paid.

**Mold remediation (sometimes).** Mold coverage varies widely by policy and state. Some policies cap mold remediation at a low dollar amount; others exclude it entirely unless it resulted directly from a covered water loss.

## How to Read Your Own Policy

You don't need to read the whole document. Look for these three sections:

1. **Perils Insured Against** (or "Covered Perils") — This lists what events trigger coverage. Look for language like "accidental discharge or overflow of water" and note any exclusions listed immediately after.
2. **Exclusions** — Search specifically for the words "flood," "seepage," "gradual," and "neglect." These are the words adjusters use to deny claims.
3. **Endorsements or Riders** — These are add-ons stapled to your base policy. A sewer backup endorsement or equipment breakdown rider can change your coverage significantly.

If you have an HO-3 policy (the most common type for single-family homes), it covers your dwelling on an "open perils" basis — meaning everything is covered *unless* specifically excluded. Personal property is usually covered on a "named perils" basis, meaning only the events listed are covered. That distinction matters when you're trying to figure out whether damaged furniture or electronics will be replaced.

## What to Do Immediately After a Water Loss

If you're dealing with an active leak or flooding right now, the sequence matters:

1. **Stop the source.** Shut off the supply valve under the fixture, or turn off the main water shutoff if you can't isolate it. In most Henderson homes built after the 1980s, the main shutoff is near the water meter at the street.
2. **Document before you touch anything.** Take photos and video of standing water, wet materials, and the apparent source. Timestamp everything. This is your evidence for the claim.
3. **Call your insurer to open a claim.** You don't need to know the full extent of the damage yet. Opening the claim starts the clock and gets an adjuster assigned.
4. **Begin mitigation — don't wait.** Most policies require you to take "reasonable steps" to prevent further damage. That means extracting standing water, moving wet belongings out of the affected area, and getting air moving. Doing nothing while you wait for an adjuster can give the insurer grounds to reduce your payout.
5. **Keep every receipt.** Emergency fans, hotel stays if the home is uninhabitable, meals if you're displaced — many policies include Additional Living Expenses (ALE) coverage that reimburses these costs.

Professional water damage restoration companies can document moisture readings, extract water, and begin drying in a way that creates a defensible record for your claim. That documentation — moisture maps, equipment logs, daily readings — often makes the difference between a smooth claim and a disputed one.

## When the Insurer Disputes or Denies the Claim

Denials happen. If your claim is denied or you believe the settlement offer is too low, you have options:

- **Request the denial in writing** with the specific policy language cited.
- **Hire a public adjuster.** Unlike your insurer's adjuster, a public adjuster works for you. They typically charge a percentage of the final settlement, but they know how to document losses in the language insurers respond to.
- **Invoke the appraisal clause.** Most policies include a process for resolving disputes over the dollar amount of a loss without going to court.
- **File a complaint with the Nevada Division of Insurance** if you believe the denial is in bad faith.

Nevada has specific timelines insurers must follow for acknowledging and resolving claims. If your insurer is slow-walking the process, a complaint to the Division of Insurance sometimes accelerates things.
