## Why Las Vegas Properties See Water Damage Differently Than Most Cities

The Clark County housing boom that reshaped the valley through the late 1990s and 2000s produced enormous quantities of stucco-clad, wood-framed homes on post-tension concrete slabs. That construction combination behaves in a specific way when water intrudes: the slab itself doesn't absorb much, but the moisture migrates laterally under luxury vinyl plank or tile adhesive, creating hidden saturation that a surface moisture meter won't catch. Meanwhile, the valley's caliche soil layer — a calcium carbonate hardpan that sits just below the surface in many neighborhoods — can impede drainage and push groundwater toward foundations during the brief but intense monsoon storms that roll through July through September.

Older homes in areas like Summerlin and the central Las Vegas corridor sometimes have galvanized supply lines that corrode from the inside out; hard water accelerates that process. A pinhole leak behind a vanity cabinet can run for weeks before it's visible, and by then the adjacent drywall and cabinet substrate have absorbed far more water than the small stain suggests.
