## Why Paradise Properties Face Distinct Fire Damage Challenges

The Las Vegas Valley's arid climate — routinely below 20% relative humidity in summer — does something counterintuitive after a fire: it dries smoke particles onto surfaces before crews can mobilize. In humid climates, soot stays slightly tacky and lifts more readily. In Paradise, that same residue can begin chemically bonding to painted drywall, stone countertops, and soft goods within the first few hours. The window for reversible surface cleaning is shorter here than in most of the country.

Paradise also has a wide range of construction eras and building types. The corridor near the Strip is dominated by concrete and steel commercial construction, but residential blocks — particularly in the quieter neighborhoods south and east of the resort corridor — include a significant number of wood-frame homes built in the 1960s through 1980s. Many of those older homes were built before modern fire-blocking requirements, which means fire can travel inside wall cavities more freely than in newer construction. That hidden structural involvement often isn't visible until a technician opens walls and checks with a thermal imaging camera.

The valley's frequent use of stucco exteriors also matters. Stucco is porous and absorbs smoke odor compounds — particularly acrolein and formaldehyde — that off-gas for weeks if not treated at the substrate level, not just the surface.

## Our Fire Damage Restoration Process in Paradise

The first priority after the fire department clears a scene is stabilization. That means boarding windows and doors, tarping any roof penetrations, and securing the structure against the heat and wind that are constant factors in the Las Vegas Valley. Leaving a fire-damaged property open — even overnight — invites secondary damage from blowing dust and extreme temperature swings.

Once the structure is secured, the documentation phase begins. Every affected surface, material, and system is photographed and catalogued before anything is removed. This record becomes the foundation of the insurance claim and the scope of work.

Demolition of non-salvageable materials follows — charred framing, melted insulation, compromised drywall. This step is not just cosmetic; it removes the primary odor sources and allows technicians to assess structural integrity. In older wood-frame homes common to parts of Paradise, this phase sometimes reveals fire travel through wall cavities that wasn't apparent from the exterior.

Odor neutralization in the desert requires a layered approach: thermal fogging, hydroxyl generation, and in some cases ozone treatment, followed by encapsulation of any remaining porous surfaces. Because Paradise's dry air concentrates smoke compounds rather than diluting them, a single-pass deodorization rarely achieves a clean result. The IICRC FSRT standard guides the sequence and verification steps.

Reconstruction — framing, drywall, insulation, paint, and finish work — closes out the project.
