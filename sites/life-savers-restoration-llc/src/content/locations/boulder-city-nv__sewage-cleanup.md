## Why Boulder City Properties See Sewage Backup Issues

Boulder City's infrastructure story is unlike most of Southern Nevada. The city was purpose-built in the early 1930s to house dam workers, which means a meaningful portion of its residential housing stock is pushing 90 years old. Cast-iron and Orangeburg sewer laterals from that era are well past their design life. Orangeburg pipe — a compressed tar-and-paper product common in mid-century construction — softens, deforms, and collapses under soil pressure, and Boulder City's caliche-heavy soil doesn't help. Caliche expands and contracts with temperature swings, placing lateral stress on aging pipe joints that were never designed for that kind of movement.

Beyond aging infrastructure, the city's position near Lake Mead and the surrounding recreation corridor means that seasonal population surges — particularly during cooler months when visitors flood the area — can spike demand on sewer capacity. Grease accumulation and non-flushable debris become more likely when short-term rental and vacation properties cycle through high occupancy. Septic systems on larger parcels at the city's edges face their own set of challenges: the rocky desert substrate limits effective drain field absorption, and a system that's borderline functional in spring can fail outright under summer heat.

## Our Sewage Cleanup and Sanitization Process in Boulder City

Sewage is classified as Category 3 water — the most contaminated class — and every step of our process reflects that. We don't treat a sewer backup like an ordinary water loss.

**Containment first.** The moment we arrive, we establish a containment perimeter to prevent cross-contamination from spreading to unaffected areas of the home. In Boulder City's older bungalows and ranch-style homes, open floor plans and shared subfloor cavities mean contaminated water can migrate farther than it appears at the surface.

**Extraction and removal.** We remove standing sewage using truck-mounted extraction equipment, then bag and dispose of porous materials — carpet, pad, drywall, insulation — that cannot be safely sanitized. Given the heat, materials that have been saturated with Category 3 water even briefly are treated as non-salvageable.

**EPA-registered disinfection.** All affected hard surfaces are treated with EPA-registered disinfectants rated for sewage contamination. We pay particular attention to subfloor sheathing and concrete slab surfaces, which are common in Boulder City construction and can harbor pathogens in surface pores.

**Structural drying.** Once the space is sanitized, we deploy commercial desiccant and refrigerant dehumidifiers alongside high-velocity air movers. In Boulder City's low-humidity environment, ambient conditions actually assist drying — but we still monitor moisture readings in walls and subfloor assemblies daily, because low surface humidity can mask trapped moisture in structural cavities.

**Clearance documentation.** Before we close out, we document final moisture readings and provide a written scope of the work completed — useful for insurance claims and for any permits the city may require for structural repairs.

## Local Note

Boulder City's historic district includes homes built with poured concrete foundations and original clay-tile sewer connections that were standard practice in the 1930s. Clay tile joints rely on compression and mortar — both of which degrade over decades of thermal cycling in the desert. When sewage backs up in one of these older homes, the lateral itself is often the underlying cause, not just a blockage. We flag this for homeowners during our assessment because addressing only the interior cleanup without identifying a failing lateral means the same backup is likely to recur.
