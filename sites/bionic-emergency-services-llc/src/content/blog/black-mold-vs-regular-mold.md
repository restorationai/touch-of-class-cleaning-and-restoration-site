## There's No Visual Test That Tells You What Species of Mold You're Looking At

Color is not a reliable way to identify mold. Stachybotrys chartarum, the mold most people mean when they say "black mold," can appear dark green, nearly black, or even gray depending on how wet the surface is and how old the colony is. Meanwhile, common molds like Cladosporium or Alternaria also grow in dark olive or charcoal shades and are far more frequently found in homes than Stachybotrys. The only way to know what species you're dealing with is a lab sample. What actually matters for your health and your house is not the species name, it's how much mold is present, how long it's been growing, and what's feeding it.

## What People Mean When They Say "Black Mold"

Stachybotrys needs a few specific conditions to colonize: a cellulose-based material (drywall paper, wood framing, ceiling tile) that has stayed wet for an extended period, usually several days or longer, and limited airflow. That's why it shows up most often behind walls after a slow leak, under flooring after a flood, or in attic sheathing after a roof failure. It tends to have a slimy or wet-looking texture when active and can dry into a flaky, powdery black residue once the moisture source is gone. It also typically carries a stronger, more musty, earthy odor than the mildew most people are used to seeing in a shower corner.

Ordinary mold and mildew, by contrast, often shows up on surfaces exposed to regular humidity rather than standing water: bathroom grout, window sills with condensation, closets with poor ventilation. It's usually a thinner growth, sometimes fuzzy, and ranges from white or gray to green or black. Both categories of mold can trigger allergic reactions, respiratory irritation, and worsened asthma symptoms in sensitive individuals. Neither should be left to spread, regardless of what color it is.

## Signs That Point to a Bigger Moisture Problem

A few details usually separate a minor surface issue from something that needs more serious attention:

- **How it feels.** Surface mildew wipes off fairly easily with a cleaner and doesn't come back quickly if the humidity is controlled. Mold that's colonized a wet building material tends to return within days of cleaning because the moisture source underneath hasn't been addressed.
- **Where it is.** Mold on a windowsill or shower tile is a ventilation and condensation issue. Mold on a baseboard, the back of drywall, or around a floor vent usually means water got into the wall cavity or under the slab at some point.
- **The smell.** A musty odor that's noticeable even when you can't see visible growth is often a sign that mold is established somewhere you can't see, commonly inside a wall, under flooring, or in an attic or crawlspace.
- **The history.** If the area was wet or flooded in the past 24 to 48 hours and wasn't fully dried, assume mold growth has started or will within a few days. Most molds, including Stachybotrys, can begin colonizing a damp cellulose surface in as little as 24 to 72 hours under the right humidity and temperature.

In Houston's climate, where indoor humidity can stay elevated for months at a time and AC systems are running almost year-round, condensation-related mold on cooler surfaces (interior-facing exterior walls, AC supply vents, attic ductwork) shows up more often than in drier climates. That's worth factoring in if you're trying to figure out why mold keeps coming back in the same spot.

## What Not to Do When You Find Mold

1. **Don't rely on bleach as a fix for porous materials.** Bleach can kill surface mold on tile or glass, but it doesn't penetrate drywall, wood, or insulation. On porous materials, the roots can remain and the growth often returns.
2. **Don't disturb a large patch without protection.** Scrubbing or sanding dry mold growth can aerosolize spores and push them further into the HVAC system or adjoining rooms. If a patch is larger than roughly a few square feet, this is past the point of a DIY wipe-down.
3. **Don't paint over it.** Painting over an active mold colony traps moisture and spores under the new coat and almost always leads to the paint bubbling or the mold reappearing within weeks.
4. **Don't ignore the smell just because you can't see the source.** An odor without visible growth usually means the mold is inside a cavity, not that there's nothing there.
5. **Don't assume a small test kit gives you the full picture.** Over-the-counter mold test kits can tell you spores are present, which they almost always are in any home, but they don't reliably tell you whether you have an active colonized area that needs remediation.

## When It's Time to Call a Professional

A small, isolated patch of surface mildew on tile or a windowsill is usually manageable with regular cleaning and better ventilation. Bring in a mold remediation professional when any of the following apply: the growth covers more than a few square feet, it's recurring in the same spot after cleaning, it followed a known water event like a leak or flood, you can smell it but can't find it, or anyone in the household has started having unexplained respiratory symptoms, headaches, or allergy flare-ups that improve when they leave the house.

A proper remediation process starts with identifying and stopping the moisture source, because treating mold without fixing the underlying leak or humidity problem just buys time before it returns. From there, containment is set up to keep spores from spreading to unaffected areas, affected porous materials are typically removed rather than treated, and the space is dried and verified before anything is rebuilt. This is different from a general cleaning job. It's a structural drying and remediation process, and it's worth treating it that way if the growth is widespread or tied to a past water event.
