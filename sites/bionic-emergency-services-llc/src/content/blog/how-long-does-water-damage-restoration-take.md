Most water damage restoration projects take between three and seven days for the drying and mitigation phase, with full repairs (drywall, flooring, paint) adding another one to four weeks depending on how much material needs to be replaced. A small bathroom leak caught early might be fully resolved in under a week. A whole first floor affected by a burst pipe, with cabinets, baseboards, and flooring torn out, can stretch past a month once you factor in permitting, material lead times, and insurance approvals. The honest answer is: it depends on how much water got in, what it touched, and how fast extraction started.

## Why the Timeline Isn't One Number

Every water loss has three phases that stack on top of each other: mitigation (stopping the water and drying the structure), remediation (removing anything that can't be saved, like soaked drywall or swollen subfloor), and reconstruction (putting the space back together). The first phase is the most predictable. The second and third depend on decisions you and your insurance adjuster make, plus how fast materials can be ordered.

In Houston, humidity adds a variable most drier climates don't deal with. Outdoor dew points regularly sit above 70°F for much of the year, which means indoor air is already carrying a lot of moisture before a leak even happens. Drying equipment has to work harder and longer to pull materials down to a safe moisture content, and homes with slab foundations (common across much of the city) trap moisture differently than homes with crawl spaces, since there's no airflow underneath to help materials release water on their own.

## The Restoration Process, Stage by Stage

1. **Inspection and water extraction (hours 1 to 24).** A technician identifies the water category (clean, gray, or black) and class (how much area and what materials are affected), then starts pulling standing water with pumps and extraction wands. This is also when moisture meters get used on drywall, subfloor, and framing to map how far the water traveled, often farther than the visible stain suggests.
2. **Structural drying (days 1 to 5, sometimes longer).** Air movers and dehumidifiers run continuously to bring wood, drywall, and framing back to a normal moisture range, dried per the IICRC S500 standard for water damage. Carpet pad is almost always removed rather than dried in place. Drywall that wicked water more than a couple of inches up from the floor usually gets cut out, since drying it in place rarely restores its structural integrity.
3. **Demolition of unsalvageable materials (overlaps with drying).** Anything that stayed wet too long, swollen MDF cabinetry, delaminated engineered flooring, baseboards that absorbed water along the bottom edge, gets removed during this window so drying equipment can reach what's behind it.
4. **Monitoring and verification (ongoing through the drying phase).** Moisture readings get logged daily. Equipment stays in place until materials hit a dry standard, not until the space looks dry to the eye. This is the step homeowners most often try to shortcut, and it's the one that causes the most repeat problems.
5. **Reconstruction (one week to over a month).** New drywall, flooring, paint, and trim go back in. Timeline here depends almost entirely on material availability and whether the work requires permitting, which it typically does for anything involving electrical, plumbing, or structural framing.

## What Slows the Timeline Down

- **Delayed extraction.** Water sitting for 24 to 48 hours before anyone starts pulling it out gives it time to wick deeper into subfloor, baseboards, and wall cavities, which means more demolition later.
- **Mold growth.** Mold can begin colonizing damp drywall and framing within 24 to 72 hours in a humid climate like Houston's. If drying starts late enough that mold shows up, the project now needs remediation steps added before reconstruction can begin.
- **Hidden moisture behind finished surfaces.** A wall can read dry on the painted surface while the insulation or sheathing behind it is still saturated. Skipping thorough moisture mapping means problems resurface weeks later as a musty smell or soft spot in the wall.
- **Insurance back-and-forth.** Waiting on adjuster approval before starting demolition or ordering materials is one of the most common reasons a project that could finish in two weeks stretches to five or six.

## What Not to Do While You Wait

Don't run a box fan or two and call it dried. Surface-dry is not the same as dry to the standard that prevents mold and structural decay. Don't put carpet pad back down, paint over a water stain, or close up drywall until moisture readings confirm the material is actually dry, not just dry enough to touch. And don't wait on a damp smell or a soft spot in flooring hoping it resolves itself; by the time it's noticeable, the water has usually been sitting longer than you think.

## When to Call a Professional

If the water came from a clean source (a supply line, not a sewage backup) and affected a small, contained area, a wet-vac and a couple of fans might genuinely be enough. Call a restoration company if any of the following apply: the water has been sitting more than a few hours, it reached carpet pad or subfloor, it came from a toilet or drain line, or you're not sure how far it traveled behind walls or under flooring. Professional equipment (commercial-grade dehumidifiers and air movers, moisture meters that read through drywall) does in days what box fans can't do at all.
