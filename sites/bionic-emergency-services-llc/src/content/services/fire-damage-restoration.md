## What Fire Damage Restoration actually involves

Fire damage restoration is really two problems layered on top of each other. There's the fire itself (charring, heat warping, structural weakening) and there's everything the fire touched without burning: smoke odor driven deep into porous materials, soot films coating every horizontal and vertical surface, and standing or trapped water from the hoses that put the fire out. The soot alone varies by what burned: protein residue from a kitchen fire behaves completely differently than the dry, powdery soot from a wood-frame structure fire or the thick, sticky synthetic soot from burning plastics and foam furniture. Each requires a different cleaning agent and a different approach, which is why a generic wipe-down often makes staining worse instead of better. A typical single-room fire with smoke spread into adjoining rooms runs several days of active work; a structural fire with charred framing and multi-room smoke damage can run multiple weeks once contents pack-out, demolition, and rebuild coordination are factored in.

## Our process

1. **Soot and damage characterization.** We identify the soot type (protein, dry, wet/synthetic, fuel oil) because that determines which cleaning chemistry and equipment we use, room by room.
2. **Water and moisture control.** Suppression water gets extracted and the structure is dried per IICRC structural drying standards before any surface cleaning begins, since cleaning over damp materials just redistributes residue.
3. **Contents pack-out and inventory.** Salvageable furniture, textiles, and belongings are documented, photographed, and moved off-site for specialized cleaning, which also protects them from continued exposure during structural work.
4. **Surface cleaning and thermal fogging.** Walls, ceilings, HVAC surfaces, and hard contents are cleaned with soot-specific agents; thermal fogging or ozone treatment is used in sealed spaces to break down odor molecules that have bonded to porous materials.
5. **Deodorization and final clearance.** Once odor and residue are addressed, we do a final walkthrough with you (and your adjuster, if present) before handing the space back for reconstruction or move-in.

## What separates a good fire damage restoration response from a bad one

The most common mistake is treating fire cleanup as a surface job. Soot is acidic and keeps degrading metal fixtures, glass, and finishes the longer it sits, so delayed response means more permanent staining and corrosion, not less. Another frequent miss: skipping HVAC inspection. Smoke particulate gets pulled into return air and recirculated through the whole house if the system and ductwork aren't cleaned or isolated early. Adjusters also look closely at whether suppression water was properly dried, since trapped moisture behind baseboards or under flooring after a fire is one of the most common causes of a reopened claim weeks later. A thorough response documents every affected material with photos and moisture readings before cleaning begins, not just after, because that documentation is what supports the claim if additional damage is found once walls or flooring are opened up.

## What does fire damage restoration cost?

Costs vary widely depending on how much of the structure was affected, how much smoke traveled into unburned rooms, and whether contents need off-site cleaning. Every loss gets a written scope before work begins so there are no surprises once the project starts, and most standard homeowners policies cover fire and smoke damage restoration, though coverage for contents, code upgrades, and additional living expenses depends on your specific policy.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Houston's humidity adds a second clock to every fire job: suppression water and trapped moisture start supporting mold growth within days in our climate, so drying can't wait until after the soot cleanup is scheduled. Many Houston homes also run central HVAC year-round, which means smoke odor spreads further through the ductwork here than it would in a home that's only conditioned seasonally, making HVAC inspection a routine part of the scope rather than an afterthought.
