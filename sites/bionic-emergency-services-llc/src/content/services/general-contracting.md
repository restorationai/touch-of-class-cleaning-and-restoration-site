A burst pipe gets dried out, a kitchen fire gets the soot wiped down, and then the homeowner is left staring at open studs, a subfloor that doesn't match, or a bathroom that's been gutted to the slab with no clear plan to put it back together. That gap between mitigation and a livable house again is where general contracting and reconstruction work starts. It also covers the renovations and remodels homeowners take on outside of any insurance claim: a dated kitchen, a bathroom that needs to be reconfigured, or a room addition that finally makes the house work for the family living in it.

## What renovations, remodels and general contracting actually involves

This work spans two starting points. Post-damage rebuild picks up where water, fire, or storm mitigation ends: framing, insulation, drywall, flooring, cabinetry, paint, and trim, brought back to match or improve on what was there before. Standalone renovation work, like a kitchen remodel or bathroom remodel, starts from a design conversation instead of a loss. Either way the scope touches the same trades: carpentry, electrical and plumbing rough-in coordination, drywall and finish work, tile and flooring, and cabinetry and fixture installation. Timelines vary with scope. A single-room drywall and paint rebuild after a small water loss might run one to two weeks. A full kitchen remodel with cabinet and layout changes typically runs four to eight weeks once materials are on hand, and that's usually the long pole: cabinets and countertops are often the longest lead-time item on the job.

## Our process

1. **Scope of work.** We walk the space, document existing conditions (including any moisture or fire-damage documentation from mitigation work already performed), and write a detailed scope that breaks out materials, labor, and sequencing before anyone picks up a tool.
2. **Permitting.** Structural changes, electrical and plumbing rough-in, and some fire-damage rebuilds require permits through the local jurisdiction. We identify what's required for your scope and handle the submission so inspections don't stall the project.
3. **Framing and rough-in.** Studs, subfloor, and any structural repairs go in first, followed by coordination with licensed electrical and plumbing trades for rough-in before walls close up.
4. **Insulation, drywall, and inspection.** Walls close up only after rough-in passes inspection where required, so nothing gets sealed in that needs to be seen again later.
5. **Finish work.** Flooring, cabinetry, tile, paint, trim, and fixtures go in last, with a final walkthrough against the original scope before we call the job done.

## What separates a good reconstruction response from a bad one

The most common failure point is sequencing: closing up walls before an inspection, or installing flooring before cabinets are set, which means ripping out finished work to fix something underneath. A second common miss is matching. When a rebuild follows a water or fire loss, insurance adjusters are looking for materials, finishes, and even paint sheen that reasonably match the pre-loss condition, not just "similar." Contractors who skip documenting existing finishes before demo often end up in disputes over what the carrier should pay to replace. A third issue is treating a post-damage rebuild like new construction and skipping the drying and moisture verification step entirely. If structural drying wasn't completed and documented to IICRC S500 standards before framing closes up, trapped moisture can show up as a mold or odor problem months later, under finishes that now have to come back out.

## What does renovations, remodels and general contracting cost?

Costs vary enormously by scope, materials, and whether the work is rebuild-only or a full design change, so the ranges below are typical industry figures, not a quote. Every project gets a written scope of work with line-item pricing before anything starts, so you know what you're agreeing to in advance. If the work follows a covered loss, your homeowners policy typically pays to restore the space to its pre-loss condition using comparable materials; upgrades beyond that, like a layout change or higher-end finishes, are usually an out-of-pocket difference.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Houston's humidity and clay-heavy soils affect rebuild decisions more than most homeowners expect. Framing and drywall installed during peak summer humidity need extra attention to moisture content before closing up walls, and many of the area's older homes sit on pier-and-beam foundations where slab moisture and foundation movement show up as cracked tile or sticking doors well after a remodel is finished if they weren't accounted for during framing. Spring and fall, with lower humidity and milder temperatures, are generally the more forgiving windows for interior finish work.
