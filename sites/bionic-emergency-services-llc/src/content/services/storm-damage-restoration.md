## What Storm Damage Restoration actually involves

Storm damage restoration covers the mitigation work that happens after wind, hail, hurricane bands, or a falling tree compromise a building's exterior: emergency roof tarping and board-up, extraction of water that's entered through the breach, removal of storm debris that's trapping moisture against the structure, and structural drying once the opening is sealed. It's distinct from a slow plumbing leak in one important way, the water source is still outside and often still active when we arrive, so the first hours are about stopping the intrusion, not just cleaning up what's already happened. Depending on how much water got behind drywall, insulation, or subfloor, drying can run several days with moisture monitoring at each check-in.

## Our process

1. **Emergency tarping or board-up.** We cover the breach, whether that's a missing section of roof decking, a shattered window, or a wall opened up by a fallen limb, to stop additional water from entering while the weather clears.
2. **Water and debris extraction.** Standing water, saturated insulation, and storm debris come out first. Tree limbs and roofing material often need to be cleared before drying equipment can even be placed.
3. **Structural drying and moisture mapping.** Our techs follow IICRC WRT and ASD guidance to map how far moisture traveled behind walls and under flooring, not just where it's visible, and place equipment accordingly.
4. **Documentation and claim support.** Every affected area gets photographed and moisture-logged before and during drying, which becomes the backbone of your insurance claim.

## What separates a good storm damage response from a bad one

The mistakes that cost homeowners the most money happen in the first 48 hours. A tarp that's stapled instead of properly battened down tears loose in the next gust and lets more water in than the original damage did. Crews that dry only the visible wet drywall and skip the wall cavity behind brick veneer leave moisture that shows up as mold three weeks later. Adjusters pay close attention to cause-of-loss separation on storm claims, wind damage and flood damage are often covered differently, and documentation that doesn't clearly distinguish wind-driven rain intrusion from rising floodwater can slow a claim down or get part of it denied. Good storm response means moisture readings at multiple depths, photos timestamped before any repair work starts, and a scope of work that matches what the adjuster will actually ask about.
