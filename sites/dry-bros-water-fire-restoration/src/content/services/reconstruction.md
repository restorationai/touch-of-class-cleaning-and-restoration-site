After the water is extracted, the fire is out, or the mold is gone, you're left staring at a wall that no longer exists, or a ceiling that came down in chunks, or a subfloor that buckled beyond saving. Mitigation stops the damage; reconstruction is what turns a gutted room back into a livable space. That transition is where a lot of property owners get stuck, juggling a separate general contractor while their insurance adjuster is waiting on a scope of work. Dry Bros handles both sides so the rebuild starts as soon as the structure is ready.

## What reconstruction services actually involve

Post-damage reconstruction isn't remodeling. The goal isn't an upgrade, it's a precise return to pre-loss condition, documented in a way your insurance carrier can approve line by line. That means replacing what was damaged, matching existing materials where possible, and meeting current building code even when the original construction predates it.

In practice, the work ranges from patching a single drywall section to full structural reconstruction, reframing load-bearing walls, replacing roof decking, reinstalling electrical and plumbing that ran through affected cavities, and finishing everything from insulation out. Chicago's older housing stock adds its own layer of complexity: balloon-frame construction common in pre-1940s two-flats and greystones behaves very differently in a fire than platform framing, and plaster-over-lath walls require a different approach to matching texture than modern drywall assemblies.

Timeline depends on scope. A single-room rebuild after a contained water loss might take one to two weeks. Structural reconstruction following significant fire damage, where framing, sheathing, and mechanical systems all need replacement, typically runs six to twelve weeks, with permit review adding time depending on the municipality.

## Our process

1. **Scope of work development.** Before a nail goes in, every affected area is documented: dimensions, materials, finish grades, code requirements. The scope becomes the basis for the insurance estimate and the construction plan. Adjusters work from line-item estimates, so vague scopes cause delays and underpayment.

2. **Permitting and code compliance.** Structural work in Chicago and most surrounding municipalities requires permits. We identify what's required, submit the documentation, and schedule inspections so the rebuild doesn't stall waiting on approvals. Work done without permits can create title and resale problems, and some carriers will deny coverage for unpermitted repairs.

3. **Structural and rough-in work.** Framing, sheathing, subfloor, and any mechanical rough-in (electrical, plumbing, HVAC) that runs through affected spaces happens before walls close. This is the phase where hidden damage, a joist that looked intact but is fire-compromised at the core, or a drain line that shifted during a floor collapse, gets addressed rather than buried.

4. **Insulation and drywall.** Vapor barriers and insulation go in to current energy code before drywall. In Chicago's climate, this matters: a wall rebuilt without proper vapor management in an exterior assembly can develop condensation problems within a single heating season.

5. **Finishing and final inspection.** Taping, texture matching, paint, trim, flooring, and fixtures bring the space back to pre-loss appearance. Final inspection closes the permit and gives your carrier the documentation needed to close the claim.

## What separates a good reconstruction response from a bad one

The most common failure in post-disaster rebuilding isn't poor craftsmanship, it's an incomplete scope. When a scope misses a damaged header, doesn't account for code-required upgrades triggered by the repair, or underestimates the finishing work needed to match existing surfaces, the project either runs over budget or leaves visible evidence that something happened here.

Insurance adjusters are trained to look for scope accuracy. A scope that's vague on material specs ("drywall" instead of "5/8" Type X drywall, fire-rated") gives the carrier room to apply lower unit costs. A scope that doesn't include line items for debris removal, temporary protection, or code upgrades will generate a supplement fight mid-project.

Experienced reconstruction contractors also know when to stop and flag a problem rather than build over it. Framing members that appear structurally sound can carry hidden char or heat damage that compromises load capacity. Subfloor panels that dried out after a water loss may have delaminated internally even when the surface feels solid underfoot. Building over those conditions creates liability and, in some cases, safety risk.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Chicago's freeze-thaw cycle affects reconstruction scheduling in ways that matter. Exterior work, roofing, siding, foundation repair, has a narrower window than interior rebuilds, and starting exterior reconstruction in late fall can mean tarping and temporary protection through winter if the project runs long. Interior humidity during winter months also affects drywall finishing: low indoor humidity in heated spaces can cause joint compound to dry too fast and crack, requiring additional coats.

For losses that involve any exterior penetration, temporary weatherproofing is part of the scope from day one, not an afterthought.
