Fire damage rarely stops when the flames do. The restoration process that follows a house fire, or even a contained kitchen fire, unfolds in stages, and understanding those stages helps you ask the right questions, make better decisions with your insurance adjuster, and avoid mistakes that can make the damage worse. Here is what the process looks like from the moment the fire department clears the scene to the day you walk back into a fully restored home.

## What You're Actually Dealing With After a Fire

Most people think of fire damage as charred wood and melted plastic. The harder problem is everything the fire produced *without* burning it directly.

Smoke travels. It moves through HVAC ducts, seeps behind electrical outlets, and settles into insulation inside walls you cannot see. Soot, the fine black residue left by incomplete combustion, is acidic. On metal surfaces, it begins to etch and corrode within hours. On porous materials like drywall, grout, and upholstery, it bonds chemically and becomes significantly harder to remove the longer it sits.

Then there is the water. Firefighting leaves behind hundreds or thousands of gallons, soaking into subfloors, wicking up wall cavities, and saturating insulation. In Chicago's older housing stock, the two-flats, greystones, and bungalows built with plaster-and-lath walls, that moisture has nowhere obvious to go. It hides, and if it is not extracted and dried within the first day or two, mold colonization can begin.

Understanding these three overlapping problems, thermal damage, smoke and soot contamination, and water intrusion, is the foundation for understanding why restoration takes as long as it does.

## The First 24–48 Hours: Emergency Response and Stabilization

Once the fire marshal releases the property, the clock starts on several simultaneous threats. A restoration crew's first job is not cleaning, it is stopping additional damage from occurring.

**Board-up and tarping.** If windows are broken, doors are compromised, or the roof is exposed, the structure needs to be secured immediately. An unsecured property is a liability risk and an open invitation for weather, especially relevant in Chicago, where a cold front can push rain or snow through a broken window within hours of a fire.

**Water extraction and drying.** Any standing water left by suppression efforts needs to come out fast. Crews use truck-mounted extractors, air movers, and dehumidifiers to begin the drying process. Industrial drying equipment is not the same as a box fan from a hardware store, it moves and processes far more air volume and is monitored with moisture meters to confirm drying is actually happening inside structural cavities, not just at the surface.

**Contents inventory.** Before anything is moved or discarded, a thorough photo and written inventory of damaged contents protects your insurance claim. Restoration companies work alongside adjusters, and documentation created in those first hours is often the most important paperwork in the entire process.

## What NOT to Do Before Professionals Arrive

The instinct after a fire is to start cleaning immediately. That instinct, while understandable, can backfire.

- **Do not run your HVAC system.** If soot has entered the ductwork, and after most fires, it has, running the system redistributes contamination throughout every room in the house, including rooms the fire never touched.
- **Do not wipe soot with a wet cloth.** Soot is oily and acidic. Wiping it wet smears it deeper into porous surfaces and can permanently stain materials that might otherwise have been cleanable.
- **Do not discard anything before it is documented.** Even items that look destroyed may have salvage value or need to be listed for insurance purposes. Let the adjuster and restoration team assess first.
- **Do not assume the structure is safe to occupy.** Smoke inhalation risk does not end when visible smoke clears. Soot particles and off-gassing from burned synthetics, carpets, furniture foam, plastics, can remain at harmful concentrations in an enclosed space long after the fire is out.

## The Cleaning and Decontamination Phase

Once the structure is stabilized and dry, the detailed work begins. This is where fire damage restoration diverges most sharply from what most homeowners expect.

Different surfaces require different cleaning methods, and using the wrong one causes permanent damage. Dry sponging is used first on flat, painted surfaces to lift loose soot without smearing. Chemical sponges, dense, dry-cleaning blocks, are used on more delicate or textured surfaces. Thermal fogging and ozone treatment are used to neutralize odor molecules that have penetrated porous materials; they work chemically, not just masking the smell.

HVAC systems require their own cleaning protocol. Ducts, registers, and the air handler itself need to be cleaned and inspected before the system is used again. In Chicago's older multi-unit buildings, this step is especially important because ductwork often connects units in ways that are not obvious from the floor plan.

Structural materials that cannot be cleaned, heavily charred framing, contaminated insulation, soot-saturated drywall, are removed and replaced. The line between what is restorable and what needs to go is a professional judgment call, and it matters for both safety and cost.

## Smoke Odor: Why It Lingers and How It's Treated

Smoke odor is the complaint that outlasts everything else. Homeowners who have repainted walls and replaced flooring still sometimes smell smoke months later, because the odor is not in the paint or the floor. It is in the framing, the subfloor, the insulation, and the HVAC system behind them.

Effective odor elimination works in layers. Hydroxyl generators and ozone machines break down odor-causing molecules at a chemical level. Thermal fogging, a process that uses a heated, deodorizing fog to penetrate the same pathways smoke traveled, is often used in combination. Sealant primers applied to structural wood lock in residual odor before new drywall goes up.

If a contractor skips these steps and goes straight to reconstruction, the smell will return. It is worth asking specifically how odor is being treated before the rebuild phase begins.

## Reconstruction and the Path Back to Normal

The final phase, rebuilding what was removed, is where the timeline varies most. A kitchen fire that damaged one wall and the ceiling above the range might take a few weeks. A fire that spread through a floor of a two-flat and required structural repairs, full duct replacement, and extensive drywall work can take months.

Permitting matters here. Chicago requires permits for structural work, electrical, and plumbing, and the permit process adds time that is largely outside anyone's control. Factor that into your expectations when you are talking to your adjuster about temporary housing.

Throughout reconstruction, the restoration company and your insurance adjuster should be in regular communication. Scope changes, discovering additional damage once walls are opened, are common, and those changes need to be documented and approved before work proceeds.

## When to Call a Restoration Professional

If the fire was contained to a single appliance and the fire department did not need to suppress it with water, you may be able to handle cleanup yourself. If suppression water was used, if smoke traveled beyond the room of origin, or if you can smell smoke in rooms that were not near the fire, the damage is almost certainly more extensive than it looks from the surface.
