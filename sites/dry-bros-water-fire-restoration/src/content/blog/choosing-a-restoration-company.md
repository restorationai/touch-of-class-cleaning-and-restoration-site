Choosing a restoration company after water damage, a fire, or a mold discovery is one of the worst decisions to make under pressure. You're stressed, possibly displaced, and your phone is ringing with contractors you've never heard of. The short answer: vet credentials before you sign anything, confirm the company bills insurance directly, and never let urgency, real or manufactured, push you into a contract you haven't read. The rest of this guide walks you through exactly how to do that, step by step.

## Why the Restoration Industry Attracts Bad Actors

Restoration work is largely invisible. A crew tears open a wall, runs equipment for several days, and reassembles everything, and you have almost no way to evaluate whether the work was done correctly until months later, when mold reappears or a buyer's inspector finds residual moisture behind the baseboard.

That gap between service and verifiable outcome is what makes this industry a magnet for storm chasers and unlicensed operators. After a major weather event in the Chicago area, a basement flood from a backed-up sewer, a pipe burst during a cold snap, a kitchen fire, contractors from out of state sometimes arrive door-to-door within hours. Some are legitimate. Many are not.

The pressure to act fast is real. Standing water causes structural damage quickly, and smoke residue begins bonding to surfaces within hours of a fire. But signing with the wrong company can cost you more than waiting a day to do your homework.

## What to Check Before You Sign Anything

These are the four things that separate a legitimate restoration contractor from one you'll regret hiring.

**1. industry certification**
The Institute of Inspection, Cleaning and Restoration Certification sets the technical standards the industry runs on, the S500 for water damage, the S520 for mold, the S700 for fire and smoke. A company whose technicians hold industry credentials has been trained to those standards. Ask specifically which certifications the technicians on *your* job hold, not just whether the company is "certified."

**2. A physical, verifiable local presence**
Search the company name plus the city. Look for a real street address, a Google Business profile with a review history that spans more than the last few months, and a license number you can cross-reference with the Illinois Department of Financial and Professional Regulation or your local municipality. A company that appeared after last week's storm and has no history before it is a red flag.

**3. Direct insurance billing**
A reputable restoration company works with insurance carriers routinely. They know how to document a loss, moisture readings, photo logs, scope of work, in the format adjusters require. If a contractor asks you to pay out of pocket and "get reimbursed later," that's not how legitimate insurance billing works. Ask directly: do you bill my carrier, or do I pay you and submit myself?

**4. A written scope of work before work begins**
You should receive a written estimate that itemizes what will be removed, what equipment will be used, how long drying is expected to take, and what the monitoring schedule looks like. Verbal promises are unenforceable. If a contractor is reluctant to put the scope in writing before starting, walk away.

## Red Flags That Should Stop You Cold

Some warning signs are obvious in hindsight but easy to miss when you're panicked about a flooded basement or a smoke-damaged kitchen.

- **Demanding a signed authorization before they'll even assess the damage.** A walkthrough and moisture reading shouldn't require a contract.
- **Asking you to sign over your insurance benefits (an Assignment of Benefits).** In Illinois, this practice has been associated with inflated claims and billing disputes. You are not required to sign an AOB.
- **Unusually low bids that don't include monitoring or clearance testing.** Water damage drying isn't a one-day job. A bid that doesn't include return visits for moisture readings is almost certainly incomplete.
- **No mention of containment or air quality on a mold job.** Mold remediation without proper containment can spread spores to unaffected areas of the home. Any contractor who doesn't address this hasn't been trained to the S520 standard.
- **Pressure to start immediately with no written documentation.** Urgency is sometimes real, but a professional can stabilize a loss and still take 30 minutes to write up a scope.

## What the Restoration Process Actually Looks Like

Understanding the process helps you evaluate whether a contractor is cutting corners.

**Water damage restoration** follows a defined sequence: extract standing water, establish drying goals based on the material type and baseline readings, place drying equipment (air movers and dehumidifiers), monitor daily until the structure reaches those goals, then remove equipment and document final readings. The professional drying standards defines this process. A job that skips daily monitoring or pulls equipment early because "it feels dry" is not complete.

**Fire and smoke damage restoration** is more complex than it looks. Smoke travels through HVAC systems, settles into porous materials, and leaves an acidic residue that continues to corrode surfaces after the fire is out. Soot on a painted wall isn't just a cosmetic problem, it's actively damaging the surface underneath. Restoration involves cleaning or removing affected materials, deodorizing with thermal fogging or hydroxyl generators, and often sealing surfaces before repainting. A company that only addresses what's visibly charred is leaving work undone.

**Mold remediation** requires identifying the moisture source first. Removing mold without fixing the underlying leak or humidity problem means it comes back. A proper remediation includes containment to prevent cross-contamination, removal of affected materials to clean wood or concrete, HEPA vacuuming, and, ideally, a post-remediation clearance test by an independent industrial hygienist. Note that clearance testing is separate from remediation; the same company shouldn't be testing its own work.

## How to Evaluate a Company's Track Record in Chicago

Chicago's housing stock skews older, and older buildings come with specific vulnerabilities: galvanized plumbing that corrodes from the inside out, basement drain systems that can't keep up with heavy rain, and plaster-and-lath construction that holds moisture long after the surface reads dry. A restoration company that works regularly in the city should understand these dynamics, not just apply a generic drying protocol designed for newer construction.

When you're evaluating reviews, look for specifics: Did the reviewer mention what type of damage they had? Did they describe the monitoring process or the documentation? A pattern of reviews that say only "great service, highly recommend" tells you less than a handful of detailed accounts describing what the crew actually did.

You can also ask the company directly: what moisture content do you target for wood framing in a basement job? How do you handle a situation where drying goals aren't being met? A contractor who can answer those questions without hesitation has done the work before.
