Mold doesn't always announce itself with a visible black patch on the wall. More often, it grows in the dark, inside wall cavities, under bathroom tile, behind baseboards, beneath subfloors, for weeks or months before anyone notices. If you've had a leak, a flood, or even just persistent humidity, and something feels off about your home, these seven signs can help you figure out whether mold is the problem.

## The 7 Signs to Look For

### 1. A musty smell that moves around
The most reliable early signal isn't something you see, it's something you smell. Mold produces microbial volatile organic compounds (mVOCs) as it metabolizes organic material, and that earthy, damp-basement odor is the result. If the smell is stronger in one room, near a particular wall, or when your HVAC kicks on, that's directional information. Chicago homes with forced-air systems can circulate mold spores from a single source throughout the entire house, so a smell that seems to come from everywhere may actually have one origin point.

### 2. Warped, bubbling, or soft drywall
Drywall absorbs moisture like a sponge. When the paper facing bubbles, the surface feels soft under pressure, or a section of wall looks slightly bowed, water has gotten in, and if it's been there more than 24 to 48 hours, mold colonization is already possible. Run your hand along the baseboard line in bathrooms, laundry rooms, and anywhere near exterior walls. Softness at floor level often means water wicked up from below.

### 3. Discoloration that isn't quite a stain
Not all mold is black. Depending on the species and the surface, it can look gray, green, white, or even orange. A yellowish ring on a ceiling tile is often a dried water stain, but if the center of that ring has a fuzzy or slightly raised texture, it's worth a closer look. Mold on grout lines in a shower tends to start as a gray haze before it darkens. On wood framing inside a wall, it can look like a faint green tint that wipes off but comes back within days.

### 4. Allergy or respiratory symptoms that improve when you leave home
This one is easy to dismiss as seasonal allergies, especially in Chicago where spring pollen counts can be high. But if you or someone in your household notices that sneezing, eye irritation, or a persistent cough clears up when you're away for a few days, and returns when you come back, the indoor air quality in your home is worth investigating. Mold spores are a common trigger for asthma and allergic rhinitis. This isn't a diagnosis; it's a pattern worth paying attention to.

### 5. A history of water intrusion you thought was resolved
A pipe that was repaired, a roof leak that stopped after a patch, a basement that flooded once and seemed to dry out, these are the situations where hidden mold most often develops. The surface dries. The structure underneath doesn't. Chicago's older housing stock, which includes a large number of brick two-flats and greystones built in the early to mid-1900s, often has plaster walls over wood lath. That lath can stay saturated long after the plaster surface reads dry to the touch, creating ideal conditions for mold growth that won't be visible for months.

### 6. Rust stains or efflorescence on basement walls
White, chalky deposits on concrete or masonry (called efflorescence) form when water moves through the wall and carries dissolved salts to the surface. It's a sign of ongoing moisture migration, not a mold problem by itself, but wherever moisture migrates, mold can follow. Rust streaks near floor drains or along the base of a water heater tell the same story. These are indicators, not proof, but they're worth treating as a prompt to look further.

### 7. Peeling paint or wallpaper with no obvious cause
Paint that peels from the inside out, where the wall surface itself seems to be separating rather than the paint film flaking, is usually a moisture problem. If there's no plumbing on the other side of the wall, check whether the exterior cladding or flashing might be allowing water infiltration. In Chicago's freeze-thaw cycles, brick mortar and window flashing take repeated stress each winter, and small gaps that seem cosmetic can allow enough moisture intrusion to sustain mold growth inside the wall cavity.

## What To Do If You Recognize These Signs

Start by narrowing the location. If you smell mold, move room by room and note where the smell is strongest. Check the areas most likely to have had water contact: under sinks, around the toilet base, along the perimeter of the tub or shower, in the corner of the basement where two exterior walls meet.

If you find visible mold on a non-porous surface, ceramic tile, glass, a metal pipe, you can clean a small area (generally under 10 square feet) with appropriate precautions. Wear an N95 respirator, gloves, and eye protection. Do not use bleach on porous materials like drywall or wood; it doesn't penetrate deeply enough to kill the root structure, and it adds moisture that can make the problem worse.

If the mold is on drywall, wood framing, insulation, or carpet, cleaning the surface won't resolve it. Those materials typically need to be removed.

## What Not To Do

**Don't run fans or your HVAC system if you suspect mold in the ductwork or near a supply vent.** Airflow spreads spores to unaffected areas of the house. If the mold source is localized, keeping air movement minimal until the source is identified and contained is the better approach.

**Don't paint over discoloration** to see if it comes back. It will, and now you've added a layer that makes the extent of the problem harder to assess.

**Don't ignore the moisture source.** Mold is a symptom. The cause is water. Removing mold without addressing why the moisture is there means it returns, often within weeks.

## When To Call a Professional

Call a mold remediation professional when:

- The affected area is larger than roughly 10 square feet
- The mold is inside a wall, ceiling, or subfloor cavity
- You or a household member has a compromised immune system, asthma, or a known mold sensitivity
- The mold followed a sewage backup or flooding from a contaminated source
- You've cleaned visible mold and it has returned
- You can smell mold but cannot find it

A professional assessment typically includes moisture mapping with a thermal camera and pin-type meters to find wet areas that aren't visible, air sampling if indicated, and a written scope of work before any material is removed. In Chicago, permitted work that involves structural elements may require a licensed contractor, worth confirming before demolition begins.

## The Remediation and Recovery Process

Professional mold remediation follows a containment-first approach: the affected area is sealed off with polyethylene sheeting and negative air pressure to prevent cross-contamination while work is underway. Affected porous materials are removed, bagged, and disposed of properly. The remaining structure is cleaned, treated, and dried to documented moisture levels before any reconstruction begins.

Drying takes time. Rushing it, closing walls before the framing reaches equilibrium moisture content, is one of the most common reasons mold returns after remediation. A reputable contractor will use moisture readings to determine when reconstruction can safely begin, not a fixed number of days.

If the mold followed a covered loss (a burst pipe, an appliance leak, storm-related water intrusion), your homeowner's insurance may cover remediation costs. Document everything with photos before anything is moved or cleaned.
