The short answer: it depends on *how* the water got in. Homeowners insurance typically covers sudden, accidental water damage, a pipe that bursts overnight, a washing machine hose that lets go, an ice dam that forces water under your shingles. It almost never covers damage that built up slowly over time, and it does not cover flooding from outside your home. Understanding that one distinction, sudden versus gradual, internal versus external, will answer about 80 percent of the coverage questions you'll face after a water loss.

## What Standard Homeowners Insurance Usually Covers

Most HO-3 policies (the standard form sold across Illinois) are written to cover "sudden and accidental" discharge or overflow of water from inside the home. In plain terms, that means:

- A supply line to your refrigerator, dishwasher, or washing machine fails without warning.
- A pipe freezes and bursts, common in Chicago's older two-flats and greystones, where uninsulated exterior walls can drop below freezing during a polar vortex.
- Your water heater cracks and empties onto the basement floor.
- A toilet overflows because of a sudden mechanical failure (not because it's been running for weeks).
- Rain enters through a roof opening created by a covered peril, like wind damage.

If the event was sudden, accidental, and started inside your home, there's a reasonable chance your dwelling coverage and personal property coverage will apply, subject to your deductible and any policy sublimits.

One thing Chicago homeowners should know: the city's housing stock skews old. A significant share of the residential buildings in neighborhoods like Logan Square, Pilsen, and Beverly were built before 1950, which means galvanized steel supply lines, cast-iron drains, and knob-and-tube wiring that can complicate both the loss and the claim. If your home is in that vintage, it's worth reviewing your policy before you have a problem.

## What Homeowners Insurance Almost Never Covers

This is where most disputes happen. Carriers routinely deny claims for:

**Gradual damage.** If a slow drip behind a cabinet has been rotting the subfloor for two years, the insurer will argue you knew or should have known. Adjusters look for staining rings, warped wood, and peeling paint, evidence the problem wasn't sudden. Even if you genuinely didn't notice, gradual damage exclusions are broadly written and frequently upheld.

**Flooding from outside.** Rising water from a river, a storm surge, or a heavy rain that overwhelms street drains and backs into your basement is not covered by a standard homeowners policy. That's a separate product, the National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) or a private flood policy. Chicago sits on flat glacial lake plain with a combined sewer system; during heavy rain events, basement backup is a real and recurring risk in many neighborhoods. If you don't have a flood or sewer-backup endorsement, you may have no coverage at all for that scenario.

**Sewer and drain backup.** This one surprises people. Even if the water came from inside your pipes, a backup that originates in the municipal sewer system is typically excluded unless you've added a specific endorsement. Many Chicago homeowners add this rider after their first basement backup, it's usually inexpensive relative to the risk.

**Neglected maintenance.** A roof that's been leaking for years, gutters that were never cleaned, a sump pump that hasn't been tested since you moved in, if the carrier can show the damage resulted from deferred maintenance, the claim is likely to be denied.

**Mold remediation (sometimes).** Policies vary widely here. Some cover mold if it's a direct result of a covered water loss; others cap mold coverage at a low sublimit or exclude it entirely. Read your declarations page carefully.

## How to Read Your Own Policy (Without a Law Degree)

You don't need to read the whole document. Focus on three sections:

1. **The declarations page.** This is the summary sheet, your coverage limits, your deductible, and any endorsements you've added. Look for "water backup," "sump overflow," or "service line" endorsements.
2. **Section I, Perils Insured Against.** This lists what's covered. Look for language like "sudden and accidental discharge" and note any exceptions.
3. **Section I, Exclusions.** This is the list of what's *not* covered. Flood, earth movement, and neglect are almost always here. Read every exclusion that mentions water.

If you find language you don't understand, call your agent, not the claims line, and ask them to explain it before you have a loss. That conversation is free.

## What to Do Immediately After a Water Loss

If you're reading this because water is happening right now, here's the sequence that matters:

1. **Stop the source.** Turn off the main water shutoff valve if a pipe is involved. In most Chicago homes it's near the front foundation wall or in the utility room. If you don't know where yours is, find it today, not during a flood.
2. **Cut power to affected areas.** Water and electricity are the immediate life-safety concern. If there's any chance water has reached an electrical panel, outlet, or appliance, shut off the circuit breaker for that area before you enter.
3. **Document everything before you touch it.** Take photos and video of standing water, affected materials, and the source of the loss. This documentation is your claim. Don't move or discard anything until you've captured it.
4. **Call your insurance company.** Report the loss promptly. Most policies require "timely notice", waiting days can give the carrier a basis to question the claim.
5. **Begin mitigation, but keep records.** Policies also require you to prevent further damage. That means extracting standing water, moving salvageable belongings out of wet areas, and ventilating the space. Save every receipt for anything you spend.

What you should *not* do: start tearing out wet drywall or flooring before the adjuster has seen the damage, unless a professional has advised it to prevent mold growth and you've documented the pre-demo condition thoroughly.

## When the Claim Gets Complicated

Insurance adjusters are not adversaries, but they work for the carrier. Their job is to assess the loss accurately, which sometimes means finding the gradual-damage exclusion, the maintenance exclusion, or the flood exclusion that limits what gets paid.

If your claim is denied or the settlement offer seems low, you have options: request a written explanation of the denial, ask your agent to advocate internally, or hire a public adjuster who works on your behalf (they typically charge a percentage of the settlement). For large losses, a property insurance attorney is worth a consultation.
