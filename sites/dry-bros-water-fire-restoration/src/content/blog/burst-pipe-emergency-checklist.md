If a pipe just burst in your home, stop reading and go turn off the water first. Find your main shutoff valve, in Chicago homes it's usually in the basement near the front foundation wall, or in a utility closet, and turn it clockwise until it stops. Once the water is off, come back here. The steps below walk you through everything from the first five minutes to the weeks of drying and repair that follow, so you know what to expect and what mistakes to avoid.

## The First Five Minutes: Stop the Water, Cut the Power

A burst pipe can push dozens of gallons into your home every minute. The faster you stop the source, the less you're dealing with afterward.

1. **Shut off the main water supply.** Don't waste time looking for the branch valve near the break, go straight to the main. In most Chicago two-flats and bungalows, it's a gate valve or ball valve on the street-side of the basement wall.
2. **Turn off electricity to affected areas.** If water is near outlets, light fixtures, or your electrical panel, flip the breakers for those zones before you step into standing water. If you're not sure which breakers to pull, shut off the main breaker.
3. **Open a faucet on the lowest floor.** This relieves pressure still sitting in the lines and lets the remaining water drain down rather than seep through walls and ceilings.
4. **Call your plumber.** The pipe itself needs a physical repair before water can be restored to the house. A restoration crew can begin drying while the plumber works, these two jobs happen in parallel, not in sequence.

If the burst is in a wall or ceiling and you can't see it, look for the signs: a bulging or discolored ceiling, paint bubbling off drywall, or the sound of water running when every fixture is off. Chicago's older housing stock, much of it built before 1960 with galvanized steel supply lines, is especially prone to interior pipe failures that don't announce themselves until significant water has already spread inside the wall cavity.

## What to Do While You Wait

Once the water is off and the power situation is safe, you have a window to limit secondary damage before help arrives.

- **Move valuables, furniture, and rugs off wet flooring.** Water wicks upward through carpet padding and wood in minutes. A rug left on a hardwood floor traps moisture and accelerates warping.
- **Mop or wet-vac standing water if you can do so safely.** Every inch of standing water removed now is less structural damage later. Don't use a standard household vacuum, it isn't designed for liquids.
- **Take photos and video before touching anything else.** Walk every affected room and document water lines on walls, wet materials, and the pipe location. Your insurance adjuster will want this, and it's much harder to reconstruct after cleanup begins.
- **Open interior doors and windows if outdoor temperatures allow.** In Chicago winters, opening windows isn't practical, but running your furnace and any box fans you have will help begin air movement.
- **Check on neighboring units if you're in a condo or two-flat.** Water follows gravity and framing channels. A burst on the second floor can be soaking a first-floor unit or a shared basement before anyone notices.

## What NOT to Do After a Burst Pipe

Some instincts that feel helpful actually make the situation worse or complicate your insurance claim.

- **Don't use a hair dryer or space heater to dry walls.** Surface heat evaporates moisture at the face of the drywall while the framing and insulation behind it stay saturated. You'll think it's dry when it isn't, and mold can begin colonizing wet framing within 24 to 48 hours in the right conditions.
- **Don't tear out drywall before documenting.** It's tempting to start ripping out wet material, but your adjuster may need to see the damage in place. Photograph first, then remove if necessary to prevent mold growth.
- **Don't assume it's dry because it looks dry.** Moisture meters read the actual moisture content of wood and drywall, your hand can't. Water behind plaster can read completely dry at the surface while the lath underneath stays saturated for days.
- **Don't delay calling your insurance company.** Most homeowner policies require you to report water losses promptly. Waiting, even a few days, can give a carrier grounds to question whether the damage was pre-existing or caused by neglect.
- **Don't run HVAC if there's any chance of sewage contamination.** If the burst involved a drain line rather than a supply line, running forced air can spread contaminants through ductwork.

## When to Call a Water Damage Professional

A mop and a fan are enough for a small appliance leak caught in the first hour. A burst pipe is almost never that situation.

Call a water damage restoration company when:

- Water has spread beyond one room or affected more than one floor
- The pipe burst inside a wall, ceiling, or under a subfloor
- Flooring is buckled, soft, or separating at seams
- You can smell something musty within 24–48 hours of the event (that's mold beginning, not finishing)
- Your basement took on water and has a finished ceiling or insulated walls
- The loss involves a drain line, toilet overflow, or any water you're not certain is clean

Professional water damage restoration involves more than fans. Technicians use thermal imaging cameras to find moisture that's migrated into wall cavities, industrial air movers and dehumidifiers calibrated to the specific volume of wet material, and moisture logs that document drying progress over days, the kind of documentation that supports an insurance claim and proves the structure was actually dried, not just surface-dried.

For appliance-related bursts, a supply line behind a refrigerator, a washing machine hose that let go, the affected area is often more contained, but the same principle applies: if water reached flooring, subfloor, or cabinetry, a moisture meter is the only way to know whether it's actually dry. Appliance leak cleanup that's done by feel rather than measurement is how a small incident becomes a mold problem three weeks later.

## The Recovery Process: What Comes After the Emergency

Once the pipe is repaired and the restoration crew is on-site, drying typically takes several days, not hours. Here's what that process generally looks like:

1. **Extraction.** Any remaining standing water is removed with truck-mounted or portable extraction equipment.
2. **Demolition of unsalvageable material.** Wet drywall, insulation, and flooring that can't be dried in place are removed to expose the structure underneath. This is called a "flood cut" when it involves cutting drywall at a horizontal line above the water line.
3. **Drying.** Air movers and dehumidifiers run continuously, usually 24 hours a day, until moisture readings in walls, floors, and framing reach acceptable levels. In Chicago's humid summers, this can take longer than the same job in a drier climate.
4. **Monitoring.** A technician returns daily or every other day to read moisture levels and adjust equipment placement.
5. **Reconstruction.** Once the structure is certified dry, drywall, flooring, and trim are replaced. This phase is separate from mitigation and may be handled by the same company or a different contractor depending on scope.
