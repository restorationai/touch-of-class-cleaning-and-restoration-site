Testing for mold starts with your nose and your eyes, a musty smell that won't go away, a dark stain that keeps coming back after you clean it, or unexplained allergy symptoms that ease up when you leave the house. If any of those sound familiar, you have two realistic options: a DIY test kit from a hardware store, or a professional inspection. The short answer is that DIY kits can confirm mold is *somewhere* in your home, but they rarely tell you where it's hiding, how much is there, or whether the species present is one you need to worry about. A professional inspection does all three.

## What DIY Mold Test Kits Actually Do

Most consumer kits, the kind sold at home improvement stores for $10–$50, work by leaving a petri dish open in a room for a set period, then mailing the dish to a lab or watching it yourself for growth. A few use tape-lift samples you press against a surface.

Here's the problem: mold spores are everywhere. Open a petri dish in any room of any home and you will almost certainly get some growth. The test is technically detecting mold, but it isn't telling you whether the concentration in your home is elevated above normal outdoor levels, what species is present, or where the source colony is. A positive result from a DIY kit is a little like a smoke detector going off, it tells you something is burning, not where the fire is or how big it is.

That said, DIY kits are not useless. They can be a reasonable first step if:

- You want documented evidence before calling your landlord
- You're buying a home and want a quick screen before paying for a full inspection
- You've already had remediation done and want a rough check before the professional clearance test

Just go in knowing what you're buying: a yes/no answer with no address attached.

## What a Professional Mold Inspection Covers

A certified mold inspector does several things a petri dish cannot.

**Moisture mapping.** Mold needs water. An inspector uses a non-invasive moisture meter and sometimes an infrared camera to find wet areas behind walls, under flooring, or above ceiling tiles, places where a colony can grow for months before it's visible. In Chicago's older housing stock, this matters a lot: balloon-frame construction from the early 1900s has interior wall cavities that run floor-to-roof with no fireblocking, and a slow leak from a second-floor bathroom can wick down two stories before it shows up as a stain.

**Air sampling.** The inspector draws air through a cassette that captures spores, then sends it to an accredited lab. The lab reports spore counts by species and compares them to an outdoor control sample taken the same day. If your indoor count is significantly higher than outdoor, or if a species like *Stachybotrys* (often called black mold) shows up indoors but not outside, you have a finding that means something.

**Surface sampling.** If there's a visible stain or suspected growth, a tape-lift or swab sample identifies exactly what's there.

**A written report.** This is what your insurance carrier, your doctor, or a future buyer will actually want to see. A professional report names the species, quantifies the levels, identifies probable moisture sources, and recommends next steps. A petri dish result does not do any of that.

## How to Read Your Home for Mold Before Any Test

Before you spend money on any test, do a systematic walkthrough. Mold follows water, so trace every place water has ever been or could be.

1. **Check under every sink.** Look at the back of the cabinet, not just the visible floor. Slow drips from supply lines or drain connections leave dark staining on particleboard long before they drip onto the floor.
2. **Look at the ceiling below any bathroom.** A grout line that's been failing for years will eventually let water into the subfloor. The ceiling below is often the first visible sign.
3. **Smell the HVAC return vents.** Mold growing in ductwork or on the air handler coil gets distributed through the whole house. A musty smell that's stronger at a vent than anywhere else is a flag.
4. **Check basement rim joists.** In Chicago's climate, the rim joist, the framing that sits on top of the foundation wall, is a chronic condensation point. It's also frequently uninsulated or poorly insulated in homes built before the 1980s. Pull back any insulation and look at the wood.
5. **Look at window frames, especially in bedrooms.** Condensation from cold winters and humid summers leaves mold on sills and in the corners of frames. This is usually surface mold and lower-risk, but it indicates a humidity problem that may be more serious elsewhere.

If you find visible growth larger than roughly 10 square feet, about the size of a standard ceiling tile, the EPA recommends professional remediation rather than DIY cleanup, regardless of species.

## What Not to Do When You Suspect Mold

A few common mistakes make the situation worse before any test happens.

- **Don't disturb visible growth before sampling.** Scrubbing or bleaching a mold colony before a professional can sample it destroys the evidence and can release a concentrated burst of spores into the air.
- **Don't run a fan in a room with suspected mold.** Airflow moves spores to unaffected areas. If you've found a wet spot or visible growth, close the room off rather than ventilating it.
- **Don't assume bleach solves it.** Bleach kills surface mold on non-porous materials like tile, but it doesn't penetrate porous materials like drywall or wood. The surface looks clean; the colony inside the material is still alive.
- **Don't ignore the water source.** Mold is a symptom of a moisture problem. Cleaning up mold without fixing the leak, the humidity issue, or the drainage problem means it comes back, usually within weeks.

## When to Skip the DIY Kit and Call a Professional

Some situations call for a professional inspection from the start, not as a follow-up:

- You've had a water loss, a burst pipe, a sewage backup, a roof leak, and more than 24 to 48 hours passed before the area was fully dried. Mold can begin colonizing porous materials within that window.
- Someone in the home has respiratory symptoms, asthma, or a compromised immune system and the cause isn't clear.
- You're buying or selling a home and need documentation for a transaction.
- A previous remediation was done and you need a clearance test to confirm it was successful.
- The visible growth is extensive, is in an HVAC system, or is in a crawl space or attic where the full scope is hard to assess.

For Chicago homeowners specifically, the combination of cold winters, humid summers, and aging building envelopes, a lot of the city's housing stock dates to the 1920s through 1950s, creates more chronic moisture exposure than many climates. That doesn't mean every home has a mold problem, but it does mean moisture intrusion is common enough that a professional inspection is rarely a waste of money when there's a real reason to suspect one.
