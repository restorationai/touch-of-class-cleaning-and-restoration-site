## Why Evanston Properties See Burst Pipe Issues

Evanston's housing stock skews old. Much of the city was built out between the 1890s and the 1940s, and a significant portion of that construction used galvanized steel supply lines that are well past their service life. Galvanized pipe corrodes from the inside out, narrowing the interior diameter over decades until the wall is thin enough that a hard freeze finishes the job. Even homes that have had partial replumbing often retain original branch lines in places that are easy to overlook, inside finished walls, under concrete basement floors, or running through uninsulated attic chases.

The proximity to Lake Michigan adds another layer of risk. Cold air off the lake can drive wind chills significantly below the ambient temperature, and north- and east-facing exterior walls in Evanston homes, particularly in the blocks closest to the lakefront, are exposed to that wind in a way that inland Chicago suburbs are not. Pipes running through those walls without adequate insulation are vulnerable during any sustained cold snap, not just record-breaking ones.

Evanston's sewer and water infrastructure is also worth noting: the city operates its own water utility drawing from Lake Michigan, and when a water line breaks inside a home, the incoming pressure from that municipal supply can push a substantial volume of water into the structure before a homeowner can locate the shutoff.

## Our Burst Pipe Cleanup and Repair Process in Evanston

The first priority when we arrive is confirming the water source is off. If a homeowner hasn't been able to locate the main shutoff, often in a basement utility area or a pit near the foundation, we help identify it before anything else. With the flow stopped, we assess what the water has reached.

Burst pipe losses in Evanston's older homes follow predictable patterns. Water released inside a plaster-and-lath wall cavity doesn't behave the way it does in modern drywall construction. Plaster is dense and slow to release moisture; it can read close to normal on a surface moisture meter while the lath and framing behind it remain saturated. We use thermal imaging alongside pin and pinless meters to map the actual wet zone, which is almost always larger than what's visible.

Extraction comes next, standing water from floors, water trapped in subfloor assemblies, and water that has migrated into basement spaces. Evanston's older homes frequently have true basements with stone or brick foundation walls, and water that reaches those spaces can wick laterally through the masonry. Drying equipment placement accounts for that: dehumidifiers and air movers are positioned to address the masonry, not just the finished surfaces.

Once drying is underway, we document conditions daily and adjust equipment based on readings. When materials are confirmed dry, we coordinate with licensed plumbers for the pipe repair itself and can manage the scope of any reconstruction needed to restore walls, ceilings, or flooring.

## Evanston Insurance Coordination

Most standard homeowner policies cover sudden and accidental water discharge, which is what a burst pipe is, but the documentation has to be thorough. We photograph affected materials before anything is moved, log moisture readings at each monitoring visit, and produce a written scope that matches the format carriers expect. That record matters if there's any question about what was wet and for how long.

Evanston also has a mix of single-family homes, two- and three-flats, and larger multi-unit buildings, particularly in the denser blocks near the Northwestern University campus and along the main transit corridors. In multi-unit properties, a burst pipe on one floor can affect units above and below, and coordinating claims across multiple policy holders adds complexity. We can document damage across units and communicate with multiple adjusters when a loss crosses unit lines.

## Local Note

In Evanston's older two- and three-flat buildings, common throughout the city's central and southern neighborhoods, the plumbing stacks often run through interior chases that were never insulated because they were assumed to stay warm from the heated living spaces around them. When a building sits vacant, loses heat during a utility interruption, or has a unit that's been unoccupied through a cold stretch, those chase temperatures can drop faster than owners expect. A pipe failure in a shared stack can release water into multiple units simultaneously. If you're managing a rental property and the heat goes out, even briefly, it's worth checking those units before temperatures recover, by the time water appears at a ceiling, it has usually been moving through the structure for hours.
