## Why Des Plaines Properties See Water Damage Issues

The geology under Des Plaines works against homeowners during wet weather. The heavy clay content in Cook County's soil resists drainage, meaning groundwater pressure builds against foundation walls during prolonged rain rather than dispersing. Many neighborhoods in Des Plaines were developed in the mid-20th century, and homes from that era commonly have block or poured concrete basements that were not waterproofed to modern standards. When the water table rises after a multi-day rain event, something the Chicago metro sees regularly in spring and early summer, those older foundations can seep or fail at the cove joint where the floor meets the wall.

The city's combined sewer system is another factor. During intense storms, combined sewers can surcharge and push water back through floor drains or sump pits before a pump can respond. That's not a maintenance failure on the homeowner's part, it's a capacity issue that affects a significant portion of the older housing stock in Des Plaines. Knowing that distinction matters when documenting a loss for an insurance claim.

## Our Water Damage Restoration Process in Des Plaines

The first step on any water damage call is containment and assessment. Before extraction equipment is placed, the source of the water has to be confirmed and stopped, a detail that sounds obvious but is often skipped when a crew rushes straight to pumping. Once the source is controlled, standing water is removed using truck-mounted extraction units capable of pulling hundreds of gallons quickly.

After bulk water removal, the work shifts to what you cannot see. In Des Plaines homes with finished basements, a common feature in the ranch-style and split-level construction that dominates much of the city, water migrates behind drywall, under subfloor, and into wall cavities well before surface readings show a problem. Thermal imaging and penetrating moisture meters map the actual wet zone, which is almost always larger than the visible damage. Drying equipment, industrial air movers and refrigerant or desiccant dehumidifiers, is then positioned based on that map, not guesswork. Moisture readings are logged daily until structural materials reach their target dry standard.

If flooring or drywall has to come out to allow drying or prevent mold colonization, that work is documented with photographs and measurements before demolition begins. That documentation becomes part of the claim file.

## Local Note

Homes in Des Plaines built in the 1950s and 1960s frequently have plaster-over-lath wall construction rather than modern drywall. Plaster absorbs water more slowly than drywall, which can make surface readings look deceptively normal while the wood lath and framing behind it stay saturated for days. Crews unfamiliar with this construction type sometimes pull equipment too early based on surface moisture alone. We probe behind the plaster layer and monitor the framing independently, because in a mid-century Des Plaines home, the wall can feel dry to the touch and still be holding enough moisture to support mold growth within a week.
