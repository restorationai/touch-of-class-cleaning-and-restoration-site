## Why Skokie's Building Stock Shapes Fire Damage Recovery

Skokie grew rapidly in the 1950s and 1960s, and that era's construction methods show up in almost every fire job here. Plaster-and-lath walls, far more common in Skokie than in newer suburbs, absorb smoke particulates differently than modern drywall. The porous lime surface holds odor compounds that a surface wipe-down won't touch; full deodorization requires thermal fogging or hydroxyl treatment that penetrates into the wall cavity itself.

The same postwar building era means older electrical systems, galvanized plumbing, and in some cases knob-and-tube wiring that was never updated. After a fire, the structural assessment has to account for what was already present before the event, not just what the fire damaged. Restoration scopes in Skokie frequently involve coordination with the village's building department for permits covering electrical, plumbing, and structural repairs, which adds a planning step that newer-construction markets don't always require.

## Our Fire Damage Restoration Process in Skokie

Every fire loss is different, but the sequence of work follows a consistent logic designed to stop damage from compounding while the full scope is being documented.

**Board-up and stabilization** comes first. Open windows, compromised rooflines, and unsecured doors let weather in and invite secondary losses, especially during Chicago-area winters when a single cold night can freeze standing water left by suppression hoses.

**Soot and smoke mapping** follows. Smoke travels through HVAC ducts, wall chases, and attic spaces far beyond the fire's origin point. In Skokie's older homes, that means checking plaster ceilings, basement joists, and any shared wall cavities in attached garages or two-flat configurations.

**Structural drying** runs in parallel when suppression water is present. Firefighting water soaks into subfloors and wall assemblies; if it isn't extracted and dried properly, mold colonization can begin within days, layering a second remediation problem onto an already complex loss.

**Deodorization and content cleaning** address the smoke residue that lingers long after visible soot is removed. Oily smoke from synthetic materials behaves differently from dry smoke left by fast-burning wood, and the cleaning chemistry has to match the residue type.

**Reconstruction** closes out the project, framing, drywall, insulation, finishes, so the property returns to a livable condition rather than stopping at the remediation stage.

## Local Note

Skokie's postwar two-flats and coach houses often share a single attic or a continuous soffit line between units. After a fire in one unit, smoke and heat routinely travel into the adjacent unit's attic space, sometimes without any visible char or scorch marks on the shared wall below. It's a pattern worth checking specifically in Skokie's older attached housing, because a unit that looks unaffected at eye level can still have significant smoke infiltration above the ceiling line. Missing that during the initial assessment means the odor problem resurfaces after restoration is supposedly complete.
