## Restoration emergencies common in Skokie

Skokie sits on the flat glacial lake plain of northeastern Cook County, and that geography shapes what goes wrong in local homes. The soil beneath most of the village is dense, poorly draining clay, the kind that holds moisture against foundation walls after a heavy rain and transmits hydrostatic pressure directly into basement floors and block walls. Spring snowmelt combined with the saturated ground that follows a Lake Michigan storm system is a reliable recipe for basement flooding across the village's residential blocks.

The housing stock compounds the risk. The majority of Skokie's single-family homes were built between the late 1940s and the early 1970s, when basement construction was standard and drain tile systems were installed with materials that have now aged past their service life. Aging clay or cast-iron drain tile fails quietly, often the first sign is water sheeting across a basement floor during a storm that neighbors handle without incident. The village also carries a meaningful share of two- and three-flat buildings and garden-style condominiums, where a single plumbing failure on an upper floor can affect multiple units and trigger disputes about scope and responsibility before a crew even arrives.

Fire and smoke calls follow a different seasonal pattern. Heating equipment, furnaces, space heaters, fireplaces that haven't been serviced, accounts for a significant share of residential fires during the Chicago-area winter. Smoke from a contained kitchen or utility-room fire travels farther than most homeowners expect; it follows HVAC ductwork and pressure differentials and deposits an oily film on surfaces in rooms that never saw flames.

## Building stock, site conditions, and permits in Skokie

Most of Skokie's single-family housing dates from the postwar construction boom, meaning full basements are the norm rather than the exception. Wall assemblies from that era typically used plaster over wood or metal lath, a system that holds moisture differently than modern drywall and can read dry at the surface while the lath and framing behind it remain saturated. Plumbing in homes of this vintage commonly includes galvanized steel supply lines, which corrode from the inside out and can fail without visible exterior warning, and cast-iron drain stacks that are serviceable but aging.

Homes built before the mid-1970s may contain asbestos-containing materials in floor tile, pipe insulation, and joint compound, as well as lead-based paint, both of which affect how demolition and remediation work is scoped and what testing precedes it. The dense clay soil across the village drains slowly and exerts sustained pressure against basement walls and floor slabs, making foundation waterproofing condition a relevant factor in every water-loss assessment. Structural repair and reconstruction permits in Skokie are issued through the village's Community Development department; the scope of the damage determines whether a permit is required before work begins.
