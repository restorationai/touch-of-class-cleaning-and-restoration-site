## Why Comanche Properties Face Distinct Fire Damage Challenges

The residential building stock in Comanche skews older. Many homes in and around the city were built with wood-frame construction, pier-and-beam foundations, and interior finishes — plaster over wood lath, tongue-and-groove pine ceilings, solid wood trim — that behave very differently from modern drywall when exposed to heat and smoke. Plaster absorbs odor compounds deeply, and smoke residue in tongue-and-groove wood can off-gas for weeks if not treated at the source rather than just at the surface.

Comanche County's climate adds another layer. The region averages low annual humidity compared to much of Texas, which means smoke odor dries into surfaces faster and bonds more stubbornly. At the same time, the area's periodic high winds — especially in late spring and fall — can spread embers and extend fire involvement to outbuildings, fencing, and adjacent structures before the fire department arrives. Post-fire, those same winds carry fine soot particles back into a structure through gaps around windows and doors, re-contaminating areas that appeared unaffected.

## Our Fire and Smoke Restoration Process in Comanche

Every fire loss is different, but the sequence of work follows a disciplined path that the IICRC S700 standard defines for fire and smoke restoration.

**Stabilization and safety assessment** come first. Before any cleaning begins, the structure is evaluated for compromised framing, utility hazards, and roof or wall openings that need emergency board-up or tarping. In Comanche's climate, an open roof after a fire can mean rain intrusion within days — a secondary water loss on top of the fire damage.

**Scope documentation** follows. We photograph and document every affected surface, material, and content item — the kind of thorough record that supports an insurance claim and prevents disputes about what was damaged versus what was pre-existing.

**Dry soot removal** is performed before any wet cleaning. Dry chemical sponges and HEPA-filtered vacuums pull loose soot from walls, ceilings, and contents without smearing it deeper into the substrate. On the older plaster and wood surfaces common in Comanche homes, this step is especially important — wet cleaning over unsealed soot on porous plaster can drive the stain in permanently.

**Odor neutralization** uses thermal fogging and hydroxyl generation to reach the same spaces smoke reached — inside wall cavities, inside ductwork, inside cabinet interiors. Surface-only deodorizing leaves odor behind in those hidden spaces, and it returns when temperatures rise.

**Structural drying and reconstruction** close out the process. If firefighting water is involved — and it almost always is — structural drying runs concurrently with smoke remediation using calibrated drying equipment monitored against daily psychrometric readings.
