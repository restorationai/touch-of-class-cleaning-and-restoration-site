Clyde sits in Callahan County where the West Texas wind scours the land flat and the sun bakes everything in between — but inside the walls of the town's older homes and commercial buildings, conditions are anything but simple. Many structures built before the mid-1980s contain asbestos-bearing materials that were standard practice at the time: floor tile adhesive, pipe insulation, textured ceilings, and roofing felts. When renovation, storm damage, or simple aging disturbs those materials, the fibers that release are invisible and odorless — which is exactly why professional asbestos abatement in Clyde is not a DIY conversation.

## Why Clyde Properties Are Prone to Asbestos-Containing Materials

Callahan County's building boom tracked closely with the broader Texas expansion of the mid-twentieth century. Ranch-style homes, agricultural outbuildings, and the small commercial blocks that line the older corridors of Clyde were frequently built with materials sourced from regional suppliers who relied heavily on asbestos for its heat resistance and durability — qualities that made obvious sense in a climate where summer temperatures regularly exceed 100°F.

The flat topography and expansive soil conditions common across this part of West Texas also mean that slab foundations shift seasonally. That movement can crack drywall compound, disturb floor tile, and fracture pipe insulation — all of which are potential asbestos sources in pre-1985 construction. A hairline crack in a ceiling that looks cosmetic may be breaking the surface of a texture coat that tested positive for chrysotile asbestos.

Popcorn ceiling asbestos removal is among the most common requests in homes of this era. The sprayed-on acoustic texture applied through the 1970s frequently contained asbestos as a binder, and it is friable — meaning it crumbles easily and releases fibers when disturbed by scraping, sanding, or even a ceiling fan vibration over time.

## Our Asbestos Abatement Process in Clyde

Every project begins with an asbestos inspection before any material is touched. Suspect samples are collected by a trained technician and submitted to an accredited laboratory. Testing results determine whether abatement is required, what containment level is appropriate, and which disposal pathway applies under Texas Commission on Environmental Quality (TCEQ) regulations.

Once abatement is confirmed, the work area is isolated with negative-air containment — plastic sheeting sealed at all penetrations, HEPA-filtered air scrubbers running continuously, and decontamination chambers at the entry point. Workers wear full personal protective equipment throughout. Asbestos-containing materials are wetted to suppress fiber release, carefully removed, double-bagged in labeled poly, and transported to a licensed disposal facility. Air Care Restoration's team is OSHA trained and EPA Lead-Safe Certified, and the process follows the standards that govern licensed abatement work in Texas.

After removal, air clearance sampling is conducted to confirm fiber levels are within acceptable limits before containment is dismantled. You receive documentation of the lab results — something your insurance carrier and any future buyer of the property will want on file.

## Clyde Insurance Coordination

Asbestos abatement is sometimes a covered line item when the exposure is tied to a covered loss — a roof breach that damages insulated ductwork, for example, or a plumbing failure that requires tearing into an older floor. Whether coverage applies depends on the specific policy language and the adjuster's determination, but thorough documentation helps. Air Care Restoration photographs affected materials before, during, and after abatement, and the lab chain-of-custody records provide the paper trail that adjusters and carriers need to evaluate a claim. Bring those documents to your first conversation with your agent.

## Local Note

One pattern that comes up repeatedly in Clyde-area homes is asbestos-containing mastic beneath vinyl floor tiles in kitchens and utility rooms. The 9×9-inch floor tile common in Texas homes built between the 1950s and early 1970s is a known asbestos source, but the black adhesive underneath it — the mastic — often tests positive even when the tile itself does not. Homeowners planning a flooring update sometimes pull a corner tile themselves to see what's underneath, which is exactly the moment that mastic can be disturbed. If you find dark, tar-like adhesive under old resilient tile anywhere in Clyde, stop the project and schedule testing before proceeding.
