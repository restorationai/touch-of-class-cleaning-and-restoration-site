West Texas storms don't announce themselves politely. San Angelo sits in a region where supercell thunderstorms can drop baseball-sized hail, spin up brief but violent tornadoes, and push straight-line winds that strip roofing in seconds — sometimes all in the same afternoon. When that kind of weather moves through Tom Green County, the damage it leaves behind ranges from shattered windows and torn fascia to structural compromise that isn't visible until water has already been working inside your walls for hours.

## Why San Angelo Properties See Significant Storm Damage

The Concho Valley's climate creates a specific combination of hazards that sets it apart from the Texas Hill Country to the southeast or the Permian Basin to the west. San Angelo averages more than 60 thunderstorm days per year, and the flat, open terrain west of town gives storms little to slow them before they reach residential neighborhoods. Hail is the most frequent culprit — it punches through asphalt shingles, cracks clay tile, and dents metal roofing in ways that aren't obvious from the ground but allow water infiltration with every subsequent rain.

The local housing stock adds another layer of complexity. A substantial portion of San Angelo's older neighborhoods feature homes built with masonry veneer over wood-frame construction — a style common across this part of Texas from the mid-20th century. That brick exterior can mask water intrusion at the mortar joints for weeks, allowing moisture to saturate wall cavities and insulation long before a homeowner notices a stain on the interior drywall. Caliche-heavy soil throughout the county also drains poorly after heavy rain, which means standing water around foundations is a recurring problem following any storm that drops more than an inch in a short window.

## Our Storm Damage Restoration Process in San Angelo

The first priority after any storm event is a thorough damage assessment — not a visual walk-around, but a systematic inspection that includes moisture mapping of walls and ceilings, documentation of structural penetrations, and photography sufficient to support an insurance claim. Air Care Restoration's IICRC-certified team uses thermal imaging and calibrated moisture meters to locate water that has migrated behind finished surfaces, because what you can see after a storm is rarely the whole picture.

Once the scope is clear, work moves in a logical sequence: emergency tarping and board-up to stop ongoing exposure, controlled demolition of materials that cannot be dried in place, structural drying with commercial-grade desiccant and refrigerant dehumidifiers, and finally reconstruction. For hail-damaged roofing, that means coordinating with the insurance adjuster's scope before any permanent repairs begin — a step that protects both the homeowner and the integrity of the claim. Because Air Care Restoration holds EPA Lead-Safe certification, work on older San Angelo homes where lead-based paint may be present follows proper containment and disposal protocols without requiring a separate contractor.

## San Angelo Insurance Coordination

Storm damage claims in Texas are governed by the Texas Department of Insurance's prompt-payment statutes, which set specific deadlines for acknowledgment and payment after a claim is filed. Knowing those timelines matters when you're deciding whether to authorize temporary repairs before an adjuster has inspected the property. In general, Texas law permits — and insurers expect — reasonable emergency mitigation (tarping, board-up, water extraction) to begin immediately; delaying that work to wait for an adjuster can actually complicate a claim if secondary damage results.

Air Care Restoration documents every step of the mitigation and restoration process with photographs, moisture readings, and written scope notes formatted to align with the line-item structure that most carriers use. That documentation package is provided to the homeowner and their adjuster at each phase of the job.

One pattern that catches San Angelo homeowners off guard: the city's mature pecan and live oak trees — common throughout established neighborhoods near the Concho River and along older residential streets — have root systems and canopy spreads that make them beautiful in calm weather and genuinely hazardous in high-wind events. When a large limb or whole tree comes down on a roof, the impact damage is obvious, but the moisture pathway it creates is not. Getting a moisture assessment done at the same time as the tree removal — not after — prevents that secondary damage from going undetected.
