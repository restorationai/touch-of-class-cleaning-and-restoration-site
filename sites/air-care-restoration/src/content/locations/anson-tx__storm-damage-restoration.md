West Texas storms don't announce themselves politely. In Anson and the surrounding Jones County flatlands, severe weather can arrive as a wall of wind-driven hail one afternoon and a slow-grinding thunderstorm the next morning — and the wide-open terrain means there's nothing to slow either one down before it reaches your roof. ## Why Anson Properties Are Vulnerable to Storm Damage

Jones County sits squarely in the southern corridor of Tornado Alley, and Anson's position on the Rolling Plains means the landscape offers almost no natural windbreak. Hailstorms that track northeast off the Caprock can arrive with little radar warning, and the same systems that produce baseball-sized hail over Abilene often still carry golf-ball-sized stones by the time they reach Anson — enough to punch through asphalt shingles, crack ridge caps, and drive water into attic insulation before a homeowner realizes the roof is compromised.

The local housing stock adds another layer of complexity. A significant portion of Anson's residential properties were built during the mid-twentieth century, when construction standards relied on solid wood framing and plaster-and-lath interior walls rather than modern OSB sheathing and drywall. That older construction holds up well in many ways, but it also means water that gets past a damaged roof can travel farther inside the wall cavity before it shows up as a stain on the ceiling — and it can take longer to fully dry because plaster releases absorbed moisture more slowly than modern materials.

Flat and gently sloping lots throughout the area drain slowly after heavy rain events. When a storm drops two or three inches of rain in under an hour — which Jones County sees more than once in a typical spring season — surface water can pool against foundation walls and find its way into crawl spaces and lower levels long after the storm has moved east.

## Our Storm Damage Restoration Process in Anson

Every storm loss is different, but the sequence that protects your property follows a consistent logic. When we arrive, the first priority is emergency stabilization: tarping breached rooflines, boarding damaged windows, and stopping active water intrusion before it migrates further into the structure. We document everything photographically before any material is moved — that documentation is what your insurance adjuster will rely on to scope the claim.

Once the structure is stabilized, we assess moisture penetration using thermal imaging cameras and calibrated moisture meters. In older Anson homes with plaster walls, this step matters more than it might in newer construction — the surface can read dry while the lath behind it is still saturated. We set drying equipment based on actual readings, not visual inspection alone, and we monitor daily until the structure reaches pre-loss moisture levels.

Tree impact is a common secondary issue after severe storms in this part of Texas, where mature elms and pecans grow close to homes. When a limb or trunk comes through a roof or wall, the structural assessment has to happen before full restoration can begin. We coordinate with structural contractors when load-bearing elements are involved and can help sequence the work so your home is enclosed and weather-tight as quickly as possible.

## Anson Insurance Coordination

Storm damage claims in Texas are governed by state prompt-payment statutes, and most standard homeowner policies cover sudden wind, hail, and water intrusion caused by storm damage — though the specifics of your deductible, coverage limits, and any exclusions are between you and your carrier. What we do is make the documentation process as clean as possible: itemized moisture logs, timestamped photographs, and a written scope of work that gives your adjuster what they need to process the claim without unnecessary back-and-forth. We work with most major carriers and can communicate directly with your adjuster throughout the job.

## Local Note

In Jones County, the combination of clay-heavy soils and flat topography means that standing water after a storm doesn't always drain the direction you'd expect. Water that pools on the west side of a structure can slowly migrate toward a foundation over the course of several hours as the ground reaches saturation — which is why a storm that seemed to spare your interior can still produce a wet crawl space or basement seepage two days later. If you had a significant rain event and haven't checked below-grade spaces, it's worth doing before that moisture has time to create secondary problems.
