## Why Albany Properties Face Particular Fire Risks

Shackelford County's climate is defined by low humidity, persistent southwest winds, and seasonal drought — conditions that accelerate how quickly a fire spreads and how thoroughly smoke penetrates a structure. Many homes in Albany were built in the mid-twentieth century, when balloon-frame construction was common. In that framing style, wall cavities run uninterrupted from the foundation sill to the roof ridge, acting like a chimney flue during a fire. Smoke travels vertically through those cavities and deposits soot in attic spaces and wall bays that look untouched from the outside. A visual inspection alone will miss it.

Albany's position along US-180 also means that agricultural and ranch structures — barns, equipment sheds, hay storage — sit close to residential property lines. A working fire in an outbuilding can expose an adjacent home to radiant heat and airborne embers long after the structure fire itself is controlled. That exposure pattern tends to produce heavy exterior char on siding and window frames alongside interior smoke infiltration through HVAC intakes, which requires a different sequencing of work than a contained kitchen fire would.

## Our Fire Damage Restoration Process in Albany

The first step on any Albany fire loss is a structured assessment that separates visible char from hidden smoke migration. Using thermal imaging, the crew maps where combustion byproducts have traveled — including inside ductwork, which in West Texas homes often runs through unconditioned attic space and can hold soot for months if not cleaned. That mapping drives the scope of work before a single surface is touched, so nothing is missed and nothing is over-demolished.

From there, the process moves in a deliberate sequence:

- **Emergency stabilization** — boarding windows and doors, tarping roof penetrations, and securing the structure against weather. Albany's afternoon wind gusts can drive rain or dust into an open structure within hours of a fire.
- **Controlled demolition** — removing only materials that cannot be restored. Char-damaged framing, melted insulation, and smoke-saturated drywall come out; structurally sound materials that can be cleaned stay in place to reduce rebuild cost.
- **Dry-ice blasting and HEPA vacuuming** — removing soot from framing, masonry, and HVAC cavities without introducing moisture into an already stressed structure.
- **Deodorization** — thermal fogging and hydroxyl treatment to neutralize smoke odor at the molecular level, not just mask it. In a tight West Texas home with minimal air exchange, odor left untreated will return every time temperatures rise. Air Care's IICRC FSRT and OCT certifications drive this phase of the work.
- **Structural drying and reconstruction coordination** — verifying moisture levels before any new drywall or insulation is installed, then completing the rebuild to current code.

## Local Note

Albany's older residential blocks include homes built with native limestone and caliche block — materials common to this part of West Texas that behave differently from standard wood-frame construction in a fire. Caliche and limestone do not char the way wood does, but they absorb smoke oils into their porous surfaces, and conventional sealers applied over uncleaned masonry will trap odor rather than eliminate it. Proper restoration on these walls requires alkaline cleaning agents formulated for mineral substrates before any sealer or paint is applied. It is a step that gets skipped when a crew is unfamiliar with the regional building materials, and the result is a home that smells like smoke again the first warm spring day.
