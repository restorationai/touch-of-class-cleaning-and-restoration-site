## Why Coleman Properties See Water Cleanup Issues

Coleman's housing stock leans heavily toward mid-century construction — pier-and-beam foundations, wood-framed walls, and older plumbing systems that were never designed for the pressure spikes that come with aging municipal infrastructure. Galvanized supply lines common in homes built before the 1970s are prone to pinhole leaks and sudden failures, often inside wall cavities where the water spreads silently for days before a stain appears on the ceiling or a floor starts to feel soft underfoot.

The clay-heavy soils common across Coleman County expand when wet and contract during dry stretches, which puts steady lateral pressure on slab foundations and crawl space walls. A heavy rain event — the kind that drops two or three inches in an hour, which does happen on the Rolling Plains — can push groundwater up through foundation cracks or overwhelm older drainage lines that were sized for a drier era. That combination of soil behavior and aging construction is why water intrusion in Coleman often involves more than one entry point.

## Our Water Cleanup Process in Coleman

When a crew arrives at a Coleman property, the first step is always moisture mapping — not a visual inspection, but a systematic reading with thermal imaging and calibrated moisture meters across floors, walls, and ceilings. Water hides. It travels along subfloor joists, wicks up drywall paper, and collects in insulation batts long before it shows on the surface. Knowing exactly where the moisture boundary sits determines how much material needs to come out and where drying equipment gets positioned.

Once the scope is documented, standing water is extracted with truck-mounted or portable extraction units, depending on access. Wet materials that cannot be dried in place — saturated insulation, buckled hardwood, soaked drywall below the flood line — are removed to expose the structural framing for drying. Industrial air movers and refrigerant or desiccant dehumidifiers then run in a closed drying system, with daily moisture readings to track progress against the IICRC S500 drying standard. The job isn't finished when the surface feels dry; it's finished when the readings confirm structural moisture is back within normal range.
