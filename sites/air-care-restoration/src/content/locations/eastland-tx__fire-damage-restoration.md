## Why Eastland Properties Are Vulnerable to Structural Fire Damage

Much of Eastland's residential housing stock reflects the building practices of the mid-twentieth century — pier-and-beam foundations, tongue-and-groove sheathing, and balloon-frame construction in the older sections of town. Balloon framing, where wall studs run continuously from the sill plate to the roof line without a fire stop between floors, allows smoke and heat to travel vertically inside wall cavities almost unimpeded. By the time a fire is extinguished, smoke residue can be present two or three stories above the room where the fire actually burned. That residue — a mix of dry smoke, wet smoke, and protein smoke depending on what burned — behaves differently on wood, drywall, plaster, and insulation, and each surface requires a different cleaning chemistry. Getting the assessment wrong means odor returns weeks after the job is "done."

Eastland's climate adds another layer. The region averages well under 30 inches of rainfall annually, and relative humidity regularly drops into the teens during summer afternoons. Low humidity accelerates the off-gassing of smoke particles into porous materials, which can actually make post-fire odor worse in the days following a loss if the structure isn't treated promptly. It also means that the firefighting water — often thousands of gallons — creates a secondary moisture problem in an environment where the building envelope wasn't designed to manage that kind of sudden saturation.

## Our Fire Damage Restoration Process in Eastland

Every fire loss is different, but the sequence of work follows a disciplined order that prevents secondary damage from compounding the original loss.

**Emergency stabilization** comes first. That means boarding openings, tarping the roof if the fire breached it, and establishing a controlled environment so that smoke odor doesn't continue migrating into unaffected areas. In Eastland's older homes, this step often includes checking whether the attic space has been compromised — balloon-frame walls frequently vent directly into attic cavities, and smoke deposits there are easy to miss on an initial walkthrough.

**Scope and documentation** follows. We photograph and catalog every affected surface, material, and content item before anything is moved or cleaned. This documentation is the foundation of your insurance claim and protects you if there are disputes about what was present before the loss.

**Structural cleaning and deodorization** is where the IICRC FSRT training matters most. Dry smoke from fast-burning fires leaves a powdery, alkaline residue that smears if you wipe it with the wrong product. Wet smoke from slow, smoldering fires leaves a sticky, acidic residue that etches surfaces. Protein smoke from kitchen fires is nearly invisible but produces some of the most persistent odors. We identify which smoke types are present and apply the appropriate cleaning agents — not a one-size approach.

**Thermal fogging and hydroxyl treatment** address odor at the molecular level, reaching into wall cavities and HVAC ductwork that surface cleaning can't access. In Eastland's dry climate, this step moves quickly, but we verify odor neutralization before closing out the job.

**Reconstruction coordination** closes the loop. Where structural materials need replacement — charred framing, damaged drywall, compromised insulation — we manage that scope so you're not coordinating a separate contractor on top of an already stressful situation.

## Local Note

One thing worth knowing about fire restoration work in older Eastland-area homes: the original plaster-and-lath interior walls that appear in houses built before the 1960s absorb smoke odor compounds differently than modern drywall. Plaster is denser, so surface contamination is often lighter — but the lath behind it, and the wall cavity insulation (frequently cellulose or old fiberglass batts), can hold odor long after the plaster face reads clean. A thorough job here requires cavity inspection and, in some cases, targeted lath-and-plaster removal to access the insulation layer. Skipping that step is one of the most common reasons a homeowner calls a second company six weeks after the first one finished.
