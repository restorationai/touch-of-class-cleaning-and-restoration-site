When a supercell rolls through Coleman County, it can drop baseball-sized hail, drive wind-blown rain sideways through window frames, and leave downed cedar elms on rooftops before the sirens stop echoing. ## Why Coleman Properties See Serious Storm Damage

The geography here works against homeowners in a specific way. Coleman County's flat terrain means there's no ridgeline, treeline, or urban density to break wind shear before it reaches a neighborhood. Thunderstorms that form along the Caprock escarpment to the northwest can accelerate across open rangeland and arrive in Coleman with little warning. Hail is a consistent threat from late spring through early fall, and the same systems that produce hail often trail damaging straight-line winds or brief tornadoes.

The local housing stock adds another layer of complexity. A significant portion of Coleman's homes were built in the mid-20th century, when roof decking was often 1×6 skip sheathing rather than modern plywood or OSB. That construction style is charming and durable in dry conditions, but it offers fewer nailing surfaces for shingles and can allow wind-driven rain to infiltrate along the gaps when a storm strips even a few shingles. Once water gets behind the fascia or into the attic cavity, it can track a surprising distance from the visible entry point before showing up as a stain on the ceiling below.

## Our Storm Damage Restoration Process in Coleman

The first priority after any storm event is stopping ongoing exposure. Before any drying or reconstruction begins, the crew performs a full exterior assessment — roof, siding, soffit, window seals — and installs temporary weatherproofing where the building envelope has been breached. Tarping a damaged roof section correctly means more than throwing plastic over the hole; it means securing edges against West Texas wind gusts that can peel a poorly anchored tarp back within hours.

Once the structure is stabilized, the interior assessment begins. Moisture mapping with thermal imaging and calibrated meters identifies where water has migrated behind walls or under flooring, even when surfaces feel dry to the touch. In older Coleman homes with plaster-and-lath interior walls, water can wick laterally through the lath before it shows on the plaster face — thermal imaging catches that movement early and prevents a small wet patch from becoming a mold-growth scenario days later.

Drying is managed to IICRC S500 standards, with commercial dehumidifiers and air movers placed based on the moisture map rather than by guesswork. Daily moisture readings track progress and guide equipment adjustments. Reconstruction — from roof decking and shingles to interior drywall, insulation, and paint — follows once the structure reaches documented dry standard.

Storm damage claims in Texas are governed by the Texas Department of Insurance's prompt-payment statutes, which set specific timelines for carrier acknowledgment and settlement. Documenting the loss thoroughly and promptly — photographs of every affected surface, moisture readings, a detailed scope of damage — puts a claim in the strongest possible position before an adjuster visits. ## Local Note

One pattern worth knowing in Coleman: the area's caliche-heavy soil drains slowly after a heavy rain, and properties with shallow foundations or older perimeter drainage can see water pool against the foundation long after the storm has passed. That standing water doesn't always make it into the crawl space immediately — it can seep in over 24 to 48 hours, meaning a homeowner who checks the crawl space the morning after a storm and finds it dry may find it wet two days later. If your property has a pier-and-beam foundation and you've had significant rainfall, a follow-up inspection 48 hours out is worth scheduling before assuming the structure is clear.
