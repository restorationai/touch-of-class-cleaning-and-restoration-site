After a fire — whether started by a lightning strike, an electrical fault in an older farmhouse, or a kitchen accident — the same open-sky exposure that makes summer sunsets dramatic makes an unsecured structure dangerous. That makes the first hour of emergency response — getting a board or tarp in place — disproportionately important for limiting secondary damage.

## Local Note

One pattern worth knowing in Haskell: older homes in town frequently have wood-framed roof structures with skip sheathing — spaced boards rather than continuous plywood decking — left over from original construction or partial re-roofing jobs. When a tarp is laid over skip sheathing, the gaps between boards create pressure points that can cause the tarp to tear under wind load if it's only surface-weighted. Our crew accounts for this by running fastener battens across the full tarp field rather than just the perimeter, distributing load across the underlying structure. It's a small difference in method that significantly extends how long the tarp holds before permanent repairs can be scheduled.
