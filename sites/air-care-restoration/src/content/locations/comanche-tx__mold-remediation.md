Comanche sits in the Cross Timbers region of Central Texas, where warm, humid air pushes in from the south every spring and summer and collides with the dry heat that bakes the area through July and August. That swing between moisture and drought is harder on older homes than most homeowners realize — and Comanche has a lot of older homes. When a slow roof leak, a sweating AC line, or a plumbing drip goes unnoticed for even a few days in this climate, mold can begin colonizing wall cavities and subfloor framing before the surface ever looks or smells wrong. ## Why Comanche Properties See Mold Issues

The soil across Comanche County holds moisture unevenly — caliche layers near the surface can shed water laterally toward a foundation rather than letting it drain straight down. Pier-and-beam foundations, common in homes built before the 1970s throughout the city, create a crawl space environment that traps humidity against the underside of the floor system. When outdoor humidity climbs in May and June and the ground stays damp from spring rains, that crawl space can read 80% relative humidity or higher even when the living area above feels comfortable. Mold doesn't need a visible leak to get started — sustained high humidity in an enclosed space is enough.

Comanche also has a significant share of homes with original wood siding, single-pane windows, and older HVAC systems that weren't designed for today's tighter insulation standards. Retrofitting insulation into those walls without addressing vapor management is a known driver of hidden moisture accumulation. If your home was built before 1980 and has been updated in pieces over the years, the intersection of old framing and new insulation is worth paying attention to.

## Our Mold Remediation Process in Comanche

Every remediation is performed in accordance with a protocol written by an independent licensed Mold Assessment Consultant — not just addressing the room where you noticed a smell or a stain, but the adjacent cavities, the attic above, and the crawl space or slab edge below. Mold follows moisture, and moisture rarely stays where it started.

Once the affected area is mapped, the crew establishes containment using negative air pressure and poly barriers to prevent spores from spreading to unaffected rooms during removal. Contaminated materials — drywall, insulation, framing sections — are removed, bagged, and disposed of properly. Remaining structural surfaces are treated with an EPA-registered antimicrobial agent. Air scrubbers with HEPA filtration run continuously throughout the project.

Because Comanche homes often have original wood framing rather than engineered lumber, the crew pays close attention to moisture readings in the structural members themselves. Wood framing that reads elevated on a moisture meter needs to be dried to an acceptable baseline before encapsulation or reconstruction begins — sealing over wet wood is a short-term fix that creates a long-term problem. Post-remediation verification is performed by the independent licensed Mold Assessment Consultant to confirm the project meets the criteria established in the Mold Remediation Protocol.

## Local Note

One pattern that comes up repeatedly in Comanche-area homes is the combination of a metal roof retrofit over original wood sheathing. Metal roofs shed water efficiently, but if the original sheathing underneath wasn't dried out before the metal panels went on — or if the ridge and eave ventilation wasn't reconfigured for the new roof profile — condensation can build up on the underside of the metal and drip onto the sheathing below for years without anyone noticing. Attic mold in these homes often doesn't show up until a homeowner goes up to add insulation or a HVAC technician is servicing a unit. If your home has a metal roof and you haven't had the attic looked at recently, it's worth a look.
