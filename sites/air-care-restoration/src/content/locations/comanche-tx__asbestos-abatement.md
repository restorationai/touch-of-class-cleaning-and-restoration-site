That building era coincides almost exactly with peak asbestos use in residential and light commercial construction, meaning attic insulation, floor tile adhesive, pipe wrap, and textured ceiling finishes in many Comanche homes may still contain the mineral. ## Why Comanche Properties Face Elevated Asbestos Risk

Comanche County's older neighborhoods carry the architectural fingerprints of a construction era when asbestos was considered a premium material. Popcorn ceilings applied through the 1970s routinely contained chrysotile asbestos. Vinyl floor tiles from the same period were bonded with asbestos-bearing mastic. Duct insulation and pipe wrap in homes built before federal regulations tightened in the late 1970s can contain friable asbestos that becomes airborne when disturbed.

The region's climate adds a layer of complexity. West-central Texas experiences wide temperature swings between seasons, and that thermal cycling causes building materials to expand and contract repeatedly over decades. In older construction, this mechanical stress can cause asbestos-containing materials to crack or crumble, shifting them from a stable, non-friable state to a more hazardous, friable condition. A material that looked intact during a home purchase a decade ago may have degraded since.

Comanche also sees periodic severe weather, including hail and high-wind events that can breach roofing and siding. When storm damage opens up wall cavities or disturbs insulation in a pre-1980 structure, the risk of asbestos fiber release rises sharply. Restoration work should not begin on an older damaged structure until sampling confirms whether asbestos is present.

## Our Asbestos Abatement Process in Comanche

Every project begins with a systematic inspection and bulk sampling of suspect materials. Samples are submitted to an accredited laboratory, and no abatement work begins until results are in hand. This step protects you from unnecessary remediation costs and ensures that any work performed is targeted at confirmed asbestos-containing materials.

Once a positive identification is made, the affected area is isolated with negative air pressure containment using HEPA-filtered air scrubbers. This prevents cross-contamination to adjacent rooms or HVAC systems during removal. All materials are wetted before disturbance to suppress fiber release, then carefully removed, double-bagged in labeled poly sheeting, and transported to a licensed disposal facility in compliance with Texas Commission on Environmental Quality requirements.

Air clearance testing follows removal to confirm fiber levels have returned to acceptable thresholds before containment is dismantled. That licensure framework, while primarily structured around mold, reflects the regulatory discipline that carries through all hazardous material work the team performs.

## Local Note

One thing worth knowing about older homes in Comanche: many were built with a combination of materials that were standard in rural Texas during the postwar boom, including asbestos-containing transite board used for exterior siding and some interior applications. Transite is a cement-asbestos composite that can look like ordinary fiber cement to an untrained eye. It is typically non-friable when intact, but cutting, sanding, or drilling it during a remodel releases fibers. If your home has exterior siding that looks like thin, rigid panels with a slightly textured surface and was built before 1980, have it tested before any exterior work begins. This material shows up in Comanche-area properties more often than homeowners expect, and it is frequently misidentified at the hardware store as something safe to cut with a circular saw.
