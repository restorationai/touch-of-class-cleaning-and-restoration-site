Costs vary by project size and scope, but getting it right protects the schedule, satisfies inspectors, and protects the finished surfaces your client just paid for.

You just wrapped framing, drywall, and paint on a commercial build near the Abilene Christian University corridor, or maybe a kitchen remodel out in Potosi. Either way, the GC is asking when the space will be ready for walkthrough, and there's still drywall dust on every flat surface, construction adhesive on the tile, and a pile of scrap lumber by the back door. That's the moment post-construction cleaning becomes a critical path item, not an afterthought.

## What Does Construction Site Cleanup Actually Include?

Post-construction cleanup is a three-phase process: rough clean, final clean, and touch-up detail. Each phase happens at a different point in the construction timeline, and skipping one creates problems for the next.

**Rough clean** happens while trades are still active or just wrapping up. The crew removes bulk debris: scrap lumber, drywall cutoffs, packaging, wire scraps, and anything that would be a trip hazard or delay the next trade. This is not a detail pass. It's about clearing the deck.

**Final clean** happens after all trades are done and before the owner walkthrough. This is where the real work is. A proper final clean on a commercial or residential build includes:

- Vacuuming and wiping all surfaces for drywall dust, which settles everywhere, including inside HVAC registers
- Cleaning windows inside and out, including removing paint overspray and adhesive labels from glass
- Wiping down all cabinetry, countertops, and hardware
- Cleaning tile grout haze from floors and walls
- Removing construction stickers and protective film from appliances, fixtures, and flooring
- Cleaning restrooms and kitchens to a sanitary standard
- Sweeping, mopping, or vacuuming all floor surfaces based on material type

**Touch-up detail** is a final pass, often 24-48 hours before occupancy or certificate of occupancy inspection. It catches what the final clean missed after the last few trades made their punch-list visits.

## Why Is Drywall Dust the Biggest Problem on Any Build?

Drywall dust is the defining challenge of post-construction cleaning, and most property owners underestimate it. Gypsum dust is fine enough to pass through standard HVAC filters and coat the interior of ductwork, fan motors, and coil surfaces. If it gets into the HVAC system before the building is occupied, it circulates through the air for months.

On a new build or major renovation in Abilene's dry West Texas climate, dust accumulation is worse than in more humid regions because there's nothing to weigh it down. A proper final clean uses HEPA-filtered vacuums before any wet mopping, because mopping drywall dust without vacuuming first just smears it into a paste on hard floors.

If the HVAC system ran during construction without temporary filters over the returns, the ductwork itself may need cleaning before occupancy. [The EPA recommends](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq) that indoor air quality be addressed during and after renovation, not just at the end. On larger commercial projects, an air duct cleaning pass after construction is standard practice, not optional.

## What Hazardous Materials Can Come Up During Construction Cleanup?

On renovation projects, especially in buildings constructed before 1980, construction cleanup can uncover materials that require more than a broom and a dumpster.

**Asbestos** is the most common. Popcorn ceilings, floor tile mastic, pipe insulation, and some drywall joint compounds used before the mid-1980s can contain asbestos fibers. If a renovation disturbs these materials, cleanup is not a standard post-construction task. Disturbed asbestos requires licensed abatement under [Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation (TDLR)](https://www.tdlr.texas.gov) rules, and the work must be performed by a licensed contractor before general cleanup proceeds.

**Lead paint** is a similar issue. Sanding or cutting painted surfaces in pre-1978 construction generates lead dust. EPA Lead-Safe certification is required for renovation work in pre-1978 residential buildings, and cleanup must follow specific containment and disposal protocols under the EPA's Renovation, Repair and Painting (RRP) rule.

**Mold** sometimes surfaces during renovation when walls are opened. If a crew finds active mold growth during demolition, that area needs to be assessed and remediated before cleanup and reconstruction continue. Continuing to work around active mold spreads spores through the building.

If any of these materials are a concern on your project, address them before the post-construction clean begins, not during it.

## How Long Does Post-Construction Cleanup Take?

Timeline depends on project size, the number of trades involved, and how much protective covering was used during construction. A rough rule of thumb for commercial projects in the Big Country:

- **Small tenant improvement or single-room renovation (under 1,000 sq ft):** 1-2 days for final clean
- **Mid-size commercial build or full residential renovation (1,000-5,000 sq ft):** 3-5 days
- **Large commercial or multi-unit project (5,000+ sq ft):** 1-2 weeks, often phased by floor or zone

These timelines assume the rough clean happened during construction. If the site was never rough-cleaned and debris has been accumulating for months, add time.

Schedule the final clean after the last trade leaves and before the punch-list walkthrough. If the HVAC system needs to run for testing, coordinate so the ductwork is cleaned or filtered before air circulates through the finished space.

## What Should You Look for in a Construction Cleanup Contractor?

Not every cleaning company is equipped for post-construction work. Here's what separates a capable contractor from one who will leave drywall dust in the corners and grout haze on the tile.

**HEPA-rated equipment.** Standard shop vacs and consumer vacuums recirculate fine dust. A post-construction crew should use commercial HEPA-filtered vacuums rated for fine particulate.

**Experience with hazardous material protocols.** If your project involves any pre-1980 construction, the contractor should know the difference between a standard cleanup and one that requires licensed abatement. They should not disturb suspect materials.

**Licensed and insured.** Verify general liability and workers' compensation coverage before anyone steps on the job site. On a commercial project, your contract likely requires it.

**Written scope of work.** A professional contractor provides a written scope before work begins, not a verbal estimate. The scope should define what is included in each phase, what materials are excluded, and what the acceptance criteria are.

**Coordination with the GC schedule.** The best post-construction cleaners understand construction sequencing. They know not to do the final detail pass before the painter's punch-list visit, and they can mobilize quickly when the schedule compresses.

## What Happens If You Skip Professional Post-Construction Cleanup?

Skipping or cutting corners on post-construction cleanup creates problems that show up later, usually at the worst possible time.

Drywall dust left in HVAC ductwork shortens equipment life and creates indoor air quality complaints from the first occupants. Grout haze left on tile permanently etches the surface if it cures fully. Adhesive residue on windows and hardware is much harder to remove after it has been exposed to West Texas summer heat for a few weeks. Construction debris left in wall cavities or under flooring creates pest harborage.

On commercial projects, a failed certificate of occupancy inspection because the building isn't clean enough delays occupancy and creates real financial exposure for the GC. On residential projects, a homeowner who moves in and finds drywall dust on every surface is a homeowner who calls back with complaints.

A professional post-construction clean is a small line item relative to the total project cost. It protects the finished work, protects the schedule, and protects the relationship with the owner.
