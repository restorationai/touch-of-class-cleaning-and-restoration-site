Whether your homeowners insurance covers water damage depends almost entirely on *how* the water got in. The short answer: sudden, accidental water damage is usually covered. Slow leaks, flooding from outside, and neglected maintenance usually are not. That distinction — sudden versus gradual, internal versus external — is the line adjusters draw on nearly every water claim. Understanding it before you file (or before you sign a remediation contract) can save you thousands of dollars and a lot of frustration.

## The Coverage Line That Actually Matters

Standard homeowners policies (HO-3 is the most common form in Texas) cover what the industry calls "sudden and accidental" discharge or overflow. Think of a washing machine supply hose that ruptures on a Tuesday afternoon, or a pipe that freezes and bursts during a West Texas cold snap. The damage was unexpected, it happened fast, and you had no reason to know it was coming. That's the profile insurers are willing to pay for.

What they are not willing to pay for is damage that built up over time — a slow drip under the kitchen sink that rotted the cabinet floor over six months, a toilet seal that has been weeping for a year, a roof flashing that has leaked every rainstorm for two seasons. Adjusters call this "long-term seepage" or "gradual damage," and most standard policies exclude it explicitly. The reasoning is that ongoing maintenance is the homeowner's responsibility.

A few specific exclusions worth knowing:

- **Flood damage** — water that enters from outside (storm surge, overflowing rivers, heavy rain sheeting across the ground) is never covered by a standard homeowners policy. That requires a separate flood policy, typically through the National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) or a private carrier.
- **Sewer and drain backup** — water that reverses up through floor drains or toilets is usually excluded unless you've added a sewer backup rider.
- **Groundwater and seepage** — water that seeps through a foundation or basement wall due to soil saturation is treated similarly to flood damage.
- **Neglected maintenance** — if an adjuster finds evidence that a leak was visible and ignored, the claim is likely to be denied on the grounds that the damage was preventable.

## What a Covered Claim Actually Looks Like

Coverage isn't just a yes/no — it also defines *what* gets paid. A typical covered water damage claim will include:

1. **The cost to repair or replace damaged structure** — drywall, flooring, subfloor, cabinetry, insulation.
2. **The cost of drying and remediation** — professional water extraction, drying equipment, and mold prevention treatment. This is where a restoration contractor's documentation matters enormously.
3. **Contents coverage** — furniture, electronics, and personal property damaged by the water, up to your policy's personal property limit.
4. **Additional Living Expenses (ALE)** — if the damage makes your home uninhabitable, most policies cover reasonable hotel and meal costs while repairs are underway.

What coverage does *not* include is the source of the problem itself. If a supply line fails and floods your kitchen, the policy pays for the flooring and the drywall — not for replacing the supply line. That distinction catches a lot of homeowners off guard.

## How to Document the Damage Before You Touch Anything

The single biggest mistake homeowners make after a water loss is cleaning up before documenting. Adjusters work from evidence. The more you can show them, the stronger your claim.

If it's safe to do so, here's the order of operations:

1. **Stop the water source first.** Turn off the supply valve behind the fixture, or the main shutoff if you can't isolate it. In Abilene, the main shutoff is typically near the meter at the street or at the point where the line enters the house.
2. **Photograph everything before moving it.** Wide shots of the room, close-ups of the water line on walls, the damaged flooring, the failed fixture or pipe. Timestamp matters — your phone's metadata does this automatically.
3. **Write down what you noticed and when.** Adjusters ask whether you had any prior knowledge of the problem. A written timeline protects you.
4. **Call your insurer to open a claim.** Most carriers have 24-hour claim lines. Opening the claim doesn't obligate you to accept a settlement — it starts the clock on their response obligation.
5. **Begin mitigation, but document as you go.** You are required by your policy to take reasonable steps to prevent further damage (running fans, removing standing water). You are not required to wait for an adjuster before you start. Keep receipts for any equipment you rent or buy.

If water has been sitting for more than 24 to 48 hours, mold colonization is already a real risk. Wet drywall and wet wood framing don't show mold on the surface until it's well established behind them. A moisture meter tells a different story than your eyes will.

## What NOT to Do After a Water Loss

- **Don't throw away damaged materials before the adjuster sees them** — or at least before you photograph them thoroughly. Disposing of evidence can complicate your claim.
- **Don't sign a broad assignment-of-benefits agreement** without reading it carefully. Some contractors use AOB clauses to take over your claim entirely, which can create disputes between the contractor and your insurer that delay your repairs.
- **Don't assume the damage is dry because the surface feels dry.** Water migrates. It travels along joists, wicks into insulation, and pools in wall cavities. A surface that reads dry to the touch can have a moisture content of 30–40% an inch behind it.
- **Don't wait to call a professional if there's any standing water or visible saturation.** The longer wet materials sit, the more expensive the remediation — and the more likely an adjuster is to question whether mold damage was pre-existing.

## When the Claim Gets Complicated

Some claims are straightforward. Many are not. Here are the situations where you're most likely to hit friction:

**The adjuster disputes the cause.** If there's any ambiguity about whether damage was sudden or gradual, the insurer may send an independent inspector. You have the right to hire a public adjuster or have your contractor provide a written scope of loss that supports your position.

**The estimate is lower than the actual cost of repairs.** Insurance estimates are generated by software (Xactimate is the industry standard) and sometimes undervalue local labor costs or miss hidden damage. A detailed remediation report from your contractor — with moisture readings, affected square footage, and equipment logs — gives you grounds to negotiate.

**Mold is discovered during remediation.** If mold is found in the course of repairing a covered loss, most policies will cover mold remediation up to a sublimit (commonly $5,000–$10,000, though this varies by policy). Mold that predates the loss or resulted from long-term neglect is typically not covered.

**The policy has a high deductible.** Texas homeowners policies sometimes carry separate wind/hail deductibles, but water damage from internal sources is usually subject to your standard deductible. On smaller losses, it may not be worth filing — a claim on your record can affect your renewal premium.
