**TL;DR:** Commercial storm damage restoration in Abilene, TX typically begins with emergency stabilization (board-up, tarping, water extraction) within hours of the storm, followed by a documented damage assessment, insurance coordination, and full structural restoration. West Texas hail and wind events can close a business for days or weeks if the response is slow. The sequence matters: secure the building first, document everything second, then start the claim.

## What counts as commercial storm damage in Abilene?

Commercial storm damage in Abilene includes any structural or interior loss caused by hail, high wind, tornadoes, or the water intrusion that follows. The Big Country sits in one of the most active hail corridors in the United States, and Abilene averages roughly 50 severe thunderstorm events per year. A single storm can punch through metal roofing on a warehouse near the Abilene Regional Airport, blow out windows on a retail strip along South 14th, and dump enough rain into an exposed interior to soak insulation, drywall, and inventory before the rain even stops.

Commercial losses differ from residential ones in a few important ways. The square footage is larger, the contents are often higher-value, and the business interruption cost starts the moment the doors close. A restaurant that cannot open for lunch service is losing real money every hour. That urgency shapes how restoration crews prioritize commercial jobs.

## What should you do in the first hour after a storm hits your building?

In the first hour, focus on three things: life safety, access control, and documentation. Do not enter a structurally compromised building until a professional has confirmed it is safe. If the roof is partially collapsed or walls are shifted, call the Abilene Fire Department for a structural assessment before anyone goes inside.

Once it is safe to enter:

1. **Photograph everything before touching anything.** Walk the perimeter first, then the interior. Capture roof damage, broken windows, standing water, displaced inventory, and any equipment that took a direct hit. Timestamp your photos. Insurance adjusters and restoration scopes both rely on this documentation.
2. **Stop active water intrusion.** If rain is still entering through a breach, cover it with whatever is available. A tarp over a roof opening or plywood over a broken window limits secondary damage while you wait for a crew.
3. **Protect undamaged inventory and equipment.** Move stock away from wet areas. Unplug electronics near water. If you have a server room or sensitive equipment, prioritize it.
4. **Call your insurance carrier to open a claim.** You do not need a full damage estimate to open the claim. You need a claim number. Get it early.
5. **Call a commercial restoration company.** Emergency board-up and tarping, water extraction, and drying equipment need to be on-site the same day if possible. Wet commercial drywall that sits for 48 hours begins to support mold growth, and the IICRC S500 standard defines the drying window as 24-48 hours for Category 1 water before contamination risk escalates.

## How does the commercial storm damage restoration process work?

Commercial storm restoration follows a defined sequence. Skipping steps or doing them out of order creates documentation gaps that slow the insurance claim.

**Phase 1: Emergency stabilization (Day 1).** Crews install emergency board-up on broken windows and doors, apply roof tarps over breached sections, and begin water extraction if rain entered the building. This stops the loss from growing.

**Phase 2: Damage assessment and documentation (Day 1-2).** A certified project manager walks the property with a moisture meter (Tramex or similar) and a thermal imaging camera to map all wet materials. This produces the moisture map that drives the drying plan and supports the insurance scope.

**Phase 3: Structural drying and debris removal (Days 2-7, depending on scope).** Industrial LGR dehumidifiers and high-velocity air movers are deployed throughout the affected areas. Wet drywall, insulation, and flooring that cannot be dried in place are removed. Drying logs are recorded daily and submitted to the insurance carrier as proof of progress.

**Phase 4: Contents handling (concurrent with Phase 3).** Damaged inventory, furniture, and equipment are inventoried, packed out if salvageable, and either restored off-site or documented for replacement. Contents restoration is a separate line item on the insurance scope.

**Phase 5: Reconstruction (Days 7-30+).** Once the structure is dry and the insurance scope is approved, reconstruction begins: framing, drywall, roofing, windows, flooring, paint. For large commercial properties, this phase can run several weeks.

**Phase 6: Final inspection and clearance.** Before the business reopens, the restored areas are inspected for moisture levels at or below the IICRC S500 standard (typically below 16% moisture content in wood framing, below 1% in concrete). Clearance documentation goes to the insurance carrier and the property owner.

## Will commercial property insurance cover storm damage in Texas?

Most commercial property policies in Texas cover sudden, accidental storm damage including hail, wind, and the resulting water intrusion. What they typically do not cover: pre-existing roof deterioration, gradual leaks, and flood damage from rising water (that requires a separate NFPA-compliant commercial flood policy through the [National Flood Insurance Program](https://www.fema.gov/flood-insurance)).

Hail claims in Texas are common enough that some carriers have added separate wind and hail deductibles, often expressed as a percentage of the insured value rather than a flat dollar amount. A $2 million building with a 2% wind/hail deductible means the first $40,000 of loss comes out of pocket. Know your deductible before the storm hits.

Documentation is the single biggest factor in claim outcomes. Adjusters are looking for:

- Pre-storm condition evidence (prior inspection reports, photos, maintenance records)
- Post-storm photos taken before any repairs begin
- A written scope of work from a licensed restoration contractor
- Daily moisture logs during the drying phase
- A contents inventory with replacement values

A restoration contractor who provides all of this as part of their standard process makes the claim move faster. One who doesn't creates gaps the adjuster will use to reduce the payout.

## What makes commercial storm restoration different from residential?

Scale is the obvious difference, but the more important one is business interruption. Residential customers are displaced and uncomfortable. Commercial customers are losing revenue every day the building is closed. That changes the priority order.

For commercial properties, restoration crews often work in phases that allow partial occupancy. A retail building might restore the back office and storage areas first so staff can work while the storefront is rebuilt. A restaurant might restore the kitchen before the dining room. This phased approach requires a project manager who understands both the restoration sequence and the business's operational needs.

Building code compliance is also more complex on commercial properties. Texas requires permits for structural repairs above certain thresholds, and commercial buildings in Abilene must meet the International Building Code as adopted by the City of Abilene. A restoration contractor doing general contracting work on a commercial property needs to pull the right permits and coordinate inspections. Skipping this step creates liability for the property owner at resale or lease renewal.

For West Texas properties specifically, the combination of flat or low-slope metal roofing (common in commercial construction here) and large hailstones creates a pattern of damage that is easy to underestimate from the ground. A roofing inspection by a certified contractor is worth doing before and after any significant hail event.
