When a pipe bursts or a fire tears through a kitchen, the structure gets the headlines, but it is your belongings that carry the real weight of the loss. Furniture saturated with Category 2 water. Photo albums filmed with soot. Clothing that smells like smoke weeks after the fire is out. Contents restoration is the work of pulling those items back from the edge, using controlled cleaning, deodorization, and climate-stable storage, so that what can be saved is saved and what cannot is documented properly for your claim.

## What Contents Restoration & Storage actually involves

Contents restoration is not packing boxes and stacking them in a warehouse. It starts with a room-by-room inventory, typically photographed and logged before anything is moved, so every item has a documented condition at the time of loss. From there, restorable items are packed out to a climate-controlled facility where they are cleaned, deodorized, and dried under controlled conditions, away from the ongoing remediation work happening in your home.

The cleaning methods vary by material and contamination type. Ultrasonic cleaning tanks remove soot and smoke residue from hard surfaces, electronics housings, and porous ceramics without abrasion. Dry-cleaning and wet-cleaning protocols handle textiles based on fiber content and the category of water or smoke involved. Ozone chambers and hydroxyl generators address embedded odor in soft goods where surface cleaning alone will not reach the source.

Storage is held in a facility that maintains consistent temperature and humidity, which matters more than most people expect. A piece of wood furniture that dries too fast in an uncontrolled environment can check and warp permanently. Leather that sits in a damp trailer will begin to mold within days. The controlled environment is not a convenience, it is part of the restoration itself.

Timeline depends on the volume of items, the type of contamination, and the scope of repairs needed to the structure before redelivery. Partial pack-outs for a single-room loss may resolve in two to three weeks. Whole-house pack-outs tied to major reconstruction can extend several months, with items held securely until the home is ready to receive them.

## Our process

1. **On-site inventory and condition documentation.** Before a single item leaves your home, every piece is photographed, logged by room, and assigned a condition code. This record becomes part of your insurance claim file and protects you if any dispute arises over pre-loss condition.

2. **Triage and pack-out.** Items are sorted into three categories: restorable, non-restorable (documented for replacement), and items the homeowner elects to retain as-is. Restorable contents are packed using appropriate materials, labeled by room of origin, and transported to the cleaning facility.

3. **Cleaning and deodorization.** Each item is cleaned using the method matched to its material and contamination type. Ultrasonic tanks, dry-cleaning, wet-cleaning, thermal fogging, and hydroxyl treatment are applied as needed. Items are not released from this stage until odor and contamination levels are within acceptable limits.

4. **Climate-controlled storage.** Cleaned items move into the storage facility, where temperature and relative humidity are monitored continuously. Items are stored by room and cross-referenced to the original inventory so redelivery can be organized room by room.

5. **Redelivery and walk-through.** When the structure is ready, contents are returned in room order. A walk-through confirms condition against the original inventory, and any items that did not restore to an acceptable standard are flagged for the adjuster's review.

## What separates a good contents response from a bad one

The most common failure in contents restoration is skipping the pre-move inventory. Without it, there is no baseline for condition at the time of loss, which creates disputes with adjusters and leaves homeowners without documentation for items that were damaged in transit or during cleaning. A thorough photo-and-log inventory before anything moves is not optional, it is the foundation of a defensible claim.

The second common failure is using a one-size cleaning method. Wet-cleaning a water-sensitive textile, or running a heat-sensitive electronic through a process it cannot tolerate, causes secondary damage that is often worse than the original loss. Operators who do this work correctly match the cleaning protocol to the item, not to what is fastest.

Insurance adjusters look for the inventory log, the condition codes, the cleaning method applied to each item, and the storage facility's climate records. A well-documented contents pack-out moves through the claims process faster and with fewer disputes. Gaps in that documentation slow everything down and sometimes result in items being depreciated rather than restored.
