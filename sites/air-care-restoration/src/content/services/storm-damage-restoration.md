A storm doesn't give you a warning before the roof decking gets wet. One hour of hail, a single downed oak limb, or a fast-moving West Texas thunderstorm can open your home to rain intrusion, structural loading, and the kind of hidden moisture that turns into a mold problem within 48 to 72 hours. Storm damage restoration is the work of stopping that clock — securing the envelope, extracting standing water, and methodically drying the structure before secondary damage compounds what the weather started.

## What storm damage restoration actually involves

Storm losses are rarely one thing. A tornado or severe thunderstorm typically delivers a combination of wind damage, impact damage from debris or falling trees, and water intrusion — all at once. That layering is what makes storm restoration different from a straightforward water call or a simple roof repair.

The immediate priority is the building envelope: any opening that allows rain to continue entering has to be closed before drying equipment can do meaningful work. That means emergency tarping and board-up, not as a formality, but as a prerequisite. Once the structure is protected, the water intrusion is assessed — not just where it's visible, but where it has traveled. Water follows framing cavities, runs along vapor barriers, and pools in wall assemblies in ways that look dry at the surface. Thermal imaging and calibrated moisture meters map what the eye misses.

From there, the work splits into parallel tracks: structural drying with commercial-grade desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers and high-velocity air movers, alongside debris removal and damage documentation for the insurance claim. Depending on the severity, reconstruction — from sheathing and insulation replacement to full interior finishing — follows once the structure passes drying verification.

Timeline varies with the size of the opening and how long rain was entering before the call. A contained roof puncture caught within hours dries differently than a gable wall that was open through a weekend storm.

## Our process

1. **Emergency securing of the structure.** Tarps, plywood board-up, and temporary fencing where needed. Nothing downstream works if rain is still entering. This step happens before any equipment is staged.

2. **Damage mapping and moisture intrusion assessment.** Infrared thermal imaging identifies moisture migration behind walls, ceilings, and under flooring that surface inspection misses. Every affected area is logged with moisture readings and photographs — the same documentation your insurance adjuster will rely on.

3. **Debris and hazard removal.** Tree limbs, broken glass, displaced roofing materials, and compromised structural members are cleared and categorized. Hazards like exposed fasteners, unstable framing, or downed utility lines adjacent to the work area are flagged before crews proceed.

4. **Structural drying and air quality control.** Commercial dehumidifiers, air movers, and where indicated, desiccant drying systems are positioned to dry the structure from the inside out. Moisture readings are logged daily against the drying standard until affected materials reach equilibrium with unaffected areas.

5. **Reconstruction scoping and execution.** Once drying is verified, damaged materials — sheathing, insulation, drywall, flooring, roofing — are replaced to pre-loss condition. A written scope is produced before any reconstruction work begins.

## What separates a good storm damage response from a bad one

The most common failure in storm restoration is treating it as a roofing job with some cleanup attached. A contractor who patches the roof and removes visible debris but never checks moisture levels inside the wall assembly leaves a hidden drying problem that becomes a mold remediation claim six weeks later.

A few things good operators do that less-experienced ones skip:

- **Thermal imaging before drying begins.** Water that entered through a compromised ridge or soffit doesn't stay there — it migrates. Skipping infrared means drying equipment gets placed where the damage is visible, not where the moisture actually is.
- **Daily moisture logs.** Insurance carriers and adjusters expect documentation showing the structure reached acceptable moisture levels before reconstruction. Verbal assurances don't satisfy a carrier's documentation requirements.
- **Identifying concealed structural damage.** Hail and wind loads can compromise roof sheathing connections, ridge boards, and rafter ties in ways that aren't obvious from a visual inspection. A thorough structural assessment protects both the homeowner and the claim.
- **Proper categorization of water intrusion.** Rainwater entering through a storm breach is generally Category 1 (clean water), but if it has contacted soil, debris, or a compromised HVAC system, that classification changes — and so does the remediation protocol.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Abilene and the broader Big Country sit in one of the more active severe weather corridors in the country. Spring and early summer bring the highest concentration of hail events, supercell thunderstorms, and tornado risk, while late summer monsoon moisture can push damaging wind events well into September. The region's clay-heavy soils drain slowly, which means water that enters a slab-on-grade foundation area during a heavy storm event can take longer to dissipate than in sandier soil profiles — a factor in how long drying equipment needs to run.

Older housing stock in established Abilene neighborhoods — much of it built in the mid-20th century — often has original roof decking, aging flashing, and minimal attic insulation, all of which make storm intrusion more likely and harder to fully dry without careful moisture mapping.
