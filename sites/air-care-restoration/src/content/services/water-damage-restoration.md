Standing water moves fast. Within the first hour, it seeps under baseboards and wicks into drywall. By 24 hours, wood subfloors begin to swell and the conditions for mold growth are already forming. By 72 hours, structural materials that looked fine on the surface can be saturated several inches deep — and a moisture meter will tell a very different story than your eyes. Water damage restoration is a race against that clock, and the goal isn't just removing visible water. It's pulling moisture out of the materials that absorbed it before the damage compounds.

## What Water Damage Restoration actually involves

Water removal is the visible part of the job — the part with the truck-mounted extractors, the wet-vacs, and the hoses running out the front door. But extraction is only the beginning. Once standing water is gone, the real work shifts to what's hidden: moisture trapped inside wall cavities, beneath flooring, and in the framing behind tile or plaster.

Structural drying uses a combination of industrial air movers and refrigerant or desiccant dehumidifiers to create a controlled drying environment. Air movers accelerate evaporation off wet surfaces; dehumidifiers pull that vapor out of the air before it can re-absorb into surrounding materials. The equipment runs continuously — typically for several days — and the drying chamber is monitored with calibrated moisture meters and thermal imaging to confirm that readings are trending toward dry, not just surface-dry.

The category of water matters significantly here. Clean water from a supply line (Category 1) is handled differently than water that has sat long enough to degrade, or that came from an appliance drain or toilet overflow (Category 2). Sewage backups and floodwater (Category 3) require containment, personal protective equipment, and antimicrobial treatment in addition to extraction and drying. The IICRC S500 standard governs how each category is handled — and a crew that treats a Category 2 loss the same as a Category 1 loss is skipping steps that matter.

## Our process

1. **Emergency water extraction** — Truck-mounted and portable extractors remove standing water from floors, carpet, and any accessible cavity. The faster this step happens, the more material can be saved rather than replaced.

2. **Moisture mapping** — Before any drying equipment is placed, the affected area is mapped with penetrating moisture meters and a thermal camera. This establishes a baseline and identifies hidden saturation that isn't visible — wet insulation behind drywall, saturated subfloor beneath hardwood, moisture migrating along a concrete slab.

3. **Containment (Category 2 and 3 losses)** — When water source or dwell time elevates the contamination category, affected areas are isolated with poly sheeting and negative air pressure to prevent cross-contamination to dry areas of the structure.

4. **Structural drying** — Industrial air movers and dehumidifiers run in a calculated configuration based on the square footage, material types, and ambient conditions. Daily moisture readings track progress against the drying goal. Equipment is adjusted or repositioned as materials dry unevenly.

5. **Final moisture verification and documentation** — Drying is not complete when the floor feels dry underfoot. It is complete when moisture readings across all affected materials return to acceptable levels for the material type and regional climate. Those readings are documented and included in the claim file.

## What separates a good water damage response from a bad one

The most common mistake in water mitigation is declaring a structure dry too soon. Surface readings can normalize while wall cavities or subfloor assemblies remain well above acceptable moisture content. Mold can colonize in as little as 48 to 72 hours in those conditions — meaning a job that appeared finished becomes a mold remediation project weeks later.

A second common failure is under-scoping the drying chamber. Placing one dehumidifier in a 1,200-square-foot affected area and calling it done is not structural drying — it is theater. Proper equipment placement follows the IICRC S500 psychrometric calculations for the space, not a rough estimate.

Insurance adjusters reviewing a water damage claim look for a moisture log: daily readings, equipment placement records, and a documented dry standard. Without that paper trail, carriers may dispute the scope or the duration of drying. ## Seasonal and regional considerations

Abilene's climate creates two distinct water damage seasons. Winter brings the risk of frozen pipes — particularly in older homes with exterior wall plumbing or uninsulated crawl spaces — and a single hard freeze can rupture copper or CPVC lines and release hundreds of gallons before the break is found. Summer severe weather, including hail and high-wind events common across the Big Country, drives roof and window intrusion losses. When rain follows a long dry period, the cracked clay soils around foundations can also allow water to migrate in ways it wouldn't during a wetter season. Both patterns mean water damage calls in Abilene tend to cluster: freeze events in January and February, storm intrusion from spring through early fall.
