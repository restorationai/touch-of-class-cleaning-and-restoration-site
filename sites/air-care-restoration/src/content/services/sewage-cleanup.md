When a sewer line backs up or a septic system overflows, what comes up with it is classified as Category 3 water — the most contaminated class of water loss, carrying bacteria, viruses, and parasites that can survive on porous surfaces for days. The smell hits first, usually in a bathroom or utility room, and the instinct is to grab a mop. That instinct is dangerous. Raw sewage contamination is not a cleanup job; it is a sanitization protocol, and the difference between those two things is what determines whether your home is actually safe to occupy again.

## What sewage cleanup and sanitization actually involves

Sewage backup cleanup is fundamentally different from a clean-water flood. The contamination is biological, not just wet. That means every porous material the sewage contacted — drywall, insulation, subfloor, carpet, wood framing — must be evaluated not just for moisture content but for pathogen exposure. Materials that can be dried in a clean-water loss often cannot be saved here.

The work involves industrial extraction of standing sewage, followed by removal of contaminated materials down to a cleanable substrate. Technicians work in full PPE — respirators, Tyvek suits, nitrile gloves — because the airborne pathogen load in an active sewage loss is significant. Once the gross contamination is removed, affected surfaces are treated with EPA-registered antimicrobial and disinfectant agents, not household bleach. Air scrubbers with HEPA filtration run continuously to capture aerosolized contaminants. Moisture readings are taken at every structural layer — surface, cavity, and subfloor — because sewage wicks into wall systems faster than clean water does.

Depending on the extent of the backup, the sanitization phase alone can take 24 to 48 hours before drying equipment is even set. Rushing that window is where most problems start.

## Our process

1. **Containment and PPE staging.** Before any extraction begins, the affected area is isolated with physical barriers and negative air pressure is established where needed. This prevents cross-contamination to unaffected rooms. All personnel on-site suit up fully before entering the loss zone.

2. **Sewage extraction and gross contamination removal.** Standing sewage is extracted using truck-mounted or portable units rated for Category 3 material. Solid waste is bagged and disposed of per applicable waste handling requirements. Saturated porous materials — drywall, insulation, flooring, cabinetry — are removed and documented before disposal.

3. **EPA-registered disinfection of all affected surfaces.** After demo, every exposed surface is treated with an EPA-registered disinfectant formulated for Category 3 losses. This is applied in multiple passes and allowed full dwell time — the contact time required for the product to actually kill pathogens, not just wet the surface.

4. **Structural drying and moisture verification.** Once surfaces pass disinfection, commercial drying equipment — desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers, high-velocity air movers — is placed to dry the structural cavity. Moisture readings are logged daily and compared against dry standard readings for the material type. Equipment is not removed until readings are consistent and within acceptable range.

5. **Post-remediation documentation.** Final moisture readings, photographic documentation of pre- and post-conditions, and a written scope of completed work are compiled for your records and for submission to your insurance carrier.

## What separates a good sewage cleanup response from a bad one

The most common failure in sewage backup cleanup is treating it like a water damage job. Technicians who skip or shorten the disinfection dwell time — because the product looks like it's working — leave pathogens behind on surfaces that will then be enclosed behind new drywall. There is no way to know this happened until mold or odor returns months later, at which point the claim is far more complicated.

A second common miss is incomplete material removal. Sewage travels. It follows wall cavities, gets under floating floors, and saturates subfloor assemblies that look dry at the surface. Experienced operators probe and moisture-map the full affected zone before deciding what stays and what goes. Insurance adjusters reviewing a sewage claim will look for documentation that Category 3 protocols were followed — specifically, that affected porous materials were removed rather than dried in place, and that EPA-registered disinfectants (not general-purpose cleaners) were used.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Abilene sits in a region where clay-heavy soils expand significantly during wet seasons and contract during the dry stretches that define West Texas summers. That soil movement puts lateral stress on older clay tile and cast iron sewer laterals, accelerating joint separation and root intrusion — two of the most common causes of recurring sewer line backups in established neighborhoods. Homes built before the 1980s in Abilene are particularly likely to have aging clay tile laterals that have never been inspected or lined. A sewage backup in one of these homes is often a symptom of a line problem, not just a clog, and addressing the cleanup without addressing the line means the backup will happen again.
