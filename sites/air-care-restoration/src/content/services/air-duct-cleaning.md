When you run your HVAC system and notice a faint musty smell, a thin film of dust resettling on surfaces within days of cleaning, or family members sneezing more than usual indoors, the ductwork is often the overlooked culprit. Over years of normal use, residential and commercial air ducts accumulate layers of dust, pollen, pet dander, insulation fibers, and in humid West Texas climates, microbial growth that gets redistributed every time the system cycles. Air duct cleaning removes that buildup at the source — not just at the register.

## What air duct cleaning actually involves

Duct cleaning is not a technician running a household vacuum into a supply register for twenty minutes. A proper HVAC duct cleaning job uses truck-mounted or high-powered portable negative air machines — typically rated at 3,000 to 5,000 CFM — that place the entire duct system under negative pressure before a single brush or whip touches the interior walls. That negative pressure matters: it prevents loosened debris from migrating into living spaces during agitation.

From there, rotary brush systems, pneumatic whips, and compressed air tools are worked through each supply and return run, dislodging debris that has compacted against duct walls and settled in elbows and transitions. Registers and grilles are removed, wiped down, and reinstalled. The air handler cabinet, blower wheel, and evaporator coil housing are inspected and cleaned as part of a complete service — skipping those components means recontaminating clean ductwork the next time the fan runs.

For homes with dryer vent cleaning needs, that line is serviced separately: lint accumulation in the exhaust run is a documented fire risk, and the clearing process differs from supply-and-return duct work.

A typical single-family home takes two to four hours. Larger homes, systems with extensive flex duct runs, or ductwork that has not been cleaned in a decade or more may run longer.

## Our process

1. **System inspection and pre-cleaning assessment.** Before equipment is staged, the duct layout is mapped, access points are identified, and the condition of the ductwork is documented — including any visible mold, pest intrusion, damaged flex duct, or disconnected runs. This step determines whether air duct sanitization is warranted after mechanical cleaning.

2. **Negative pressure setup.** A high-CFM negative air machine is connected to the main trunk or a strategically chosen return — placing the system under negative pressure before agitation begins. Supply registers are sealed with foam blocks so that loosened debris travels toward the collection point rather than into the room.

3. **Mechanical agitation of all runs.** Rotary brushes and pneumatic whip lines are introduced through access points and driven through each supply and return run. Stubborn compacted debris in elbows and at register boots is worked free. The blower compartment and air handler cabinet are cleaned during this phase.

4. **Air duct sanitization (when indicated).** If the pre-cleaning inspection identified microbial growth, a residual odor source, or post-remediation conditions, an EPA-registered sanitizing agent is fogged or applied through the duct system after mechanical cleaning is complete. Sanitization is a separate step — applying it before mechanical cleaning would be like painting over dirt.

5. **Post-cleaning verification and documentation.** Registers are reinstalled, the system is returned to operation, and airflow at each register is confirmed. Before-and-after photographs of duct interiors, the blower wheel, and the air handler are provided as part of the service record.

## What separates a good duct cleaning from a bad one

The most common shortcut in residential duct cleaning is skipping negative pressure — a technician with a portable vacuum and a brush can dislodge debris without capturing it, spreading particulate into the living space rather than removing it. If a company cannot describe how they contain debris during agitation, that is a meaningful gap.

A second frequent miss is treating duct cleaning as register-only work. The blower wheel is one of the highest-accumulation points in the entire system; a dirty blower wheel recontaminates supply runs within weeks of a cleaning that ignored it.

For older Abilene homes — many built in the 1950s through 1970s with original galvanized steel ductwork — technicians should check for deteriorating duct liner, gaps at joints that have lost their mastic seal, and any asbestos-containing insulation on or near duct surfaces before mechanical agitation begins. Disturbing those materials without proper protocol creates a hazard that outlasts the cleaning job. Air Care Restoration is EPA Lead-Safe Certified and OSHA trained, and the pre-cleaning inspection accounts for these conditions.

For dryer vent cleaning specifically, the full length of the exhaust run — including any in-wall or in-ceiling sections — should be cleared and the exterior termination cap inspected. A clean first six inches of vent does not mean the run behind the wall is clear.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

Abilene's climate creates a specific set of duct-loading conditions. The region's persistent wind and frequent dust events — particularly during dry spring months — push fine particulate through building envelopes at a higher rate than more sheltered climates. Homes with older weatherstripping, gaps around penetrations, or attic ductwork in contact with blown-in insulation tend to accumulate debris faster than the national average cleaning interval of three to five years would suggest.

Summer heat also means HVAC systems in West Texas run long cycles, moving large volumes of air through ductwork daily. That sustained airflow carries whatever is in the duct system into living spaces continuously. Scheduling a cleaning before peak cooling season — late spring — means the system starts summer with clean distribution.
