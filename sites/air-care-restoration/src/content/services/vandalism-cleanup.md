## What Vandalism Cleanup actually involves

Vandalism cleanup is not a single task. It is a sequenced response to several categories of damage that often occur together: aerosol and paint-based graffiti on exterior masonry, stucco, brick, metal, or glass; broken glass embedded in flooring, upholstery, and HVAC returns; structural breaches at doors, windows, and frames; and interior damage to drywall, fixtures, and contents.

Each material type requires a different removal method. Latex spray paint on unpainted brick responds to alkaline strippers and low-pressure washing. Oil-based paint on glass requires mechanical scraping followed by solvent treatment. Permanent marker on a painted interior wall often means the affected surface needs a stain-blocking primer before any topcoat will hold. Attempting a one-size approach to graffiti removal is one of the fastest ways to damage the substrate underneath.

Broken glass cleanup is its own discipline. Visible shards are the easy part. Fine glass particles migrate into carpet backing, settle into HVAC floor registers, and embed in upholstered furniture. An industrial HEPA vacuum and UV light inspection are standard tools for a thorough sweep. Skipping that step leaves a hazard that shows up weeks later.

Timeline varies by scope. A single exterior graffiti tag on a smooth painted surface can be addressed in a few hours. A multi-surface incident involving broken windows, interior damage, and structural repair to a door frame is a multi-day project.

## Our process

1. **Damage documentation and scope assessment.** Before any cleaning begins, the crew photographs and catalogs every affected surface, material type, and point of entry. This documentation is the foundation of any insurance claim and determines which removal methods apply where.

2. **Hazard control and site security.** Broken windows and compromised entry points are boarded or temporarily glazed to secure the property before cleanup proceeds. This step also prevents secondary damage from weather or additional unauthorized entry.

3. **Graffiti and paint removal, surface by surface.** Removal agents are matched to the substrate: alkaline strippers for masonry, solvent-based products for metal and glass, abrasive media blasting where appropriate, and low-pressure washing to clear residue. Painted surfaces that cannot be fully stripped are primed with a stain-blocking product before any finish coat.

4. **Glass and debris removal.** All visible broken glass is collected and bagged for disposal. Affected carpet, flooring, and upholstered surfaces are then swept with an industrial HEPA vacuum. Floor registers and HVAC returns in the impact zone are inspected and cleared.

5. **Surface restoration and repair.** Damaged drywall is patched, primed, and finished. Door frames and lock sets are assessed for structural integrity. Exterior surfaces are repainted or recoated to match the existing finish. The goal is a property that shows no visible evidence of the incident.

## What separates a good vandalism cleanup response from a bad one

The most common mistake in post-vandalism restoration is treating graffiti removal as pressure washing and calling it done. Pressure washing at the wrong PSI drives paint deeper into porous masonry rather than lifting it, and it can erode mortar joints in older brick. A competent crew tests the removal method on a small, inconspicuous area before committing to the full surface.

Insurance adjusters look for two things in a vandalism claim: a complete photographic inventory of damage taken before any work begins, and a written scope that separates cosmetic repair from structural repair. Mixing those categories in a single line item creates disputes. Experienced restoration crews document them separately from the start.

Glass cleanup is consistently under-scoped by less experienced operators. A single broken window in a room with carpet and floor registers requires more than sweeping the visible pieces. Adjusters increasingly ask for HEPA vacuum logs and post-cleanup inspection notes on glass-involved losses.

For properties with painted or coated exterior surfaces, color-match documentation matters. A repaint that is two shades off on a commercial storefront or a historic home creates a new problem on top of the original one.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

In Abilene and across West Texas, exterior masonry and stucco are common building materials, and both are porous. Paint and aerosol products penetrate those surfaces faster in summer heat, when the substrate is warm and dry. A tag applied in July heat has often bonded more deeply by the following morning than the same tag applied in cooler conditions. Prompt response is especially important during the warmer months.

Wind-driven debris after a vandalism incident can also complicate glass cleanup. Abilene's persistent southwest winds mean that fine glass particles on an exterior surface can travel further than they would in calmer climates, and any adjacent HVAC intake should be inspected after a broken-glass event.
