## What Reconstruction Services actually involves

When water, fire, or storm damage tears into drywall, framing, flooring, or cabinetry, mitigation only gets the property dry or clean. Reconstruction is the work that comes after: replacing what had to be removed, rebuilding what was structurally compromised, and returning the space to a livable or code-compliant condition. That can mean swapping out a few feet of baseboard and drywall after a small pipe leak, or it can mean reframing a kitchen wall, re-hanging drywall, re-running electrical, and refinishing floors after a fire burned through a load-bearing section. The scope depends entirely on what the demolition phase exposed, which is why reconstruction always starts with a written scope of work rather than a guess.

For most Corona homes, this means working with plaster-and-lath construction in older neighborhoods near Downtown and newer stucco-over-framing builds in developments south toward Eagle Glen. Each construction type behaves differently once it's opened up, and a reconstruction crew needs to know the difference before framing goes back in.

## Our process

1. **Scope of work and documentation.** Before any material is ordered, we document the damaged area with photos, measurements, and a line-item scope that matches what the mitigation phase removed. This document is what gets submitted to your insurance carrier and what you sign off on before work starts.
2. **Permits and code review.** Structural work, electrical, plumbing relocation, and certain drywall or framing repairs in Corona require permits through the city's Building Division. We pull the permits required for the scope and schedule inspections at the points the inspector needs to see open framing or rough-in work.
3. **Framing and structural repair.** Compromised studs, joists, subfloor, or load-bearing elements get replaced to match the original structure. This is the step where a rushed job shows up years later as a sagging floor or a door that won't close.
4. **Rough-in and inspection.** Electrical, plumbing, and insulation go back in before drywall closes the wall. Inspections happen here, not after the fact.
5. **Finishing.** Drywall, texture, paint, flooring, trim, and cabinetry are installed to match the pre-loss condition as closely as materials allow. We walk the space with you before calling the job complete.

## What separates a good reconstruction response from a bad one

The most common mistake in post-damage rebuilding isn't bad carpentry, it's mismatched timing between mitigation and reconstruction. If drying isn't verified complete before framing closes up, moisture gets sealed behind new drywall and the same mold or rot problem resurfaces months later. A reconstruction crew that didn't do the mitigation work (or doesn't coordinate with whoever did) often skips this check entirely.

Adjusters also look closely at whether the scope of work matches the actual damage, not a padded estimate or a stripped-down one. Underscoping to win a low bid means change orders mid-project and delays; overscoping invites a claim denial on the excess. A scope built directly from documented mitigation findings, photographed before closing any wall, holds up better on both sides.

The other place corners get cut is matching. Flooring, trim profiles, and cabinet finishes that don't match the surrounding, undamaged areas create a visibly patched result even when the construction itself is sound. Sourcing materials that match existing finishes takes longer but is part of what a written scope should account for up front.

## What does reconstruction services cost?

Reconstruction costs vary more than almost any other restoration service because the scope depends entirely on what was damaged, how much had to be removed, and what finish materials are being matched. The ranges below are typical industry figures, not a quote. Every loss is different, which is why we provide a written scope of work with itemized pricing before any reconstruction begins.

| Scenario | Typical range |
|---|---|
| Single room drywall and paint repair | $1,500 - $4,000 |
| Flooring replacement (per room) | $2,000 - $6,000 |
| Kitchen cabinet and counter rebuild | $8,000 - $25,000 |
| Partial structural reframing (wall section) | $5,000 - $15,000 |
| Fire damage reconstruction (single room, structural) | $10,000 - $40,000+ |
| Whole-floor reconstruction after major loss | $40,000 and up |

Homeowners insurance typically covers reconstruction costs tied to a covered peril, such as a burst pipe or a fire, up to your policy's dwelling coverage limits, though cosmetic upgrades beyond matching pre-loss condition are usually an out-of-pocket addition.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Corona's dry summers and occasional winter storms both create reconstruction work, but on different timelines. Summer fire season means reconstruction crews are often rebuilding around smoke and structural fire damage, where framing and electrical often need full replacement rather than patching. Winter and early spring storms bring roof and window-related water intrusion, where the reconstruction scope is usually smaller but time-sensitive, since open framing left exposed during a storm cycle can pick up new moisture before the rebuild even starts.
