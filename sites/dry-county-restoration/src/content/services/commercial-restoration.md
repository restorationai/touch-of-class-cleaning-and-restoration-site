A burst supply line over a weekend can flood a suite before anyone notices on Monday. A kitchen fire in one tenant space can push smoke through a shared HVAC system into every unit on the floor. Commercial losses behave differently than residential ones because the building keeps multiple stakeholders, multiple code requirements, and revenue exposure in play at the same time. Dry County Restoration handles commercial water damage, commercial fire damage, and the retail, office, and multi-tenant restoration work that comes with running a business out of a damaged space.

## What Commercial Restoration actually involves

Commercial restoration covers the same core disciplines as residential work, water extraction, structural drying, fire and soot cleanup, but layered with building systems, tenant coordination, and compliance that a single-family home doesn't have. That means raised flooring in server rooms, drop ceilings hiding ductwork and sprinkler lines, HVAC systems that can spread contamination from one suite to the next, and retail or restaurant spaces where health department sign-off matters as much as drying logs. Equipment scales with square footage: commercial-grade LGR dehumidifiers, axial air movers staged for open floor plans, and in fire losses, HEPA air scrubbers sized for the HVAC zone rather than a single room. Timelines vary widely. A single-suite water loss might dry in three to five days. A multi-floor fire with reconstruction can run weeks, and the restoration crew is often working around an active lease, tenant move-back schedule, or operating hours that can't fully stop.

## Our process

1. **Loss assessment and business impact triage.** Before any equipment goes in, we identify life-safety concerns, confirm utilities are safe to run, and map which parts of the space can stay operational versus which need to be closed off.
2. **Containment and phased work planning.** In multi-tenant buildings or active retail spaces, we build a plan that isolates the affected area with physical barriers and negative air where needed, so neighboring units or the sales floor aren't shut down unnecessarily.
3. **Extraction and mitigation specific to commercial systems.** This includes pulling water from under raised flooring, drying wall cavities behind fixtures, and addressing HVAC ductwork that may have carried moisture, soot, or odor into spaces far from the original loss.
4. **Reconstruction and code compliance.** Drywall, flooring, and fixtures go back per the agreed scope, with permits pulled where the work requires them and attention to ADA and, for food service tenants, health department requirements before reopening.
5. **Final walkthrough and documentation.** We close out with moisture readings, photos, and a written record that supports both the insurance claim and any business interruption documentation the property manager or tenant needs.

## What separates a good commercial restoration response from a bad one

The most common miss in commercial losses is treating the building like a big house. Moisture behind a server rack or under a raised computer floor doesn't show up the same way it does in residential drywall, and a rushed inspection will miss it. HVAC cross-contamination is another one: smoke or mold spores pulled into a return vent can show up in a tenant space two floors away weeks later if the ductwork wasn't addressed at the source. Adjusters reviewing commercial claims also look for pre-loss documentation, inventory records, and a clear line between mitigation costs and business interruption costs, so a restoration company that doesn't separate those categories in its paperwork can slow down a claim significantly. A careful operator documents moisture and air quality readings by zone, not just by building, and builds a phased plan that keeps as much of the business running as the loss allows.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Corona's commercial buildings see two recurring patterns. Flat commercial roofs common on retail strips and office parks are vulnerable to ponding and leaks during atmospheric river storms in winter, and that water tends to travel along ceiling grids before it shows up over a tenant space far from the actual roof failure. In late summer and fall, Santa Ana wind events and regional wildfire smoke can infiltrate HVAC intake systems in commercial buildings, leaving a persistent smoke odor in office and retail spaces that never had an open flame on-site.
