A broken window, a kicked-in door, a roof torn open by fire or wind — the damage that follows in the next few hours can dwarf whatever caused the opening in the first place. Rain gets in. Looters do too. Soot-laden air migrates into rooms that weren't touched by flames. The building envelope is the first thing that has to close before anything else can be assessed or dried or rebuilt. That's what emergency board-up and tarping is: not a cosmetic patch, but the first structural decision of the entire recovery.

## What emergency board-up and tarping actually involves

Board-up and tarping is the process of mechanically securing every breach in a structure — windows, doors, wall openings, and roof penetrations — to stop weather intrusion, unauthorized entry, and the continued spread of damage from an already-active loss.

For window and door openings, crews cut and fasten plywood panels (typically ⅝" CDX) directly to the framing or surrounding masonry, not just taped over the glass frame. Openings left unsecured even overnight invite secondary water intrusion that can saturate subfloor assemblies, activate dormant mold spores within 24 to 48 hours, and turn a contained fire loss into a combined fire-and-water claim.

For roof damage, tarping involves laying and anchoring polyethylene sheeting — commercial-grade, not the blue hardware-store variety — over the damaged section, weighted and fastened at the ridge and eaves to withstand sustained wind. A tarp installed without ridge anchoring will peel back in the first Santa Ana condition, leaving the opening worse than before.

The work is fast by design: most openings can be secured within a few hours of arrival. The goal is not a finished repair — it's a weathertight, secure structure that stops the clock on further loss.

## Our process

1. **Damage assessment and photo documentation.** Before a single sheet of plywood is cut, every breach is photographed and measured. This documentation becomes part of the insurance claim file — adjusters need to see the condition of the opening before securing, not just after.

2. **Hazard identification.** Fire-damaged structures present specific entry hazards: compromised floor joists, live electrical panels, weakened roof decking. Crews identify these before committing weight to any surface. OSHA-trained technicians assess structural stability before staging materials inside.

3. **Mechanical board-up of windows, doors, and wall openings.** Plywood panels are cut to fit each opening and fastened to solid framing — not drywall, not window casing alone. Where masonry or stucco surrounds the opening, appropriate anchoring hardware is used. Every panel is labeled with the opening number that corresponds to the site diagram.

4. **Roof tarping and anchoring.** Tarps are laid from the ridge down, overlapping any adjacent intact roofing by at least two feet, and secured with batten boards screwed through the tarp into the roof decking — not sandbags, which shift. Ridge anchoring prevents wind uplift. The perimeter is sealed to reduce wind-driven rain from entering at the edges.

5. **Final walkthrough and scope handoff.** The completed board-up is re-documented with photos, and a written scope of the work performed is provided to the property owner and, when applicable, the insurance carrier. This scope is what the adjuster uses to authorize the next phase of work.

## What separates a good board-up response from a bad one

The most common failure in board-up work isn't the materials — it's the anchoring. Plywood screwed only into window trim or vinyl casing will pull free under pressure. Roof tarps laid flat without ridge attachment become sails in the first wind event. These aren't minor oversights; they're the difference between a loss that stays contained and one that compounds overnight.

A second common failure is incomplete coverage. Crews in a hurry will board the obvious openings — the front windows, the door — and miss the attic vent knocked loose by the fire department, the crawlspace access that's now exposed, or the HVAC chase that connects the fire-damaged wing to the rest of the house. Insurance adjusters reviewing a subsequent water or mold claim will ask whether all openings were secured. The site diagram and photo set from the initial board-up is the answer to that question.

Finally, documentation quality matters as much as the physical work. An adjuster cannot authorize coverage for secondary damage that occurred after board-up if there's no timestamped record showing what was secured and when. Thorough pre- and post-board-up photography is not optional — it's the evidentiary foundation of the claim.

## Seasonal and regional considerations

In the Inland Empire and the Corona area specifically, two seasonal patterns drive the majority of emergency board-up calls. Santa Ana wind events — most frequent from October through March — generate the roof damage and broken-window calls that arrive in clusters after a single overnight wind. A structure left open after that kind of event faces immediate re-exposure if the next Santa Ana follows within days, which is common.

The second pattern is the post-fire window. After a wildland or structure fire, the combination of compromised roofing and the region's dry, windy conditions means that even a small opening can allow significant particulate and weather intrusion within hours. Securing the structure before the next wind event is not a suggestion — it's the difference between a recoverable loss and a total.
