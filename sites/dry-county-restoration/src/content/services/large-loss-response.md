When a loss crosses a few thousand square feet, involves multiple structures, or shuts down a business, the standard residential playbook doesn't scale. A single apartment building with water intrusion on four floors, a commercial kitchen fire that knocked out HVAC and electrical, or a multi-unit property hit by wind and flood in the same storm all need a different kind of response: more equipment staged faster, a documentation system that can survive a six-figure claim, and a crew structure that can run multiple zones at once without losing track of moisture readings, soot layers, or contents. ## What large loss and catastrophic response actually involves

A large loss is typically defined by scope, not just dollar figure, though multi-million dollar restoration claims are common in this category: multiple affected units, mixed categories of damage (water, fire, wind) on one property, or a commercial operation where downtime itself is part of the loss. The work draws on the same core disciplines as residential water, fire, and mold work, structural drying per IICRC S500, soot and smoke protocols, antimicrobial application, but at a scale that requires psychrometric mapping across dozens of rooms, bulk equipment deployment (desiccant dehumidifiers, negative air machines, axial movers by the pallet), and a project management layer that tracks moisture logs, drying curves, and line-item costs across the entire structure simultaneously. Timelines run from days for a contained commercial flood to months for a catastrophic fire rebuild, and the first 24 to 48 hours of mitigation decisions usually determine how much of the structure is salvageable versus how much moves into reconstruction.

## Our process

1. **Rapid site assessment and scope mapping.** Before equipment moves in, the building gets zoned: which areas are affected, which categories of contamination are present, and which systems (electrical, HVAC, plumbing) need to be isolated before work starts.
2. **Mobilization and bulk equipment deployment.** Large loss jobs need large loss equipment counts, truck-mounted extraction, commercial-grade dehumidification, air scrubbers for multiple containment zones, staged and running concurrently rather than sequentially.
3. **Multi-zone drying and monitoring.** Moisture readings get logged by zone on a documented schedule, not spot-checked, so drying decisions and equipment pulls are defensible if an adjuster or engineer reviews the file later.
4. **Contents and structural triage.** Salvageable contents are inventoried and either cleaned on-site or packed out; structural elements are evaluated for demolition versus drying based on material type and saturation depth.
5. **Documentation handoff and reconstruction transition.** A complete file, photos, moisture logs, equipment run times, line-item scope, moves from mitigation into the reconstruction phase or directly to claim settlement.

## What separates a good large loss response from a bad one

The failure point on big jobs is almost never the drying science, it's coordination. Crews that aren't used to catastrophic loss response tend to under-staff the first 24 hours, which lets secondary damage (mold colonization, additional material failure) catch up to the drying effort before it's ever ahead of it. Documentation is the other gap: large loss adjusters expect zone-by-zone moisture logs, daily equipment counts, and photo-backed progress, not a single summary report at the end. A response that can't produce that paper trail in real time tends to get claims disputed or delayed, which costs the property owner more than the mitigation itself. Good catastrophic response also means having a clear line between mitigation scope and reconstruction scope from day one, so the transition between phases doesn't stall while everyone waits on a new estimate.
