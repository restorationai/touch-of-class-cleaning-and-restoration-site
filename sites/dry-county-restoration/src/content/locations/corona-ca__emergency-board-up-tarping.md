## Why Corona Properties See Board-Up and Tarping Emergencies

The Santa Ana wind events that roll through the Temescal Valley corridor and funnel across Corona's hillside neighborhoods every fall and winter are the most predictable source of sudden structural exposure in this area. Gusts regularly exceed 60 mph during peak events, and that's enough to lift ridge-cap shingles, crack older single-pane windows, and bring tree limbs through rooflines on homes that were built decades before current wind-load standards.

Corona's housing stock spans a wide range of construction eras. Neighborhoods closer to downtown include homes built in the 1950s and 1960s with wood-frame construction and original stucco exteriors — materials that hold up well in dry heat but become vulnerable when a fire or impact event compromises the envelope. Newer master-planned communities on the city's eastern and southern edges tend to have tile roofs, which look intact after a wind event but can have cracked or displaced tiles that aren't obvious from the ground. Both types of homes need fast, accurate assessment before a temporary cover goes up.

Wildfires in the hills above the Temescal Valley and along the Cleveland National Forest boundary also create board-up demand — not always from direct flame contact, but from radiant heat that shatters windows and from firefighting operations that sometimes require forced entry. Fire damage board-up in those situations has to account for soot-coated surfaces and the possibility that framing near the breach is still warm.

## Our Emergency Board-Up and Tarping Process in Corona

When you call (951) 667-9910, the first question we ask is what's open and what's inside. That shapes everything: a broken window board-up on a single-story ranch home is a different job than emergency tarping on a two-story tile roof after a wind event.

**Assessment and documentation** come first. Before any material goes up, we photograph the breach, the surrounding structure, and any visible interior damage. That documentation matters for your insurance claim and for the HOA review process that some Corona communities require before exterior alterations — even temporary ones.

**Board-up** uses exterior-grade plywood cut and fitted to the opening, secured with screws rather than nails to reduce additional damage to the frame. For fire damage board-up situations, we choose fastener placement carefully around heat-affected framing.

**Roof tarping** on Corona homes requires attention to tile and flat-roof geometry. Heavy-duty polyethylene tarps are weighted and anchored to avoid the very wind uplift that caused the damage in the first place — a tarp that catches wind becomes a second problem. On tile roofs, we work around the tile field rather than across it to avoid adding breakage.

The goal is a weathertight closure that holds until permanent repairs can be scheduled, not a quick cover that fails in the next Santa Ana event.

## Local Note

One thing that catches homeowners off guard in Corona's hillside areas: after a wind event, the damage visible from the street often understates what's happening at the roof ridge. Tile roofs in this climate are designed to shed water, not to resist lateral wind load at the peak. A row of displaced ridge caps can look minor from the driveway but leaves a channel directly into the attic space. During Santa Ana season, we always recommend a close-up ridge inspection before deciding a tarp isn't necessary — a few displaced tiles in the wrong spot can allow more water intrusion than a visibly cracked field tile lower on the slope.
