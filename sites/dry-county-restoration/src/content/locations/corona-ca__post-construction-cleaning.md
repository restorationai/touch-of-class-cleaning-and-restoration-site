Corona's construction boom hasn't slowed down — new tract homes pushing out toward the 15 freeway, gut-renovated mid-century ranches closer to the city core, and commercial tenant improvements scattered across the Inland Empire's fastest-growing corridor. When a build or remodel wraps, what's left behind isn't just sawdust. It's drywall compound haze settled into every horizontal surface, silica-fine concrete dust worked into HVAC returns, caulk smears on freshly installed windows, and adhesive residue on tile that looks clean until the afternoon light hits it wrong. Post-construction cleaning in Corona demands a different standard than a routine deep clean — and the region's dry, dusty climate means that fine particulate doesn't just sit on surfaces, it migrates.

## Why Corona's Building Environment Shapes the Cleanup

The Inland Empire's low humidity and persistent Santa Ana wind patterns create a construction-site condition that coastal contractors don't often encounter: ultra-fine dust becomes airborne repeatedly, long after crews have packed up. A renovation sealed tight overnight can still show fresh dust migration by morning because thermal cycling — hot days, cooler nights — pulls air through gaps around rough-in penetrations and unsealed framing cavities. Corona's elevation and position in the valley also means that grading and concrete work on new pads kicks up decomposed granite and silica-bearing soil that infiltrates a structure during framing before any weather barrier is in place.

Homes built in Corona's older residential pockets often have original single-pane windows and less-than-airtight construction, which means renovation dust travels further into living spaces than it would in a newer sealed envelope. Newer developments, particularly those built to current Title 24 energy standards, have tighter envelopes — but that means HVAC systems recirculate construction particulate more aggressively if air handlers aren't protected or cleaned post-build.

## Our Post-Construction Cleaning Process in Corona

The sequence matters more than the product list. Cleaning in the wrong order — wiping surfaces before removing bulk debris, or running a mop before HEPA vacuuming — just redistributes contamination.

Here's how a typical final clean unfolds on a Corona job:

**Rough-out phase:** All construction debris — cut material, packaging, fasteners, tape, backing paper — is removed from the structure before any cleaning begins. This protects finished surfaces from scratches and gives cleaners a clear field.

**HEPA vacuuming:** Every horizontal surface, every windowsill, every cabinet interior, every baseboard, and every HVAC grille gets HEPA-filtered vacuuming before any wet method touches it. This is where the silica and drywall dust gets captured rather than smeared.

**Detail cleaning:** Window tracks, door hardware, light fixtures, outlet covers, and grout lines get hand-cleaned. Paint overspray on glass is removed with appropriate tools — razor blades on tempered glass, plastic scrapers on coated surfaces — without scratching.

**Surface-by-surface final pass:** Countertops, cabinetry, appliances, and flooring get a final wipe-down calibrated to the material. Stone countertops get pH-neutral cleaners; LVP flooring gets dry or damp methods only; tile gets grout-line detailing.

**HVAC and return cleaning:** Filters are replaced, return grilles are cleaned, and supply registers are wiped. If the system ran during construction, we flag the homeowner or GC that duct cleaning may be warranted — that's a separate scope, but it's a conversation worth having before move-in.

**Final walk:** Every room is walked with a high-lumen flashlight held at a low angle to catch surface haze, smears, and missed debris before sign-off.
