Chino sits in one of the Inland Empire's dustiest corridors — caught between the Santa Ana winds that barrel through Cajon Pass and the fine particulate matter that drifts west from the dairy operations and agricultural land that still define the city's eastern edge. That combination means the air moving through a Chino home's ductwork carries a heavier load of dust, dander, and organic debris than you'd find in a coastal community. Over time, that buildup doesn't just reduce airflow — it circulates through every room every time the system kicks on.

## Why Chino Properties See Elevated Duct Contamination

The Inland Empire's climate is hard on HVAC systems in ways that aren't immediately obvious. Summer temperatures in Chino regularly push past 100°F, which means residential systems run long, hard cycles from May through October. Extended run time pulls more particulate through the filter and into the duct lining. When those same systems sit idle through cooler months, settled debris can attract moisture from overnight condensation — creating conditions where dust becomes a substrate rather than just a nuisance.

Chino's housing stock adds another layer. Much of the city developed rapidly through the 1980s and 1990s, and tract homes from that era were built with sheet-metal ductwork that has had decades to accumulate debris at seams, elbows, and return-air boots. Older flex duct — the corrugated silver tubing common in attic runs — develops interior ridges as it ages and sags, and those ridges trap particulate that a simple filter change never reaches.

## Our Air Duct Cleaning Process in Chino

The process starts with a camera inspection of the main trunk lines and a selection of branch runs. That step matters in Chino homes because it tells us whether we're dealing with a straightforward debris buildup or something more involved — pest intrusion, disconnected joints, or visible microbial growth near the air handler. We don't quote a scope until we've seen what's actually inside.

Cleaning itself uses truck-mounted negative-air equipment that places the duct system under continuous vacuum while rotating brush heads dislodge debris from the duct walls. Every supply register, return grille, and accessible boot gets cleaned individually — not just blown through. If sanitization is warranted (common after a rodent intrusion or a period of deferred maintenance), we apply an EPA-registered antimicrobial agent to interior duct surfaces after the mechanical cleaning is complete.

Dryer vent cleaning is often requested at the same time, and for good reason: lint accumulation in dryer exhaust runs is a documented fire risk, and Chino's long, hot summers mean dryers work harder and generate more lint per cycle than they would in a milder climate.

## Local Note

Homes built in Chino's master-planned communities during the late 1980s and early 1990s often have return-air chases framed directly into interior walls rather than using dedicated metal duct. Those wall-cavity returns are nearly impossible to clean mechanically — and they're also prone to pulling air from unconditioned attic space if the framing gaps weren't sealed at build. When we encounter this configuration, we flag it for the homeowner before cleaning begins, because no amount of duct cleaning compensates for a return that's drawing attic air around the filter entirely. It's a Chino-era construction detail that surprises a lot of homeowners who assumed their system was standard throughout.

## Equipment and Methods

The truck-mounted vacuum unit we use generates significantly more negative pressure than portable shop-vac style equipment — a distinction that matters in longer duct runs common in Chino's larger single-story ranch homes, where branch lines can extend 40 feet or more from the air handler. Portable units often can't maintain adequate suction at that distance. We also use HEPA-filtered collection so that what we pull out of the ducts doesn't re-enter the living space during the cleaning process.

For homes with older fiberglass duct board (common in some Chino builds from the 1980s), we adjust brush pressure and technique to avoid abrading the interior liner — damaged duct board sheds fibers into the airstream, which is worse than the original contamination.
