## Why Corona Properties See Water Damage Differently

Corona's housing stock spans several distinct eras, and each one presents its own water damage profile. Tract homes built in the 1980s and early 1990s — common in the western and central parts of the city — often used polybutylene supply lines that have long since exceeded their service life. That plumbing fails quietly, sometimes seeping behind walls for weeks before a stain appears on drywall or a floor buckles. Newer master-planned communities built in the 2000s and beyond tend to have longer interior plumbing runs and multi-story layouts where a second-floor leak can travel through floor assemblies and reach the subfloor, engineered wood, and ceiling drywall of the room below before anyone notices.

Corona's climate adds another layer. The region averages very low annual rainfall, but when storms do arrive — particularly between November and March — they arrive fast. Clay-heavy soils common across Riverside County don't absorb water quickly, so surface water and roof runoff can pond against foundations and find entry points through cracks in slabs or through the base of exterior walls. The result is a category of water intrusion that looks like a plumbing failure but is actually storm-driven groundwater.

## Our Water Damage Restoration Process in Corona

When a call comes in, the first priority is stopping the source — whether that means locating the shutoff for a burst supply line or identifying where storm water is entering. From there, the process follows the IICRC S500 standard for water damage restoration, which means moisture mapping before a single piece of equipment is placed.

Using thermal imaging cameras and calibrated moisture meters, the crew traces how far water has migrated — through wall cavities, under flooring, into cabinets. In stucco-clad homes, which are nearly universal in Corona, water can wick up the exterior sheathing behind the stucco and travel laterally along the building paper in ways that don't show up on the interior at all. That migration gets documented and addressed before drying begins.

Structural drying uses commercial-grade desiccant and refrigerant dehumidifiers alongside high-velocity air movers positioned to create directed airflow through the affected assembly. Daily moisture readings track the drying curve against psychrometric targets — drying is not declared complete based on how something looks or feels, but on what the meters read. The team holds IICRC certifications in Water Restoration (WRT) and Applied Structural Drying (ASD), which means the drying plan is built on documented science, not guesswork.

## Corona Insurance Coordination

Most standard homeowners policies cover sudden and accidental water damage — a burst pipe, a failed appliance supply line, an ice maker that leaked overnight. What they typically don't cover is long-term seepage or maintenance-related deterioration, which is why the documentation phase matters so much. Dry County Restoration photographs affected materials, records moisture readings at each inspection point, and produces a scope of loss that aligns with the format carriers and their adjusters expect to receive.

For Corona homeowners in HOA-governed communities — and there are many, particularly in the master-planned developments along the eastern corridors of the city — the documentation also helps clarify the boundary between what the HOA's master policy covers (often the structure to the original specifications) and what the individual unit owner's policy covers (improvements, contents, and loss of use). Getting that boundary wrong delays claims and can leave a homeowner holding costs that should have been covered.
