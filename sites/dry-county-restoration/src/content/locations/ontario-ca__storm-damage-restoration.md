## Why Ontario Properties See Significant Storm Damage

The Inland Valley's geography creates a compression effect during Pacific storms. As air masses push inland from the coast and drop into the Ontario basin, they accelerate — and that wind load is compounded during Santa Ana conditions, when dry, fast-moving air can gust well above typical coastal storm speeds. Ontario's housing stock reflects several distinct eras: post-war ranch homes built in the 1950s and 1960s with original wood-framed roofs and single-pane windows, and later developments from the 1980s through the 2000s with tile roofing that, while durable, can lose individual tiles in high-wind events and leave underlayment exposed to rain. Both types are vulnerable in different ways. Older homes often have deteriorated roof decking and original fascia boards that absorb water quickly once a breach opens; newer tile-roof homes may look intact from the street while harboring water intrusion at the ridge or around dormers.

The region's expansive clay soils — common across much of San Bernardino County — also affect how storm water moves after heavy rain. Clay soil saturates quickly and drains slowly, which means water pools against foundations and pushes into crawl spaces and slab edges rather than dispersing. After a significant rain event, that saturation can persist for days beneath a surface that already looks dry.

## Our Storm Damage Restoration Process in Ontario

Every storm loss is different, but the sequence of work follows a consistent logic: stop ongoing damage first, document everything for your insurer, then dry and restore in the right order.

**1. Emergency stabilization.** If a tree has come through a roof or a window is open to the weather, the first priority is a temporary barrier — tarping, board-up, or both — to stop water from continuing to enter. We carry materials on every vehicle for this.

**2. Moisture mapping.** Wind-driven rain penetrates in non-obvious paths. Water that enters at a ridge vent may travel along roof sheathing and appear inside a wall cavity two rooms away. Technicians use thermal imaging and calibrated moisture meters to trace the actual path of intrusion, not just the visible wet spots.

**3. Controlled demolition and drying.** Saturated insulation, drywall, and flooring cannot be dried in place — they have to come out. Structural drying follows the IICRC S500 standard, with commercial desiccant dehumidifiers and high-velocity air movers placed based on psychrometric readings, not guesswork. Daily monitoring tracks progress until materials reach documented dry standard.

**4. Reconstruction.** Once the structure is verified dry, rebuild begins — roofing, drywall, insulation, flooring, and paint matched to existing finishes where possible.

## Local Note

One pattern that shows up repeatedly in Ontario's older neighborhoods — particularly in areas with homes built before 1980 — is stucco applied directly over wood lath without a modern weather-resistant barrier behind it. During a wind-driven rain event, water finds its way into cracks or around window flanges and wicks directly into the framing. Because the stucco exterior feels solid and shows no obvious damage, homeowners sometimes assume the interior is fine. It often is not. If your home is from that era and you experienced a significant storm, a moisture scan of the exterior walls is worth doing even if you see no interior staining — the framing can be wet while the drywall reads normal.
