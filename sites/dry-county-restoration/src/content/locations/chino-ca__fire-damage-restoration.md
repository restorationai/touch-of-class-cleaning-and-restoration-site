## Why Chino Properties Face Particular Fire Damage Challenges

Chino's housing stock spans several decades and building eras. Tracts developed in the 1970s and 1980s often used aluminum wiring in branch circuits — a known contributor to arcing and ignition events — while newer master-planned communities built in the 2000s and beyond feature open floor plans that allow smoke to travel rapidly through large shared air volumes before a fire is even detected. The city's agricultural and dairy legacy also means some parcels sit near open land where brush fires can approach residential fences with little buffer.

Santa Ana conditions — low humidity, high temperatures, strong offshore winds — typically arrive in fall and early winter, and Chino's inland position amplifies them. During those windows, fire spreads faster and smoke is drier and more deeply penetrating than in coastal communities. That dry smoke residue bonds tightly to porous surfaces: drywall paper, wood framing, insulation batts, and the inside of ductwork. The longer it sits, the harder it is to remove without abrasive or chemical intervention.

## Our Fire Damage Restoration Process in Chino

Every fire loss is different, but the sequence follows a disciplined framework. When a crew arrives, the first priority is safety assessment — confirming structural integrity and checking for utility hazards before anyone enters the affected area. From there, the process moves through these stages:

**Emergency board-up and tarping.** Burned-through walls, missing windows, and compromised roofing sections are secured against weather and unauthorized entry. In Chino, where afternoon winds can drive rain into an open structure during winter storm systems, this step protects materials that are still salvageable.

**Smoke and soot mapping.** Technicians trace smoke migration through the structure — inside wall cavities, through HVAC returns, into attic spaces. Soot that has traveled beyond the fire origin room is catalogued and photographed for the insurance file.

**Controlled demolition of unsalvageable materials.** Charred framing, melted insulation, and fire-damaged drywall are removed to clean lines. California's Title 17 and local San Bernardino County health guidelines govern disposal of materials that may contain asbestos — a real consideration in Chino homes built before the mid-1980s — so suspect materials are tested before demolition proceeds.

**Deodorization and air scrubbing.** Thermal fogging and hydroxyl generation address odor molecules embedded in structural cavities. HEPA air scrubbers run continuously during this phase. A home that smells clean at the surface but has untreated ductwork will re-contaminate within days once the HVAC cycles.

**Drying and humidity control.** Fire suppression water — from sprinklers or hose lines — adds a secondary moisture problem. Structural drying equipment runs in tandem with smoke remediation so the rebuild phase starts on a dry substrate.
