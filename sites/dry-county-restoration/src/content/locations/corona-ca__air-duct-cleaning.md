Corona sits in the Inland Empire's western edge, where summer temperatures regularly push past 100°F and the Santa Ana winds sweep dry, particulate-heavy air through the passes for weeks at a time. That combination — extreme heat cycling your HVAC hard, followed by wind events that drive fine dust and debris into return vents — means duct systems here accumulate buildup faster than in coastal Southern California cities. If your system has been running through a few of those summers without a cleaning, what's circulating through your home is worth a closer look.

## Why Corona Properties See Duct Issues

The Inland Empire's climate does specific things to residential duct systems. During Santa Ana conditions, fine particulate matter — including soil dust from the surrounding hills — gets pulled through return air grilles, especially in homes where the air handler sits in a garage or attic space. That dust doesn't just settle in the ducts; it coats the evaporator coil and blower wheel, reducing airflow efficiency and creating a surface where biological growth can take hold when humidity eventually returns.

Corona's housing stock adds another layer. The city grew rapidly through the 1980s and 1990s, and a large portion of its single-family homes were built with flexible duct runs in attic spaces — a design that works fine when installed taut and supported, but over time those ducts can sag, kink, or develop small separations at the joints. A sagging flex duct collects debris at the low point; a separated joint pulls unconditioned attic air (and whatever's in it) directly into your living space. Neither problem is visible without pulling access panels and inspecting the runs.

Dryer vent lines in Corona homes also deserve attention. Many tract homes from this era were built with dryer locations on interior walls, which means longer vent runs with one or more elbows — exactly the configuration that accumulates lint fastest and poses the highest fire risk if left uncleaned.

## Our Air Duct Cleaning Process in Corona

The process starts before any equipment goes into your vents. A technician walks the system — locating every supply and return register, identifying the air handler location, and noting any visible duct damage or disconnections. In attic-mounted systems common to Corona's tract homes, that means a physical attic inspection, not just a visual check from below.

Cleaning uses negative-pressure extraction: a high-CFM vacuum unit connects to the main trunk line while technicians work register by register with compressed air tools, dislodging debris and driving it toward the collection point rather than redistributing it through the house. Supply and return sides are cleaned separately. The air handler cabinet — blower wheel, evaporator coil housing, and drain pan — is inspected and cleaned as part of the same visit, because a clean duct connected to a fouled air handler accomplishes very little.

Where sanitization is requested, we apply an EPA-registered antimicrobial to duct surfaces after mechanical cleaning. This is not a substitute for cleaning — it's a follow-on step for systems where biological growth has been identified or where the homeowner has documented allergy or sensitivity concerns.

Dryer vent cleaning follows a similar negative-pressure approach, with a rotary brush run through the full length of the vent line and a final airflow check to confirm the termination cap is clear and functional.

## Equipment & Methods We Use for Air Duct

The core tool is a truck-mounted or portable high-CFM vacuum unit capable of maintaining negative pressure across the duct system during cleaning — this is what separates professional duct cleaning from the low-cost "blow and go" services that use a shop vac and a leaf blower. Agitation tools include rotary brushes sized to the duct diameter, compressed air whips, and skipper balls for flexible duct sections where a rigid brush can't travel the full length.

For video inspection of duct interiors — useful when a homeowner reports unusual odors or suspects pest intrusion — we use a flexible camera system that can document conditions in trunk lines and main branches before and after cleaning.

## Local Note

Corona's tract developments from the late 1980s and early 1990s were frequently built with R-4.2 flex duct in attic spaces — a specification that met code at the time but is significantly below current California energy standards. Homeowners who've had HVAC work done in recent years may have had some runs replaced with higher-rated duct, but in many homes the original flex is still in place. That older flex duct has a foil inner liner that can become brittle with age and temperature cycling; aggressive cleaning with oversized brushes can damage it. We size our agitation tools to the actual duct diameter and inspect liner condition before brushing, rather than running a one-size approach through every system.
