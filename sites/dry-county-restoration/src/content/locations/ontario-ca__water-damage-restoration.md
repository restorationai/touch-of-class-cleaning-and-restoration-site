## Why Ontario Properties See Water Damage Differently

Ontario's housing stock spans several distinct eras. Neighborhoods developed in the 1950s and 1960s — many of them built on slab foundations with copper supply lines that have now reached the end of their service life — account for a significant share of the sudden-leak calls we receive in this part of the Inland Empire. Older copper plumbing develops pinhole leaks at fittings and elbows, and because the lines run through or under the slab, the leak can saturate the concrete substrate and wick into flooring adhesive, baseboards, and lower wall cavities before any visible water appears at the surface.

The region's clay-dominant soil compounds the problem. When Ontario's soil is dry — which is most of the year — it pulls away from foundations, creating micro-gaps where groundwater and irrigation runoff can channel toward the structure. Seasonal rains, when they do arrive, can reverse that movement abruptly. Properties with landscape irrigation systems set for the summer and never adjusted for winter storms are especially vulnerable to exterior water intrusion at foundation walls and garage slabs.

Ontario is also home to a large inventory of light industrial and mixed-use commercial properties, particularly in the corridors near the airport and along major east-west arterials. Sprinkler system discharges and roof drain failures in those buildings produce high-volume water events that require commercial-grade extraction equipment and coordinated moisture mapping across large square footages.

## Our Water Damage Restoration Process in Ontario

Every job starts with moisture mapping — not a visual inspection, but instrument-based readings using thermal imaging and calibrated moisture meters to locate water that has traveled beyond the obvious wet zone. In Ontario's slab-on-grade homes, that often means checking the concrete substrate, the flooring system above it, and the lower 12 to 18 inches of adjacent drywall before any drying equipment is placed.

Once the affected area is fully documented, water extraction comes first. Standing water is removed with truck-mounted or portable extraction units, and then the slower work begins: placing desiccant or refrigerant dehumidifiers, high-velocity air movers, and in-wall drying systems where cavity moisture is detected. Drying targets are set to the IICRC S500 standard and verified with daily moisture readings — not estimated by feel or schedule.

Materials that cannot be dried in place — saturated drywall below the flood cut line, wet insulation, compromised flooring underlayment — are removed and documented photographically before disposal. That documentation matters for your insurance claim, and we maintain it throughout the job.

## Local Note: Slab Leaks and the "Dry Surface" Problem

One thing that catches Ontario homeowners off guard: in this climate, the surface of a floor can feel completely dry — and even read low on a basic pin-type moisture meter — while the concrete slab beneath it is still holding significant moisture. The combination of low ambient humidity and high temperatures accelerates surface evaporation but does not pull moisture out of dense materials at the same rate. If your flooring was wet and now "seems fine," that is the moment to get a thermal scan done, not a reason to skip remediation. Moisture trapped under tile, vinyl plank, or hardwood in Ontario's heat can promote microbial growth faster than in cooler, more humid climates — because the warmth accelerates biological activity even when the visible water is gone.
