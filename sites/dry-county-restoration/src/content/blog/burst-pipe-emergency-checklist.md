If a pipe just burst in your home, here's what to do right now: shut off your main water supply, cut power to any affected rooms at the breaker panel, and get everything off the floor that you can move in the next two minutes. Then call a plumber to fix the break and a water damage restoration company to handle the water that's already in your walls, floors, and ceiling. The rest of this guide walks through every step — including the mistakes that turn a manageable repair into a months-long ordeal.

## What You're Actually Dealing With

A burst pipe doesn't just spill water — it pressurizes it. A standard residential supply line runs at 40–80 PSI, which means even a small split can push dozens of gallons into a wall cavity or subfloor in minutes. By the time you notice the ceiling bubbling or water pooling in a room that shouldn't have any, saturation is often already deep.

In Corona and the surrounding Inland Empire, the most common culprits are:

- **Aging galvanized or copper supply lines** in homes built before the 1980s, where corrosion eventually wins
- **Polybutylene pipe** (gray plastic, common in 1980s–90s construction) that becomes brittle over time
- **Sudden pressure spikes** after a water main repair in the neighborhood
- **Appliance supply lines** — the braided hose behind a washing machine or the saddle valve feeding a refrigerator ice maker — which can fail without warning and are often overlooked until water is already spreading across the floor

Knowing the source matters because it tells you how long water may have been running before you noticed, and whether the break is in a pressurized supply line (fast, high-volume) or a drain line (slower but often dirtier).

## Immediate Steps: The First 15 Minutes

Work through these in order. Don't skip ahead.

1. **Shut off the main water supply valve.** In most Corona-area homes, this is at the front of the property near the street, inside a concrete box flush with the ground. Turn it clockwise until it stops. If you can't locate it or it won't budge, call the water district — EMWD or Western Municipal Water District serves much of this area and has emergency lines.

2. **Turn off electricity to affected areas.** Water and live circuits are an immediate electrocution risk. Go to your breaker panel and cut power to any room where water is present or where it could have traveled through the ceiling. If the panel itself is in a wet area, do not touch it — call an electrician first.

3. **Open faucets to drain pressure.** Once the main is off, open the lowest faucet in the house (usually a hose bib or a sink near the garage). This relieves residual pressure and helps drain water out of the pipes rather than letting it continue seeping into building materials.

4. **Document everything before you touch it.** Take a slow video walkthrough on your phone — walls, floors, ceilings, the source of the break, any visible damage. Your insurance adjuster will want this, and it's far easier to capture now than to reconstruct later.

5. **Move what you can off the floor.** Rugs, furniture legs, cardboard boxes, anything sitting in standing water. The longer porous materials stay wet, the more damage they absorb — and the faster mold colonization begins. Under the right conditions (warm, humid, organic material), mold can begin establishing within 24–48 hours.

6. **Call a licensed plumber to repair the break.** The water damage restoration process can't begin until the source is fixed. If you have a home warranty, check whether it covers plumbing before calling an outside contractor.

## What NOT to Do

Some of the most expensive mistakes happen in the first hour, when people are moving fast and stressed.

- **Don't use a wet/dry shop vac as your only drying strategy.** It will pull surface water, but it won't touch moisture that has wicked into drywall, insulation, or subfloor. Structural drying requires professional equipment — high-capacity air movers and dehumidifiers that run continuously for days, not hours.

- **Don't close the house up and crank the heat.** Warm, stagnant air accelerates mold growth. If outdoor humidity is low (common in the Inland Empire's drier months), opening windows can help. But don't assume ventilation alone is drying your structure.

- **Don't tear out drywall before documenting.** Insurers need to see the damage in place. Photograph and video everything first. If you do need to open walls for ventilation or to find the source, keep the removed material until the adjuster has signed off.

- **Don't assume it dried out on its own.** Water behind plaster or drywall can read dry at the surface while the lath, framing, or subfloor underneath stays saturated for weeks. Surface dryness is not structural dryness.

- **Don't delay filing an insurance claim.** Most homeowner policies require you to report a loss promptly and to take reasonable steps to prevent further damage. Waiting several days before calling your carrier can complicate or reduce your claim.

## When to Call a Water Damage Professional

Some situations you can manage with a mop and a fan. A burst pipe is not one of them.

Call a water damage restoration company if any of the following are true:

- Standing water is more than a puddle — anything that has spread across a room or soaked into flooring
- Water has reached drywall, insulation, cabinetry, or subfloor
- The leak ran for an unknown amount of time before you discovered it
- There's any visible staining or soft spots on ceilings or walls
- The source was an appliance supply line (washing machine, refrigerator, dishwasher) — these leaks often travel further than they appear because the water follows the subfloor before surfacing
- You're in a home with a slab foundation, where water can migrate under the concrete and into adjacent rooms before appearing anywhere visible

A restoration crew will use moisture meters and thermal imaging to find water that isn't visible to the eye, then set drying equipment calibrated to the specific materials in your home. The goal isn't just removing standing water — it's getting the structural moisture content back to a normal baseline, typically measured in readings rather than guesswork.

## The Recovery Process: What Comes After the Drying

Once a restoration company has dried the structure — a process that typically takes several days of continuous equipment operation and daily moisture monitoring — the repair phase begins. This is where a contractor rebuilds what was removed or damaged: drywall, flooring, cabinetry, paint.

A few things to expect:

- **Drying logs matter for your claim.** A reputable restoration company will provide daily moisture readings that document the drying progress. Keep these records — they're part of your claim file.
- **Mold testing may be recommended** if the water sat for more than 24–48 hours before drying equipment was deployed, especially in wall cavities where you can't see what's happening.
- **Flooring decisions come last.** Hardwood, laminate, and engineered wood floors often need to be fully dried and assessed before anyone can tell you whether they can be saved or need replacement. Don't let anyone pressure you into a decision before the moisture readings support it.
- **Permits may be required** for structural repairs in California, depending on the scope. Your contractor should pull the appropriate permits through the City of Corona's building department.
